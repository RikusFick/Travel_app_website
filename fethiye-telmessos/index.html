  1. 1

    The ancient theatre

    In or beside the ancient theatre near the harbour front, facing the stone seats. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are at the ancient theatre of Telmessos. Two thousand years ago this harbour town was Telmessos, a city of ancient Lycia, and this theatre was its gathering place by the sea.

    Official local pages describe a theatre built in the early Roman period and repaired in the second century AD. Digging brought the seating you see back into the open. A walkway , a diazoma , divides two groups of seats. Four entrances and exits served the cavea; one is damaged; the other three remain in place. In later centuries the upper seating rows were taken as fill when the modern harbour front was built.

    Official wording puts the original capacity near six thousand. What you see on the surviving seats is far fewer , the same page gives about two to two and a half thousand on the surviving seats. Both figures come from that local page; neither is averaged away.

    Stand and look from the seats toward the water. This was a theatre built almost at the shore, repaired under Rome, stripped of its upper rows for harbour fill, then dug out again so walkers could sit where audiences once sat. The diazoma under your feet once divided the crowd; the damaged entrance on one side shows how much stone was moved when the modern front was built. Official local wording recovered the seating only after excavation brought the buried cavea back into the open.

  2. 2

    Paspatur and the earthquake of 1957

    In the narrow lanes of Paspatur, the old bazaar quarter. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are in Paspatur, the old bazaar quarter of Fethiye. Official local history records that under Rome the town was known as Meğri, or Makri , “far country” in the wording of that page. After Rome split, Meğri stayed inside the eastern empire. In twelve eighty-two Menteşe Bey took it from Byzantine hands; in fourteen twenty-four it entered Ottoman lands. In eighteen seventy-four Meğri became a district under the Menteşe livası. In nineteen thirty-four the name became Fethiye, in memory of Fethi Bey, one of the first Ottoman military pilots, killed when his aircraft came down near Tiberias in nineteen thirteen.

    Fethiye has lived with earthquakes for generations. On the night of the twenty-fourth and twenty-fifth of April, nineteen fifty-seven, a strong quake struck the Fethiye district and its surroundings. A scholarly account in the Atatürk Research Center journal records sixty-seven people killed , twenty-seven in the district centre and its villages, forty in other districts. The same account lists thousands of houses destroyed or ruined beyond use: nearly three thousand eight hundred houses completely destroyed, hundreds more left uninhabitable, and hundreds damaged. Schools, mosques and public buildings were among the losses , dozens of schools and several mosques and official buildings destroyed or heavily damaged.

    Relief and rebuilding followed. The lanes around you belong to that rebuilt town. As new streets and public buildings rose, visitors began to arrive in greater numbers, and the harbour front below you grew over the older shoreline.

    Look at the rebuilt lanes. Low shops, narrow streets and the smell of food , this is modern Paspatur on ground that has been cleared and raised more than once.

  3. 3

    A Lycian sarcophagus in the street

    By the large stone sarcophagus in the town centre. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Beside you stands a large stone sarcophagus among modern streets. Look at it: a heavy coffin with a high lid and carved work along the long sides. It is a tomb in the street , shops and traffic around an ancient burial , and a reminder that Telmessos still surfaces in the modern town.

    What you see is what is visible above the pavement. Do not climb it; leave the stone as you find it.

  4. 4

    Tombs on the hillside

    On the lower slope at the eastern edge of the old town, near the Lycian sarcophagi on the hillside. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    On the hillside above you are Lycian sarcophagi and rock tombs. Look up. More stone coffins stand on the slopes, and above them, cut into the cliff, are the temple-fronted rock tombs of Telmessos. The grandest of them is the Tomb of Amyntas , the King's Tomb , treated in its own short guide.

    Official museum and ministry pages describe three temple-type tombs on this south hillside of ancient Telmessos, Ionic façades in antis, and burial couches inside. They date the cluster to the fourth century BC and call the better-preserved tomb a symbol of Fethiye. Other tombs copy civil architecture , houses rather than temples.

    Official local history records that the city fell under Persian rule in five hundred and forty-five BC and submitted to Alexander the Great in three hundred and thirty-three BC. After Alexander’s death it stood for a time under a Ptolemaic king of Egypt , the Kaymakamlık page names Ptolemy , then under Rome, when the living town was called Meğri. Earthquakes and rebuilding erased most of the houses and streets of that living city. What remains for walkers is largely what was cut for the dead: tombs in the rock and coffins too heavy to clear away. Official provincial culture wording for Fethiye says remains of Telmessos appear in many parts of the modern town , the theatre by the water, the tombs in the cliff, and the castle on the ridge.

    From here the Amyntas cliff is a short climb. If you take that path, use the separate Amyntas guide for the King’s Tomb and the smaller temple and house-type façades beside it.

  5. 5

    Fethiye Castle

    On the hill above the old town, among the ruined walls of the castle, with the harbour and the bay below. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    These broken walls stand on the hill south of the modern town , the acropolis of ancient Telmessos, in the wording of the provincial culture ministry. The same official account places modern Fethiye on the site of that ancient harbour city, stretching from the mountain foothills down to the bay. From here the city watched its harbour and the water below.

    Official provincial culture wording says a castle here is thought to have been built by the Knights of Saint John, and was used again in the Ottoman period. On the hilltop you find wall remains and a cistern; on the east face of the hill, small rock tombs. That is what those opened official accounts record , no more, no less.

    Stand and look down. Theatre, bazaar lanes, street sarcophagus and cliff tombs all lie in the bowl of the modern town. Telmessos is under your feet and on the cliffs above you , a harbour city that kept building for the living while carving space for the dead.

    From Persian and Hellenistic rule through Rome, Meğri, Menteşe and the Ottoman district, the name changed; the rock tombs and the theatre seats stayed. In nineteen thirty-four the district took the name Fethiye. In nineteen fifty-seven the town was largely rebuilt after the quake. What you walk is that layered place: Lycian tombs in the cliff, a Roman theatre by the water, Ottoman and Republican streets in between, and a castle ridge watching the bay.
