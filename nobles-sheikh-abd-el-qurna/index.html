  1. 1

    Sheikh Abd el-Qurna: the hillside of the nobles

    On the lower slopes of the hill of Sheikh Abd el-Qurna, on the west bank of the Nile opposite Luxor. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You stand on a rocky ridge across the river from Luxor, looking over a slope honeycombed with carved doorways. This hillside is Sheikh Abd el-Qurna, a major sector of the Theban Necropolis. It is named for the white-domed shrine of a local Muslim saint.

    Together with Karnak, Luxor Temple, and the royal valleys, these private chapels belong to UNESCO's World Heritage site of Ancient Thebes. They honor New Kingdom craftsmanship at its peak.

    The people buried here were not kings. They were viziers, mayors, scribes, stewards and overseers. Many lived in the fifteenth and fourteenth centuries BC, under kings such as Thutmose the Third, Amenhotep the Second and Amenhotep the Third. Many of their tombs follow a common plan. There is an open courtyard, and a chapel cut into the rock in the shape of a T. A shaft or steep passage leads down to the burial chamber.

    What makes these small tombs special is what is painted and carved on the walls. Instead of gods and underworld journeys alone, you find fields being ploughed, ships unloading, craftsmen at work, banquets with musicians, and funeral processions. The owners wanted to carry their working lives, their families and their pleasures into the afterlife. So the walls record how an official of the New Kingdom wished to be remembered.

    Many of the tombs are small, and inside they can be dim and close. They are usually opened and ticketed in small groups, and which ones are open can change from season to season. Rules on photography and the number of people allowed inside also vary, so follow the signs and the guards' instructions. You can visit the tombs in any order.
