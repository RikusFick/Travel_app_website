    The drawing room of Europe

    Start at the western arcade of Piazza San Marco looking toward the basilica across Venice's only true piazza.

    Read along

    You're now looking at Piazza San Marco, the only square in Venice that Venetians simply call "the Piazza".

    For a thousand years this was the heart of the Republic of Venice: its church, its government and its stage for great ceremonies. A famous remark, often credited to Napoleon, though there's no proof he said it, called it "the drawing room of Europe".

    Look along the sides. On your left, the long arcade is the old Procuratie, once homes and offices for the Procurators of Saint Mark, high officials of the Republic. On your right, the newer Procuratie.

    Behind you, the end of the square was rebuilt under Napoleon's rule, around 1810. It's called the Napoleonic Wing.

    Under the arcades are the famous old cafés. Caffè Florian, on the right-hand side, opened in 1720.

    In the nineteenth century, when Austria ruled Venice, Austrian officers favoured Caffè Quadri, on one side of the square, while Venetians went to Florian's, on the other.
