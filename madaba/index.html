  1. 1

    A town of the Bible, reborn

    On the street outside the Greek Orthodox Church of St George, the Church of the Map. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Welcome to Madaba, the City of Mosaics. You are on a high plateau about thirty kilometres south-west of Amman, in the old land of Moab.

    Madaba is a town of the Bible. In the Book of Numbers, an ancient victory song names it among the towns of Moab, "as far as Medeba" (Numbers 21:30). The Book of Joshua lists "all the tableland of Medeba" in the inheritance east of the Jordan (Joshua 13:9). The prophet Isaiah, lamenting over Moab, adds: "over Nebo and over Medeba Moab wails" (Isaiah 15:2). Control of the town swung between Israel and Moab, and King Mesha of Moab boasted of it on his famous victory stone.

    Under Rome and Byzantium, Madaba flourished. The records of the Church Council of Chalcedon, in 451, already name a bishop of Madaba, and its churches and mansions were paved with mosaics. Then came a long sleep. A great earthquake in the mid-eighth century left the town largely in ruins, and it was abandoned.

    The revival came in the early 1880s. Christian families from Karak in the south received Ottoman permission to resettle the ruins, guided by their priests. Under Ottoman rules, they could build new churches only on the sites of ancient ones. As they cleared the ground, beautiful floors kept appearing under their feet, and their priests urged them to protect every one. That is why this whole town is a gallery of mosaics. Inside is the greatest of them.

  2. 2

    The map in the floor

    Inside St George's, at the walkway beside the mosaic map near the front of the church. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    At your feet is the oldest surviving map of the Holy Land: a map made of stone. In 1884, while preparing to build this church over the ruins of a Byzantine one, workers found traces of a coloured floor. The church went up, and in 1896 a young scholar from the Patriarchate of Jerusalem, Father Cleophas Koikylides, recognised what it was. He published the first study the following year.

    The map was laid in the sixth century. Scholars date it by what it shows: it includes the New Church of the Virgin in Jerusalem, dedicated in 542, and nothing built much later. Most sources place it around the middle of that century.

    When it was new, it was enormous, about twenty-one metres by seven, made of more than two million tiny stones. Fire, damp, building work and time destroyed much of it. The surviving fragment is between ten and sixteen metres long and about five metres wide, depending on how you measure the scattered pieces.

    Notice something odd. The map does not face north. It faces east, toward the altar, like the church itself. So if you stand looking toward the altar, the places on the floor lie in their real directions. The Mediterranean is behind you, and the desert is ahead. Each place is labelled in Greek, and modern study has identified well over a hundred place-names on it. Many quote or echo the Bible.

  3. 3

    The Holy City

    At the walkway nearest the large walled city in the middle of the map. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Find the biggest thing on the map: an oval city ringed by walls and towers. This is Jerusalem, labelled "the Holy City", shown as the centre of the land.

    Look closely and you can walk its streets. Running through the middle is a long street lined with columns, the cardo, the main avenue of Byzantine Jerusalem. Halfway along it, steps rise to a large church with a golden dome: the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, built over the place of Christ's tomb and resurrection. Near one end of the cardo stands the New Church of the Virgin, the building that helps date the map. The gates are there too.

    This is more than art. The map has helped scholars understand the layout of Jerusalem after its rebuilding, and for many of them this floor is a key source for the shape of the city in the sixth century.

  4. 4

    The Jordan, the Dead Sea and the baptism

    At the walkway beside the river and the lake on the map. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Follow the blue band of the River Jordan as it runs into the Dead Sea. The artists filled it with life. Fish swim down the river, and one appears to turn back from the salt water of the Dead Sea. Two boats sail on the lake. In the desert of Moab, a lion chases a gazelle, though at some point someone scrambled its tiles, so the lion is hard to recognise.

    Look along the river for crossing places and for a place called Bethabara, the shrine of Saint John the Baptist, near where Jesus was baptised. On the east bank, the map marks "Aenon, where now is Sapsaphas", which the official site of the Baptism Site connects with its own spring in Wadi al-Kharrar.

    Jericho stands among its palm trees. Further on are Bethlehem and many other places of the Gospels. One account suggests the map partly served pilgrims, as a guide to the holy places. Whatever its purpose, the people of Madaba could walk across the whole of the Bible's landscape every time they came to church.

  5. 5

    The Roman street and the Burnt Palace

    In the Madaba Archaeological Park, on the stone paving of the Roman street. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are standing in the Madaba Archaeological Park, created from 1991 by Jordan's Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities with the American Center of Research and international partners.

    Under your feet lie the large flagstones of Madaba's Roman street, which once crossed the town from east to west toward vanished city gates. Columns lined the roadway, and many were later carried off for reuse in newer buildings.

    Beside the street stand the remains of the Burnt Palace. According to ACOR, a mosaic uncovered here in 1905 was first thought to belong to a church. Excavations in 1985 showed it instead decorated a grand private residence that burned in the late Byzantine era and was abandoned. Its floors depict trees, birds, fish, hunting scenes and, near one entrance, a pair of sandals in a medallion. ACOR's report notes the complex may have been destroyed in the earthquake of AD 747 or 748.

  6. 6

    The Virgin and the tragedy of Hippolytus

    Inside the large stone shelter of the park, over the Church of the Virgin and the Hippolytus Hall. This is stop 6 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This shelter protects two floors, one on top of the other, from two very different worlds.

    Below is the hall of an early sixth-century house, the Hippolytus Hall. It is named after its mosaic, which, ACOR reports, shows characters from the Greek tragedy of Phaedra and Hippolytus, each with a caption. In another panel, Aphrodite sits on a throne beside Adonis, among Graces and little Cupids, while a country girl carries a basket of fruit. Along one side, three cities appear as crowned women on thrones: Rome, Gregoria and Madaba itself, each holding a small cross on a long staff. Even in a Christian town, educated families cherished the classical myths.

    Later, a church was built over this house: the Church of the Virgin Mary, found in 1887. Its round centre holds an elegant later mosaic of interlaced circles and a star. ACOR records the prayer in its central medallion. It urges worshippers: "If you want to look at Mary, virginal Mother of God, and at Christ whom she bore, universal King, only Son of the only God, purify your mind, flesh, and works."

  7. 7

    The prophet of the rain

    In the park, at the remains of the Church of the Prophet Elias. This is stop 7 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This church was discovered in 1897 and is dedicated to the prophet Elijah, called Elias in Greek. ACOR records that its dedication inscription dates the mosaic to 607 or 608, in the last decades of Byzantine rule here.

    The second inscription, in a circle in the nave, is a prayer. Scripture tells how Elijah prayed and rain returned to the land after a long drought (1 Kings 18:41–45). The mosaic preserves the town's prayer. "You who with your prayer set in motion, as is fitting, the clouds, bearers of rain, and who give mercy to the people, O prophet, remember also the benefactors and this humble city." In a dry land like this, it was a heartfelt prayer.

    Beneath the church lies a crypt dedicated to Saint Elianus, reached by two stairways. In the old life of Madaba, the dead and the living, the prophets and the town's benefactors, were all bound together under the same roof.

  8. 8

    The sea in the desert

    At the Church of the Apostles, under its modern shelter, about five hundred metres south of the park. This is stop 8 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    ACOR notes that this church was given a new shelter as part of the same project as the park. Inside is one of Madaba's loveliest floors. One detailed account dates it to the year 578, and says it was never disfigured, so its figures survive intact.

    At the centre, in a medallion about two metres across, a woman rises from the waves, surrounded by fish and sea creatures. She is Thalassa, the Sea, a figure from the old Greek world. Around her, pairs of birds face each other in a lattice of flowers and fruit, and young men and animals play in the vines at the edges.

    Think of the people who walked on this floor: farmers and traders on a dry plateau, far from any sea, standing on an image of the ocean. Madaba's mosaics were their windows onto a wider world, from the oceans to the streets of Jerusalem. Let the colours stay with you as you step back into the modern town.
