  1. 1

    The Venetian harbour

    On the harbour front, with the lighthouse at the end of the long mole ahead. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This Venetian harbour was the heart of Chania for four and a half centuries of Venetian rule, from 1204 to 1669.

    The Greek National Tourism Organisation dates the harbour to the fourteenth century.

    It was built for trade, and as a shield against pirate raids.

    The Venetians called the town La Canea, and for a time it was the capital of the island.

    The town's tourism office ranks the harbour of that age second only to Venice itself.

    More than forty galleys could lie here, and from here they policed the sea trade of the eastern Mediterranean.

    To the north, the Venetians raised a long stone wall to break the waves rolling in from the open sea.

    Beside that wall stands the bastion of Saint Nicholas.

    Under Ottoman rule, the Municipality records, prisoners were put to death there.

    Venetian houses line the western side, and a domed mosque rises at the water's edge.

    On the far side of the harbour mouth, a stone lighthouse marks the end of the breakwater.

    At the western end, the walls of a Venetian fort guard the entrance.

  2. 2

    The Yali Mosque

    In front of the domed building on the harbour front. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This domed building is the Yali Mosque, the mosque of the waterfront.

    It went up in the second half of the seventeenth century, after the Ottoman conquest.

    It honours Küçük Hasan, the first Ottoman commander of the garrison of Chania.

    Porters loaded and unloaded ships on this spot, so it was also called the mosque of the porters.

    The national tourism organisation calls it the oldest Muslim building on Crete.

    The town's tourism office calls it the first mosque the Ottomans built on the island.

    Research by the archaeological service found that a small church had stood on the site before it.

    An Armenian architect designed it, the heritage portal says.

    It is a cube crowned by one large dome, carried on four stone arches.

    A porch with six small domes wraps around the western and northern sides.

    At first that porch was open, and it was closed in around 1880.

    Palm trees once shaded the courtyard, among the graves of pashas and Janissaries.

    Prayers ended in 1923, when the last Muslims left Crete in the exchange of populations.

    The minaret was pulled down in 1920, or in 1939 by another account, and only its base survives.

    Over the years the building served as an archaeological museum, a warehouse and a folk art museum.

  3. 3

    The lighthouse

    At the lighthouse at the end of the mole, at the harbour entrance. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This lighthouse stands at the tip of the breakwater, at the entrance to the harbour.

    The Venetians built the first tower here towards the end of the sixteenth century.

    Its present shape belongs to a later age.

    Between 1830 and 1840, Crete was handed to Mehmet Ali, the ruler of Egypt.

    Under his rule, the Egyptians rebuilt the lighthouse, and the Municipality says that is why it is often called the Egyptian lighthouse.

    The national tourism organisation adds that they gave it the form of a minaret.

    The base has eight sides, the middle has sixteen, and the top is round.

    Late in the Ottoman period, a ladder was added on its eastern side.

    Pipes were set into the base as well, to let sea water pass beneath it.

    The Municipality gives its height as twenty-one metres above the sea.

    Its light, by the same account, reaches seven nautical miles.

    The town ranks it among the world's most ancient lighthouses.

  4. 4

    The Venetian shipyards

    On the eastern side of the harbour, beside the row of vaulted stone halls. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    These vaulted stone halls are the Venetian shipyards, known in Greek as the neoria.

    Galleys were built and repaired in them, and laid up through the winter.

    Each hall opened onto the water, so that ships could be hauled in and out with ease.

    Venice ordered shipyards for Chania and Rethymno in 1467, by the town's account.

    The work was slow, and the first two halls here were finished only in the early sixteenth century.

    By the end of that century, seventeen halls stood along the southern side.

    The westernmost and grandest is the Grand Arsenal, begun in the 1580s.

    A second floor was added to it in the Ottoman period.

    Seven of the old halls stand together beside the marina.

    When the Ottomans invaded Crete, the town used them to store food through the sieges.

    At the eastern corner of the harbour stand the Moro shipyards, named after the Venetian official who proposed them.

    The mini map says five were begun after 1607 and two were finished.

    The national tourism organisation counts three Moro halls, the last of them left unfinished for centuries.

  5. 5

    Agios Nikolaos in Splantzia

    In Splantzia Square, in front of the church with both a minaret and a bell tower. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This is Agios Nikolaos in Splantzia, a church that has served three faiths.

    Dominican friars built it as the church of their monastery around 1320.

    The town's tourism office puts its building a little earlier, before 1320.

    After the Ottomans took Chania in 1645, they made it a mosque.

    They named it the Sovereign's Mosque, in honour of Sultan Ibrahim.

    The mini map calls it the main mosque of the city.

    Tradition held that the sword of the dervish who first entered the city was kept here, and that it worked wonders.

    The tall minaret on the southwest corner has two balconies, a mark of the mosque's rank.

    Its details follow Venetian building habits, the Municipality notes.

    In the eighteenth century a fountain for ritual washing was built in the square in front.

    In 1918, by the account of a Cretan travel guide, the building passed to Orthodox worship and took the name of Saint Nicholas.

    A bell tower was added on the opposite side.

    The same guide claims that no other church in Greece pairs a minaret with a bell tower.

  6. 6

    The Municipal Market

    At the market's main entrance on the square. This is stop 6 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This is the Municipal Market of Chania, a building of about four thousand square metres.

    Its plan is a cross, with an entrance on each of the four arms.

    The town council first debated a covered market in 1908, as part of a plan to renew the town.

    The plan meant pulling down part of the Venetian fortifications and filling in the moat.

    By one local account, the engineer Konstantinos Drandakis completed the designs in 1909.

    Work on the foundations began in August 1911.

    The site was the main Venetian rampart, the town's tourism office says.

    The building was finished in the second half of 1913.

    That was the year Crete was united with Greece.

    The Prime Minister, Eleftherios Venizelos, opened it on the fourth of December 1913.

    The ceremony came just three days after the official celebration of union.

    In May 1941, days before the Battle of Crete, an air-raid shelter was finished on its southeastern side.

    By one local account, the Germans took over the building for their army during the occupation.

    In 1980 the Ministry of Culture listed it as a protected monument.

    The town's tourism office ranks it among the most impressive markets in the Balkans.

  7. 7

    Kasteli and the Jewish quarter

    On the low hill of Kasteli, just behind the harbour. This is stop 7 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Kasteli is the low hill behind the harbour, and the oldest part of the town.

    The Ministry of Culture identifies Chania with ancient Kydonia, one of the leading cities of Minoan Crete.

    People first settled here as early as the New Stone Age, the national tourism organisation says.

    Excavations began in the 1960s, and Swedish archaeologists joined the Greek service.

    The Byzantine wall around the hill rests on a Hellenistic one, and much of it was built from the stones of ancient Kydonia.

    Below the hill lay Evraiki, the small Jewish quarter of Chania.

    Its synagogue, Etz Hayyim, began as a Venetian Catholic church of Saint Katherine.

    The synagogue dates the building to the fourteenth or fifteenth century.

    An Ottoman attack in the 1540s left it without a roof.

    After the Ottoman conquest, the Jewish community acquired it and made it a synagogue.

    By one account, the Nazis arrested the whole community, two hundred and sixty-three people, in late May 1944.

    They were put aboard the ship Tanais, which a British submarine torpedoed on the ninth of June.

    There were no survivors, and two thousand years of Jewish life on Crete came to an end.

    Restored by the scholar Nikos Stavroulakis, the synagogue was dedicated again in October 1999.

  8. 8

    Fort Firka and union with Greece

    At the western end of the harbour, by the walls of Fort Firka. This is stop 8 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This is Fort Firka, the fort that guards the western side of the harbour mouth.

    The Venetians called it the Revellino del Porto.

    The Ministry of Culture says building began around the middle of the sixteenth century, and ended a few years before the city fell in 1645.

    The town's tourism office calls it a seventeenth-century fortress.

    It held barracks and stores for war material, and it was the seat of the city's military commander.

    On its northern side, six vaults held large gun ports aimed across the harbour entrance.

    The Ottomans used it as barracks, and its name comes from the Turkish word for barracks.

    Its vaults also served as a prison, from Ottoman times until the years of the Greek Civil War.

    The Municipality says Greek rebels were among those held here.

    At the entrance stands a brick-red building that once housed the Venetian naval guard.

    On the first of December 1913, the flag of union was raised on the corner tower.

    By the Municipality's account, it was the first time in centuries that a Greek flag flew over the fort.

    Three days later, Venizelos opened the new market in the town.
