  1. 1

    The Place of Truth

    At the edge of the ruined village, looking across the low stone walls of the houses. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The low walls around you are what is left of a village built for one purpose. It housed the people who dug and painted the tombs of Egypt's kings. Its ancient name was Set Maat , usually translated as the Place of Truth , and its workmen held the title of servants in the Place of Truth.

    Notice its setting. The settlement sits tucked inside a dry desert hollow near the royal valleys. Placing it apart from ordinary towns may have kept the secret of royal burials closely guarded. It was also a hot, enclosed place. The surrounding rock reflects the sun, and a hill to the north cuts off the breeze.

    The earliest datable remains belong to around the reign of Thutmose the First, roughly 1500 BC. The community lasted, in some form, for about four to five hundred years. Counts of the houses differ. Some give about sixty-eight houses at its peak. Others suggest anywhere from forty to a hundred and twenty households, depending on how much royal building was under way.

    The village was abandoned around 1110 to 1080 BC, towards the end of the New Kingdom, when tomb robbery, raids and unrest made the valley harder to defend.

    This place matters beyond its tombs. Excavations here, mostly by a French team under Bernard Bruyère from 1922 to 1951, produced one of the best-documented accounts of daily life anywhere in the ancient world.
