    In front of you is the Galata Tower. Look up. For centuries, this stone tower has watched over Istanbul. With its pointed cap, it is one of the familiar shapes on the city's skyline.

    But here's the surprise: it wasn't built by the Byzantines or the Ottomans. It was built by Italians, merchants from the Republic of Genoa.

    In the year 1267, the Genoese, who were allies of the Byzantine emperors, were given their own colony here, on the hill north of the Golden Horn. They called it Pera. Over the following decades they expanded it uphill, and between 1335 and 1349 they ringed it with walls and towers. The tower in front of you dates from 1348, and it stood at the highest point of those walls, looking down over the whole colony and the harbour. There may have been an earlier Byzantine tower on this spot, but the tower you see is the Genoese one.

    The tower has often been called , the Tower of Christ, after a cross that once crowned its roof, though some historians think its real Genoese name was the Tower of the Holy Cross.

    Almost all of the Genoese walls were pulled down in the nineteenth century as the city spread north. Only small sections survive. The tower is the great survivor.

    The Galata Tower is on UNESCO's Tentative List of World Heritage Sites, together with other towers the Genoese built along their trade routes across the Mediterranean and the Black Sea.
