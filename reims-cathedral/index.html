    Read along

    The west facade of Reims Cathedral, crowded with statues, is one of the great achievements of Gothic art. The cathedral is about one hundred and fifty metres long, and its towers rise more than eighty metres.

    The cathedral was rebuilt from 1211, after fire destroyed the earlier church in 1210. This was the coronation church of France: more than thirty kings were crowned in Reims, the last Charles X in 1825. In 1991 the cathedral, with the nearby Palais du Tau and the Abbey of Saint-Remi, was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

    The west front alone is nearly forty-nine metres wide, and the whole building carries some two thousand three hundred statues. They were meant as a stone Bible and a royal stage: the faithful and the kings arriving for their coronation entered beneath them.

    The most famous coronation here came in the middle of the Hundred Years' War. On 17 July 1429, after Joan of Arc had cleared the way to Reims, the dauphin was crowned Charles VII, a turning point in the war; her equestrian statue stands on the square outside.

    Above the central doorway, where most cathedrals place a carved tympanum, Reims has a small rose window, and above the doorway itself stands the Coronation of the Virgin, the group given pride of place on a coronation church. Higher up, the great west rose shows at its centre the Dormition of the Virgin, and here the glass spills beyond the round frame to fill the whole pointed arch. The octagonal upper towers of the west front were finished only in the 1460s.
