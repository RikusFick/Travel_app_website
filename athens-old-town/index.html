  1. 1

    Monastiraki Square

    In the middle of Monastiraki Square, by the metro entrance. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Monastiraki Square takes its name from a church.

    Monastiraki means little monastery, and the small church on the square was once the main church of that monastery.

    It is the Pantanassa, and both the city guide and one Byzantine survey trace it to the tenth century.

    It is said to have served a community of nuns, whose cells stood where the square opens out.

    Roughly one third of the building sits beneath the level of the street around it.

    That sunken floor is a simple measure of how much the ground of Athens has risen over the centuries.

    Adrianou Street, which runs off the square, follows a line that was already part of the ancient city.

    From the middle of the square, a single glance towards the Acropolis takes in a mosque and a Byzantine church.

    Few places in Athens show its layers so plainly.

    The district was also known for trading in old goods.

    The city guide says the second-hand bazaar nearby took its name from two Jewish merchants, Noah and Elias Yusurum, leading antique dealers of the early 1900s.

    So a Byzantine convent, an Ottoman mosque and a Jewish family name all left their mark on this one square.

  2. 2

    The old mosque

    The next story is about The old mosque.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The old mosque on the corner of the square was built in 1759, according to the Museum of Modern Greek Culture, which looks after it.

    Mustafa Aga Tzisdarakis, then the Ottoman ruler of the town, had it built.

    He chose the heart of the town market, the Lower Bazaar, beside the fountain that marked the centre of trade.

    Its founding inscription survives above the entrance door.

    For the Christians of Ottoman Athens, life was quieter in one striking way.

    By the city guide's account, church bells were banned by decree and taken away.

    Ottoman rule over Athens lasted nearly four hundred years.

    After independence the mosque found new work.

    In the 1910s, the museum says, the architect Anastasios Orlandos restored it.

    Soon after, it became the first home of a new museum of Greek handicraft.

    That museum grew into the Museum of Modern Greek Culture, and the mosque stayed in its care as one of its branches.

    So a building raised for Muslim prayer came to tell the story of Greek folk art.

  3. 3

    Kapnikarea

    On Ermou Street, beside the small Byzantine church that sits in the middle of the street. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Kapnikarea stands right in the middle of Ermou Street, and the street simply parts around it.

    It dates from the eleventh century.

    The city guide says probably 1052; others say around 1050.

    Its core is a domed church in the shape of a cross.

    A porch along the west side was added later in the same century, and a chapel of Saint Barbara was joined on to the north near the end of Ottoman rule.

    The odd name has two explanations.

    It may come from a Byzantine hearth tax, the kapnikon, and from the tax collector who paid for the church.

    Or it may come from kamouha, a precious cloth woven with gold.

    The city guide adds that it may stand on an ancient temple of a goddess, perhaps Athena.

    In the nineteenth century the planners of the new capital wanted it gone.

    By one account, King Ludwig the First of Bavaria, the father of King Otto, saved it.

    The University of Athens, its owner, gives 1932 as the year the church passed to it.

  4. 4

    The Metropolis and the Little Metropolis

    In Mitropoleos Square, between the cathedral and the small marble church beside it. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Metropolis and the Little Metropolis stand side by side, and the small one is far older.

    The little church goes by several names: Panagia Gorgoepikoos, Saint Eleftherios, or the Little Metropolis.

    Gorgoepikoos means the Virgin who answers prayers quickly, a title taken from an icon held to work miracles.

    Saint Eleftherios is the protector of women in pregnancy.

    Its plan is the usual Byzantine cross with a dome.

    What makes it unique is the outside.

    Its walls are built with about ninety carved slabs taken from older buildings.

    They range from ancient Greek and Roman pieces to early Christian and Byzantine ones, like an open-air gallery of sculpture.

    Its date is disputed.

    Estimates run from the ninth to the early thirteenth century.

    The scholar Manolis Chatzidakis linked it with Michael Choniates, bishop of Athens from 1180 to 1204.

    Others place it later, in the early Ottoman years.

    In the nineteenth century, by one account, it even held the public library of Athens.

  5. 5

    Plaka

    On Adrianou Street, in the heart of Plaka. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Dionysis Zivas, the architect who led its rescue, calls Plaka the oldest known neighbourhood of Athens.

    He writes that people have lived here without a break for some five thousand years.

    In the city's darkest times, he adds, Plaka was Athens.

    The quarter wraps around the lower north and east slopes of the Acropolis rock.

    It is roughly a half circle, about a kilometre long.

    The city guide traces it from Filomousson Square down to Monastiraki.

    Some of its lanes already ran here in Classical times.

    Its buildings, though, are younger.

    Many were wrecked in the War of Independence, and the people of Plaka rebuilt them on nearly the same street plan.

    They kept the old Athenian house types, but took in touches of the Neoclassical style that came with the new kingdom.

    Zivas dates the present face of Plaka to the century after Athens became the capital in 1833.

    One older house survived nearby, the Benizelos Mansion, built in the eighteenth century.

    The city guide calls it the oldest surviving house in Athens, and the home of a saint, Agia Filothei.

  6. 6

    The secret under Plaka

    On one of Plaka's quiet side streets. This is stop 6 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The secret under Plaka is a network of pipes and cables, and it is the reason the lanes look the way they do.

    After 1950 Athens went through a building boom.

    Cars filled Plaka, and old homes were pulled down to make car parks.

    Residents moved out, and bars and night clubs moved in.

    Zivas counted a hundred and ninety entertainment venues in the mid-1970s.

    Protests began in the 1960s.

    The Environment Minister Stefanos Manos then gave the study to Zivas, an architect at the Athens Polytechnic.

    The plan protected Plaka as one whole settlement, not house by house, which Zivas calls a first for Greece.

    Half the length of its streets became pedestrian lanes, paved in marble.

    Every buried network was renewed, from water and sewers to gas, power, telephone and cable television.

    The overhead power cables came down, and the street lamps were replaced with copies of the old gas lights.

    Shop signs were strictly controlled, and about five hundred of eleven hundred buildings were listed.

    Zivas dates the works from 1979 to 1986, after studies that began in 1973.

    People came back to live in Plaka, and Europa Nostra gave the scheme a medal.

  7. 7

    Anafiotika

    In the narrow lanes of Anafiotika, just under the rock of the Acropolis. This is stop 7 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Anafiotika feels like a village on a Cycladic island, tucked under the Acropolis rock.

    By the city guide's account, it was built in the middle of the nineteenth century by stonemasons from the island of Anafi.

    King Otto had brought them to Athens to build his new palace.

    By night, the guide says, they put up small whitewashed homes for their families here.

    The slope offered ground like the one they knew at home, and it was cheap.

    The houses began as illegal building.

    Two churches of the seventeenth century roughly mark its edges, Saint George of the Rock to the east and Saint Simeon to the west.

    On the edge of the quarter stands an older church, Agios Nikolaos Rangavas, usually dated to the eleventh century.

    It belonged to the Rangavas family, the line of the emperor Michael the First, who ruled in the early ninth century.

    Its small bell is the one that, as the story goes, was hidden in a crypt when church bells were banned.

    In 1833 it was the first bell to ring for the end of Ottoman rule in Athens.

    It rang first again on the twelfth of October 1944, when the German troops left.

  8. 8

    The Lysicrates Monument

    In the little square on Tripodon Street, beside the round marble monument. This is stop 8 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Lysicrates Monument is the best-preserved monument of its kind in Athens.

    An inscription on it says that Lysicrates set it up in 335 or 334 BC.

    He was a choregos, one of the rich citizens who paid for a chorus at the drama festivals.

    His chorus won, and the prize was a tripod, which stood on top of this little round building.

    This street, the Street of the Tripods, was lined with such monuments on the way to the Theatre of Dionysus.

    Six Corinthian columns stand on a square stone base, among the earliest of that order in Athens.

    The frieze tells how Dionysus was seized by pirates.

    Later it gathered nicknames.

    One story says the orator Demosthenes shut himself inside to train his voice with pebbles in his mouth, another that he studied here late at night by lamplight.

    Another name, the Lantern of Diogenes, links it to the philosopher.

    In 1669, the Ministry of Culture says, Capuchin friars built it into their monastery and used it as a reading room.

    Lord Byron stayed there, and Lord Elgin tried and failed to ship the whole monument to England.

    The monastery was destroyed in the War of Independence, but the monument survived.

    It was copied in Edinburgh, in London and as far away as Sydney.
