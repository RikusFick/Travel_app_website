  1. 1

    Ephesus on the mountain

    In the centre of the village, among the shops and cafés by the stream. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––

    This is Şirince, a village of white houses with tiled roofs, tucked into the hills about eight kilometres from Selçuk , and from ancient Ephesus below.

    Greek records knew it as Kirkince, and as Oreon Efes , Ephesus on the mountain. Ottoman archives call it Çirkince from the late sixteenth century into the nineteen-twenties. In the nineteenth century it was a prosperous place, mostly Greek Orthodox, living on figs grown for export and other produce from the surrounding hills. When the British engineer John Turtle Wood began digging at Ephesus in the eighteen-sixties, the villagers came to watch with pride. Wood wrote that they saw themselves as descendants of the ancient Ephesians.

    Look at the houses climbing the two slopes on either side of the stream. Most of what you see belongs to the nineteenth century, the village's richest years.

    The Church of St John the Baptist

    This Church of St John the Baptist , Ioannes Prodromos in the Greek dedication , was one of the village's two churches. The other stands on the hill at the entrance and is known as Saint Demetrios.

    A Greek inscription over the north door records that the church was built in eighteen hundred and five on the site of an older church that had been taken down. It is a rectangular building of cut stone, divided inside into three aisles. The central aisle was roofed with domes and a barrel vault.

    The two churches are reminders of a great turning point. In nineteen twenty-three, Greece and Turkey agreed to exchange their populations. The Orthodox Christians of Kirkince left for Greece. In their place came Muslim families from villages near Kavala, in northern Greece , among them Müştiyan and Somokol , who had had to leave their own homes. Both communities lost the places where they were born.

    Most of the families who left Kirkince settled together in northern Greece, in a village near Katerini later called Nea Efesos , New Ephesus. Vines, wine-making and olives had been the backbone of Kirkince. Official accounts say that economy was interrupted for a time under newcomers from a tobacco district, before those skills revived in later decades.

    The newcomers made the village their own. In the later nineteen-twenties the name changed. Ottoman papers had called it Çirkince for centuries. "Çirkince" sounds like , the Turkish word for "ugly". A careful scholarly study dates the change to nineteen twenty-seven under the İzmir governor Kazım Dirik; a tourism encyclopaedia places it in the mid-nineteen-twenties and records the popular story that Dirik said a place this beautiful could not be called ugly, and renamed it Şirince , "the pleasant one".

    The new name stuck. The village is known for its fruit wines , a revival of the old wine-making tradition of Kirkince.

    Below you are the rooftops of Şirince. This small village holds a long story: Greek Orthodox farmers, Muslim families from across the Aegean, and visitors who come for the lanes and the views.

    Most of the old houses have two storeys. The ground floors are thick stone. The upper living floors are timber frames with an infill of brick or stone, plastered and whitewashed. Upper rooms often jut out over the street, and the houses sit along the slope so that none blocks its neighbours' view.

    This stone church on the hill at the entrance to Şirince is known as the church of Saint Demetrios , also called Ayasosti in local naming. No dedication inscription survives, so its original name and exact building date are uncertain, but it belongs to the nineteenth century.

    It is a single long hall of rubble stone, covered by a ribbed timber barrel vault and lit by windows along the sides and in the west front. Inside stands a finely carved wooden iconostasis , the screen that closed off the sanctuary , with painted panels of the apostles.

    After the population exchange of nineteen twenty-three, newcomers used the building for a time as a mosque. A prayer niche was cut into the south wall, and the base of a minaret was added at the south-east corner. Later abandoned, the church has since been restored.
