    The Roman Bridge

    Start on the sixty-arch granite bridge spanning nearly eight hundred metres across the Guadiana River.

    Free sample: stop 1

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This long granite bridge, running on dozens of round arches across the wide bed of the Guadiana, is the Roman Bridge of Mérida, and it is the reason the town is here at all.

    Today it is about 790 metres long and stands on sixty arches; three of them stayed buried until the late 1990s, when the riverbanks were cleared. The Romans built it in the last decades of the first century BC, at the same moment as the town, at a shallow point where a natural island splits the river and the bed is solid rock. Originally it was two bridges, one to each side of the island.

    The bridge has a core of Roman concrete, rubble bound with lime mortar, faced with large granite blocks left rough on the outside. The piers are rounded on the upstream side, so they part the current, and many have small openings above them that let floodwater through, easing the push of the river on the structure. Where the two halves met, a great dam-like breakwater stood on the island, splitting and steering the water in floods.

    The town was founded in 25 BC as Augusta Emerita, a colony for veterans of the emperor Augustus, and became capital of the province of Lusitania. Roads to Lisbon, Córdoba, Toledo and Zaragoza met here, and all had to cross this bridge, including the great north–south road later called the Vía de la Plata. In 1993 UNESCO listed the remains of the Roman town as a World Heritage Site.

    It is a patchwork of repairs. The ten arches nearest the town are the most original, with their rusticated stone blocks. The central section was rebuilt after a great flood in 1603 and finished in 1611, joining the two old bridges into one, and further arches were blown up in the Peninsular War against Napoleon and rebuilt in the nineteenth century.
