  1. 1

    The Corniche

    On the Corniche promenade, facing the sea. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are on the Abu Dhabi Corniche, the city's great seafront, which runs for about eight kilometres along the north-western shore of the island. On one side are the towers of the city; on the other, beaches, palm-lined paths, cycle lanes and the calm water of the Gulf.

    The shore did not always look like this. Before oil, Abu Dhabi was a small town of coral-stone and palm-frond houses gathered around the fort of Qasr Al Hosn, a few hundred metres inland from where you stand. The beach was a working place. Boats were drawn up on the sand, and fish was landed and sold. Pearling dhows set out from here each summer for the oyster banks, crewed by divers who held their breath on a weighted line while a puller waited above.

    Oil exports began in the 1960s, and under Sheikh Zayed the town was replanned as a capital. The new seafront was made, not found. Through the 1970s dredgers worked along this coast, building the breakwater and pumping sand and spoil from the seabed onto the shallows behind sea walls. Photographs from the mid-1970s show the reclaimed ground drying and cracking in the sun near where Emirates Palace now stands. On that new land the Corniche road was laid out, with gardens between the road and the water. The city's first five-star hotel, the Hilton, opened on the Corniche in 1973.

    Look along the beach. Parts are public beach with lifeguards, and families come in the cooler evenings to walk, cycle and picnic. The water is shallow and protected by islands and sandbanks offshore. Look, too, at the lawns, shaded seating and fountains between road and beach. Almost nothing green grows here without help, and these gardens are watered constantly, much of it with desalinated or treated water. That same technology turned a small island town into a city of more than a million people.

    The Breakwater and the flag

    On the Breakwater, with the city skyline across the water. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Breakwater is the strip of land at the western end of the Corniche, built out into the sea in the 1970s reclamation works to shelter the new shore. From here you get one of the best views of the Abu Dhabi skyline, a line of towers rising straight from the water's edge.

    Look up at the flagpole. It stands about 123 metres high. When it was put up in 2001, Guinness World Records recognised it as the world's tallest free-standing flagpole, a title other countries have since taken. The pole is a steel mast, made in tapering sections. A mast this tall needs regular care in salt air and summer heat. In 2016 it was refurbished: the top ten metres were replaced, LED lighting was added, and internal ladders were fitted so that technicians could climb it from the inside.

    The flag is the flag of the United Arab Emirates: green, white and black horizontal stripes with a vertical red band at the hoist. It was designed in 1971 by a young Emirati, Abdullah Mohammed Al Maainah, then about nineteen. His design was chosen in a public competition, and the flag was first raised on 2 December 1971, the day the federation was born. The colours are often explained as fertility, peace, the defeat of enemies and the courage of the people; the designer himself has said he drew them from a line of Arabic poetry about Arab unity and values. Both explanations are widely repeated.

    Every year on Flag Day, early in November, people across the country raise the flag together at the same moment. On national days, this stretch of waterfront fills with crowds, and fireworks are launched over the water.

  3. 3

    Heritage Village: the souk and the crafts

    Inside Heritage Village, near the craft workshops. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Heritage Village was opened in April 2001 by Sheikh Sultan bin Zayed Al Nahyan. It was built by the Emirates Heritage Club, which he chaired. It covers about 16,800 square metres, and the aim was to show the whole traditional life of the country in one place: fishing, farming, herding and desert life.

    Look for the workshops. The craftsmen here work without machinery. A metalworker hammers steel into a khanjar, the curved ceremonial dagger once worn at the belt by Emirati men. Its hilt and sheath were often decorated with silver. A potter throws cups, bowls and water jars on the wheel. In the old days, unglazed water jars were valued because water seeping through the clay evaporates and keeps the contents cool. A glass-blower turns discarded bottles and window glass into ornaments. A woodworker makes boxes inlaid with shells from the Gulf. Visitors are sometimes invited to try.

    Look also for the women's crafts. Al Sadu is woven on a narrow ground loom from sheep, goat and camel hair, spun by hand and dyed, in bold bands of geometric pattern, for tent dividers, rugs, cushions and camel trappings. Al Khoos uses dried palm leaves, split into strips and plaited into long braids, which are then sewn edge to edge into baskets, mats and food covers. Both crafts are on UNESCO's lists of intangible cultural heritage.

    The little market is called Al Souq Al Shaabi, the people's market, and sells spices, perfumes, herbs and crafts. Notice the buildings around you: walls of coral stone or mud, and roofs and fences of palm fronds, called barasti. Barasti houses were the summer homes of the coast. The fronds let the sea breeze straight through while giving shade.

    Desert camp, falaj and mosque

    Near the Bedouin tent and the water display. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Here is a Bedouin camp. The black tents were woven from goat hair by the women of the family, in long strips sewn together and stretched over wooden poles with guy ropes. The loose weave lets hot air escape in summer, and when it rains the fibres are said to swell and close, keeping the inside largely dry. Inside, a woven Sadu curtain divided the tent. One side was for the men to receive guests, with coffee always ready. The other side was for the women and family. Hospitality was a duty in the desert: a traveller could expect shelter and food for some days without being asked his business.

    Nearby is a model of a falaj, the irrigation channel that brought water from underground to the date gardens of the oases. It runs on gravity alone, along a gently sloping tunnel dug by hand from a line of shafts. Its water is shared out by time, each farmer receiving his turn to open the gate. Several aflaj in Al Ain are still flowing.

    Look for the well too. Water was raised in a leather bucket on a rope that ran over a wooden pulley. To pull it up, an animal, often a bull or camel, walked down a sloping ramp away from the well, then back up again for the next load. It was slow, steady work, done every day of the year.

    There is also a small mosque in the village, a reminder that prayer was part of daily life in every settlement, however small. Please keep your voice low as you pass it.

    From the edge of the village, look across the water to the city: the old way of life and the new, only a few hundred metres apart.

  5. 5

    Emirates Palace from outside

    On the approach to Emirates Palace. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    In front of you is Emirates Palace. It was built for a deadline. Abu Dhabi had been chosen to host the Gulf Cooperation Council summit in December 2005, and it wanted a building that could house the visiting heads of state, hold the summit and then serve as a great hotel. The design was by the architects WATG, with chief architect John Elliott. Construction, by the Belgian contractor BESIX, began at the end of 2001. At the peak, about 20,000 workers were on site. The building was completed in 2004 and opened in 2005, in time for the summit.

    Look at its scale. The complex covers about 85 hectares, on land reclaimed from the sea. Its main building stretches about a kilometre from end to end, with its own beach of about 1.3 kilometres, and the white sand was imported from Algeria. Around it are gardens with some 8,000 trees, where more than 150 species of birds have been recorded.

    Look at the colour. The stone of the façade was chosen to echo the deep red sands of Al Ain, which turn pink at sunset. One of the architects has also pointed out a practical side: a sand-coloured building does not look dirty after a dust storm. The façade's traditional patterns were made with modern etching and casting.

    Count the domes if you can. There are 114 smaller domes, and above them the great central dome. The building sits on a rise, so it can be seen from kilometres away. The road to the entrance winds through gardens, past fountains and under arches. The architects designed that approach on purpose, to build anticipation. Underneath, a two-lane service road runs below the whole complex, so deliveries are never seen.

    Besides being a hotel, with 394 rooms and suites, including 22 residential suites for heads of state, it has hosted state visits, summits and national events. Since 2020 it has been run as Emirates Palace Mandarin Oriental.

  6. 6

    Under the central dome

    In the grand atrium under the central dome. This is stop 6 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Look up into the central dome. Its highest point is given as about 72.6 metres above the ground, and the atrium beneath is usually described as about 42 metres across. One of its architects has called it among the largest interior spaces anywhere, bigger than St Paul's Cathedral in London. Yet it was designed to feel calm rather than overwhelming.

    The decoration is geometric, in the tradition of Islamic art, worked in natural stone, antique gold and mother-of-pearl. The gold is real: according to the hotel and its architects, about 26,000 metres of 22-carat gold leaf were applied inside the building. It is cared for by an in-house gold-leaf specialist. Gold leaf is beaten so thin that it is laid on with a soft brush over an adhesive layer, then burnished to a shine.

    Now look down at the floor. More than 90,000 square metres of marble were used in the complex. Here in the main space, the architects used granite cut with water jets into much larger pieces than older methods allowed. One of them has said that this "supersizing" is part of why the building feels serene: fewer joints, larger calm surfaces.

    The numbers elsewhere are on the same scale: 1,002 crystal chandeliers, some 7,000 doors, 128 kitchens, and around 200 fountains. There is a ballroom for about 2,500 guests and a theatre described as the size of a West End stage. The palace was designed in three parts: private palaces for each Gulf head of state, a conference centre, and the hotel. That is why it feels as much like a seat of ceremony as a place to stay.

    Stand still for a moment under the dome and listen. Even with people moving around you, sound rises into the dome and fades. It is a modern building, made to feel like a palace from a story.

    Etihad Towers

    Near the Emirates Palace entrance road, looking across Corniche Road toward the five glass towers. This is stop 7 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Across the road from the palace stand five towers of glass and steel that seem to lean and twist as you walk past them. Together they are Etihad Towers, a single complex finished in 2011 and designed by DBI Design, an Australian practice, which describes winning the commission through a two-stage international competition.

    The towers differ in height. The tallest, Tower 2, rises about 305 metres, and the shortest about 218 metres. Between them they hold a five-star hotel, now the Conrad Abu Dhabi Etihad Towers, apartments, an office tower and a shopping area at their base. The architects describe the whole as a "mini-city", where people can live, work, stay and shop without leaving the complex; they give the total as 885 apartments across three residential towers.

    Look at the shapes: each tower is a smooth, curving form of glass, and from this side the five read as one group at the western end of the Corniche, facing the palace and the open water. The architects present the five as parts of a single design rather than separate buildings.

    High up in Tower 2, on the 74th floor, is the Observation Deck at 300, named for its height of about 300 metres. It is described as the highest viewing point in Abu Dhabi. From there you can see the palace below you, the full curve of the Corniche running toward the city centre, the Breakwater and the islands of the Gulf beyond.

    Building began in 2006, according to the architects, the year after the palace across the road opened. Together the palace, finished for the summit of 2005, and the towers, finished six years later, frame the western end of the Corniche, two very different answers to the question of how a capital should look.
