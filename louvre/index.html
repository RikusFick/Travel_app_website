    The Pyramid

    This part of the route looks at The Pyramid.

    Free sample: stop 1

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Pyramid of the Louvre, all glass and steel, was designed by the architect I. M. Pei and opened in 1989 as the museum's new main entrance. It stands about twenty-one metres high on a base about thirty-five metres wide.

    Many Parisians hated the idea at first: a modern pyramid in front of a royal palace. Today it is one of the city's best-known sights.

    Around you rises the palace itself. Kings lived here until Louis XIV moved the court to Versailles, and in 1793, during the Revolution, the Louvre opened as a public museum.

    Look closely at the skin of the pyramid. It is made of 603 diamond-shaped panes and 70 triangles of glass, held on a web of metal bars and cables. Its sides rise at an angle of about fifty-one degrees. Pei wanted the glass as clear as possible, so it would not tint the old façades seen through it; by one account, a special low-iron glass was made for it, and the metal frame of steel and aluminium weighs about two hundred tonnes.

    The pyramid was born from the Grand Louvre plan, announced by President François Mitterrand in 1981, which moved the Finance Ministry out of the palace and gave almost all of it to the museum. Mitterrand chose Pei himself, without a competition. The underground hall beneath your feet was the real point of the project: one central entrance, with passages leading to each wing. Critics called it an Egyptian tomb in a Renaissance courtyard. A rumour that it has exactly 666 panes still circulates; it does not.

    Around the main pyramid stand three smaller ones, which light the hall below, and a fifth, upside down, lies under the Carrousel to the west. The design was fought over for years; by one account, a full-size outline of the pyramid was strung up in this courtyard with cables in 1985, so that doubters could judge the volume for themselves.
