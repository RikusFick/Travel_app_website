  1. 1

    Bab al-Azab, the old lower gate

    On the road or the open square below the western walls of the Citadel, looking up at the tall stone gate of Bab al-Azab. You view the gate from here; visitors do not walk through it. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Read along

    This is Bab al-Azab, the old main gate in the southern walls of the Citadel. The gate you see was built in 1754 by Radwan Katkhuda al-Galfi, an Ottoman-era commander.

    The fortress above it is much older. Sultan Salah al-Din, known in Europe as Saladin, ordered it in 1176. He chose a spur of the Muqattam hills, where rocky high ground made attack difficult.

    Saladin did not live to see it finished. It was completed under Sultan al-Kamil, who moved in around 1207. From then on, it was the official residence of Egypt's rulers. That lasted until the mid-nineteenth century, when Khedive Ismail moved the court to Abdeen Palace, in the new city below.

    Now picture the narrow, steep lane that climbs behind this gate. On 1 March 1811, Muhammad Ali, the Ottoman governor of Egypt, held a ceremony at the Citadel. It was to honour his second son, Ahmad Tusun, who was leaving to lead an army to Arabia. He invited the Mamluk leaders. They came in full dress, on decorated horses.

    As they rode down this lane in the procession, troops barred the gate ahead. Soldiers on top of the walls opened fire. Their leader, Shahin Bey, was killed outright, and most of the others with him. Estimates of how many Mamluks came that day vary greatly. Some counts give twenty-four leaders and sixty followers.

    The massacre ended the Mamluks as a power in Egypt. Muhammad Ali became its uncontested ruler, and he made the Citadel the centre of his rule.
