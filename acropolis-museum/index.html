    Under the glass floor of the Acropolis Museum lie the ruins of an ancient neighbourhood. They came to light in the excavation carried out for the museum's construction, and the building was designed to take them in.

    Architects and archaeologists worked closely together to place the museum's foundation columns without damaging the remains. The lead designer was Bernard Tschumi, whose firm wrote that the site confronted them with sensitive archaeological excavations.

    The first human activity on this plot dates from somewhere between 3500 and 3000 BC. Until the early fifth century BC it lay on the outskirts of the city, outside the old walls.

    By the beginning of the first century BC, a dense network of streets ran here, lined with houses with small courtyards, shops and workshops. In 86 BC the troops of the Roman general Sulla destroyed the neighbourhood.

    It boomed again from the middle of the second century AD, with larger houses, colonnaded courtyards, painted rooms and, in the grandest homes, private baths. In AD 267 raiders from the north, the Herulians, ravaged the city and destroyed the site.

    The site was finally abandoned in the early thirteenth century AD.

    The museum opened to the public in June 2009, and the excavation itself opened to visitors in June 2019.
