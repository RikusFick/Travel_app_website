  1. 1

    The Ounila riverbed

    On the riverbed or the footbridge, between the modern village and the ksar. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Look up from the crossing. In front of you, a whole village of earth rises up the slope of a hill, tower above tower, to a ruined granary at the very top. This is the ksar of Ait Benhaddou, a fortified village at the foot of the High Atlas on its southern side, within Ouarzazate province. Morocco's 2021 conservation report places it 30 kilometres north-west of the town of Ouarzazate. The protected area itself is small, about three hectares, with a buffer zone of some sixteen hectares around it.

    Beneath your feet is the river of the Ounila valley, which Morocco's 2021 report calls the Oued El Maleh. The same report says the bridge linking its two banks brought new life in and around the site. Many families left the ksar, and UNESCO names that abandonment as a threat to the earthen buildings. Morocco's latest report says it is hard to encourage the residents to come back.

    Look at the green strip of fields and trees along the banks. The river feeds them, but it also floods. On the 2021 report's site plan, this belt of farmland is marked as a zone where building is not allowed, and the plan also marks khettaras, underground water channels. Morocco's latest conservation report says the banks have been reinforced against erosion since 2006. Its restoration plan also protects the site and the fields from floods, partly with gabions, wire cages filled with stones.

    The village was a trading post on a caravan route through this valley. UNESCO names the mountain pass it used as Tizi n'Telouet.

  2. 2

    The lower gate and walls

    At the main gate in the defensive wall at the foot of the ksar. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are at the edge of the defences. The houses of the ksar crowd together inside a wall reinforced by towers at its corners. Look along the wall and pick out the towers.

    Now look at the gate itself. It is a baffle gate: the passage turns, so that no one could charge straight through it.

    Morocco's 2021 report says the ksar keeps its original layout in full: its places of worship, squares and houses, its cemetery, its landmark towers, and the wall that marks its edge.

    The ksar was one of a string of trading posts on the road from the lands south of the Sahara to Marrakech, through the Draa Valley and the High Atlas passes.

    UNESCO calls the ksar a striking model of the building traditions of southern Morocco, and a complete panorama of the earthen building techniques of the pre-Saharan valleys. It describes the living quarters as a compact group, closed and suspended. Yet none of the buildings you see is thought to predate the seventeenth century. UNESCO adds that their structure and techniques had spread through the southern valleys long before.

    The same listing counts the ksar an outstanding one, showing the main kinds of earthen building seen since then along the Draa, Todgha, Dadès and Souss valleys.

  3. 3

    The lower lanes and the tower houses

    In a narrow lane of the lower village, in front of one of the tall fortified houses. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are inside a village built of earth and wood. Put your hand on a wall, gently. Morocco's conservation reports name the two traditional earth techniques used here: pisé, which is rammed earth, and adobe, which is mud brick.

    The 2021 report says the buildings are raised entirely in raw earth, packed down in layers inside a frame, and in adobe. UNESCO describes these buildings as perfectly adapted to the climate, and the 2021 report praises the comfort they give against heat and cold. But earth needs constant care. UNESCO warns that the buildings are very vulnerable when they are no longer maintained and repaired.

    Look at the doors and windows on the lanes. They are made of wood. UNESCO reports that the building materials have remained earth and wood, and that local monitoring has kept cement out. The few concrete lintels that slipped through have been hidden under earth rendering. UNESCO added Ait Benhaddou to its World Heritage List in 1987.

    Now look at the houses. Some are modest, but others look like small castles, with high towers at their corners. Look up to the top of the towers. The upper parts are decorated with geometric patterns in clay brick. UNESCO's description notes that the large houses down in this lower village were regularly maintained, with well conserved decoration. Morocco's 2025 restoration plan counts five kasbahs, as these tall fortified houses are called, in the ksar; its 2021 report speaks of three. After the earthquake of September 2023, Morocco's report found deep vertical cracks in most of their facades.

    The 2021 report shows one lordly house, called a tighermt, whose facade collapsed in the floods of 2014 and was restored in 2018.

  4. 4

    The heart of the village

    On the climb, among the community buildings of the ksar. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    A ksar is more than a cluster of houses. It is a community. There is a mosque, a caravanserai where the trading caravans could stop, and a public square. There is also the shrine of a local holy man, Sidi Ali or Amer. Outside the walls lay the threshing floors for the grain, and the ksar has two cemeteries, one Muslim and one Jewish.

    The mosque is a place of prayer, so please pass it quietly.

    Morocco's 2021 report also counts a synagogue among the places of worship of the ksar, with the mosque and the saint's shrine.

    UNESCO's World Heritage Committee has noted with concern that shops and tourist lodgings are spreading inside the ksar at the expense of homes. One idea in Morocco's 2025 report is a new craft complex in the modern village, to take in all the shops set up inside the ksar.

    The first restoration campaign ran from 1990 to 1995, the 2021 report records, when the site was close to abandoned.

    It was carried out by CERKAS, Morocco's conservation centre for the earthen architecture of the Atlas regions, with funding from the United Nations Development Programme and technical help from UNESCO.

    Since 2014 a state programme has been restoring the houses, with the stated aim of helping residents stay on in decent living conditions. Its rules call for traditional techniques, the involvement of the residents, and the training of a skilled local workforce. Under the safeguarding plan in force since 2015, any rebuilding has to respect traditional forms, materials and techniques, and new building is strictly controlled. UNESCO's listing itself warns that this way of living in earth has become vulnerable through irreversible social, economic and cultural change.

    If someone invites you into a shop and you only want to look, a polite word is enough.

  5. 5

    The agadir and the view from the summit

    At the top of the hill, beside the remains of the granary, looking out over the valley. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You have reached the top. Around you are the remains of the agadir, a large fortified granary, built on the highest point of the hill. Morocco's reports call it Ighrem n'iqddarn, the potters' granary, and the 2021 site plan marks the ruin of a collective granary here, beside the ruins of a fortified enclosure. UNESCO counts a fort and a loft on the hilltop among the village's shared spaces, and the current restoration plan includes the remains of the granary and the fortress.

    The 2021 report says the floods of 2014 damaged this summit building and the main lane at the bottom of the ksar, and that the stone-built passage up to the fortress collapsed. Find a safe spot to stand, away from the edges and crumbling walls.

    Now look out. From up here you can see a long way along the valley. Below you, the ksar spills down the hillside, its flat roofs and towers stepping toward the river. Beyond the river, palms line the banks of the Ounila, and on the far side sits the modern village.

    In September 2023 an earthquake struck the Al Haouz region. Morocco's report puts its epicentre about 110 kilometres from here as the crow flies. The next day, it adds, CERKAS sent an emergency team to assess the damage and make the passages safe, so that visitors could move freely inside. The report calls the damage here not considerable: the buildings that had been restored and kept in good condition held up well. Its lesson is that local earth, sand and wood, with the old techniques of pisé and adobe, are a building culture that matters in a disaster.

    Watch your step on the way down.
