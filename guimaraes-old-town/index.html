    The Toural and the Wall Tower

    In the Toural square, facing the old tower with the words "Aqui nasceu Portugal".

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Toural is the main square of Guimarães, and on the old wall tower beside it are the words "Aqui nasceu Portugal": Portugal was born here.

    The words recall the events of 1128, when Afonso Henriques won the battle of São Mamede near Guimarães and took control of the county of Portugal, the step that led to an independent kingdom. Guimarães is often called the first capital of Portugal, and the early history of the town is bound up with the birth of Portuguese national identity.

    In the seventeenth century this was an open space outside the main gate of the walled town, where cattle fairs and other markets were held. In 1878 it was turned into a public garden, fenced with an iron railing.

    After Portugal became a republic, the garden was moved elsewhere, and a statue of Afonso Henriques, the first king, was placed in the centre of the square. Some years later the statue went up to the castle park. Around 2011 and 2012, the years when Guimarães was European Capital of Culture, the square was remodelled once more, and a Renaissance fountain of three basins, which had stood here in the sixteenth century, was put back.

    On the west side of the square stands the Church of São Pedro, which the Pope raised to the rank of a basilica in 1751. The tower with the famous words is the Alfândega Tower, the only tower left from the medieval town wall.

    The Padrão do Salado

    In the Largo da Oliveira, beside the open Gothic porch in front of the church.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Padrão do Salado is an open Gothic porch of four pointed arches, sheltering a stone cross.

    According to tradition, King Afonso the Fourth had it raised in the 1340s to mark the battle of the Salado, when he helped his son-in-law, King Afonso the Eleventh of Castile, against a Muslim invasion. The battle was fought on the thirtieth of October 1340, near the Salado river close to Tarifa, in the far south of Spain.

    The cross inside was given by Pero Esteves, a merchant from Guimarães who lived in Lisbon. The town's own page dates the cross to 1342, while the state heritage record puts it nine years after the porch, so the exact year is uncertain. It is carved in limestone, was once gilded and painted, and is said to have come from Normandy. It shows Christ crucified on one side and the Virgin on the other. The saints carved on its shaft have no link to Guimarães or to the battle, which is one reason it is thought to have been made for another place.

    The monument stands in the Largo da Oliveira, in front of the church, and it is a classified National Monument.

    The Church of Nossa Senhora da Oliveira

    In the Largo da Oliveira, facing the church front.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Church of Nossa Senhora da Oliveira, Our Lady of the Olive Tree, faces the square with its Gothic front.

    Its story begins in the tenth century, when Countess Mumadona Dias had a monastery built here, the same noblewoman whose castle stood on the hill above. In the twelfth century the monastery became a collegiate church, run by a college of canons, and in the Middle Ages it was an important religious centre, much visited by pilgrims on their way to Santiago de Compostela.

    Little is left of the first buildings. The church owes its present Gothic form to King João the First. Before the battle of Aljubarrota in 1385, which secured his throne and Portugal's independence from Castile, he made a vow to Our Lady of the Olive Tree, and after his victory he paid for the rebuilding of the church.

    In 1911, under the new republic, the collegiate was dissolved. Its old buildings beside the church now house the Alberto Sampaio Museum, named after a nineteenth-century historian of Guimarães. The museum was created by decree in 1928 and opened to the public in 1931, to show the goods of the region's vanished religious institutions, above all those of the collegiate itself. Its collection is mainly sacred art, with important pieces from the fourteenth, fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

    Among the works currently displayed in the museum are two pieces tied to King João the First and the battle of Aljubarrota. One is a padded military tunic of the fourteenth century, the loudel, said to have been worn by the king at the battle. The other is a triptych of the Nativity in gilded silver. The museum tells it as a thank-offering from the king to Our Lady of the Olive Tree, and records two versions of its origin: that the king gave his own weight in silver to have it made, or that it belonged to the King of Castile and was taken as booty at Aljubarrota.

    The Praça de Santiago

    In the Praça de Santiago, looking towards the old town hall.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Praça de Santiago is the small square next to the Largo da Oliveira, lined with old houses with balconies under projecting roofs.

    The two squares are joined by the old town hall, the Antigos Paços do Concelho, whose ground floor is an open arcade. On its roofline stands a stone figure of a warrior, placed there in 1877 and brought from the old customs house, which was pulled down in 1876. Tradition says it stands for the town itself, or for the part the men of Guimarães played in the conquests in Africa.

    The square takes its name from Saint James, Santiago in Portuguese. According to tradition, the apostle brought an image of the Virgin Mary to Guimarães and it was placed in a square here. A chapel of Saint James stood in the square until the late nineteenth century, and nothing of it remains. To recall it, a scallop shell, the symbol of Saint James, is set into the paving, together with the opening Latin words of the charter that Count Henry gave to the settlers of Guimarães: "To you, men who came to settle in Guimarães, and to those who wish to live here."

    Most of the houses here belong to the great period of building that followed the Middle Ages. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries brought grand houses, new civic buildings and the laying out of the town's squares, and many of the houses around the Largo da Oliveira and the Praça de Santiago date from the seventeenth century.

    The houses around these squares show the building style typical of Guimarães: a ground floor of granite, with the upper floors built on a timber frame. This way of building was developed here in the Middle Ages.

    The Rua de Santa Maria

    On the Rua de Santa Maria, the old street between the castle hill and the Largo da Oliveira.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Rua de Santa Maria is one of the oldest streets of Guimarães, running between the castle hill and the Largo da Oliveira.

    The town grew from two cores. Countess Mumadona's monastery stood in the lower town, where the Church of Our Lady of the Olive Tree is today, and her castle stood on the hill above, in the upper town. This street was laid out to join them. For centuries it was lived in by clerics, nobles and people of standing, such as the canons of the collegiate, which made it the street of the town's elite. Beside the grand houses, some with coats of arms and wrought-iron balconies, stand simpler houses with fine wooden balconies.

    The town was later enclosed by a defensive wall, and the two cores grew together into one. Beyond the walls, the town spread around the convents of the Franciscan and Dominican friars, who settled in Guimarães in the Middle Ages. Despite changes in modern times, the old town keeps its medieval street plan.

    The Couros Zone

    In the Couros quarter, beside the old tanning tanks and the tannery buildings.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Couros Zone is the old leather-tanning quarter of Guimarães, named after the craft, couros meaning leather.

    It grew up outside the medieval walls, along a small stream that also carries the name Couros, which flows on towards the river Ave. Leather was worked here from the Middle Ages. For centuries the hides came from cattle slaughtered in the region, and later they also arrived from overseas. The work was slow, heavy and wet. The skins were cleaned of hair and fat, then soaked again and again in stone tanks with crushed oak bark, whose tannin turned raw hide into leather. The industry was at its height in the nineteenth century and the first half of the twentieth. After that it declined, and the last tannery here closed in 2005. The tanneries, workers' houses and streets of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries remain.

    The historic centre of Guimarães became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001, and the listing was later extended to include the Couros Zone.
