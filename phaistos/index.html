    Read along

    Phaistos holds the western end of the Messara, and no plain on Crete is wider or more fertile. Water came from the river below, the Geropotamos, which ancient writers called the Lethaios, and it loops round the hill on its east and north sides. Its position was about control: the ruler here is thought to have commanded both the harvest of the plain and the route down to the sea and the harbours of the Messara bay. In the south of the island no city was richer or stronger.

    In legend, Minos founded it as one of his three great cities on the island, and his brother Rhadamanthys reigned here after him. Homer knew it too: in his list of the cities that sent men to Troy, this is the one he calls well peopled.

    The Minoan story at Phaistos is dated from about the middle of the third millennium BC. People had made their homes on this hill long before that, and under the later palace the excavators found the stone footings of Neolithic houses, with figurines, stone tools and potsherds in them.

    The hill is only about a hundred metres above sea level, yet the view runs across the whole valley to its mountains, great Psiloritis among them, with the Libyan Sea to the south.

    A smaller neighbour stood down the same river: Agia Triada, a royal villa that some call the little palace. It is thought to have been the summer home of the king of Phaistos, and it has given up more fragments of wall painting than the rest of Minoan Crete put together.

    The rediscovery began with an Englishman, Captain Spratt, who identified the site while travelling across the island. After him the digging became an Italian story, beginning with the first systematic campaign under Federico Halbherr in 1900, though accounts differ on when that campaign ended.

    In 1950 Doro Levi took over, and went down to the earliest levels of the palace and to what lay beneath them. In 2025 the site joined the UNESCO World Heritage List, as one of six Minoan palatial centres.
