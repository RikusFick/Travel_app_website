    Read along

    You are standing inside the outline of a mosque meant to be the biggest anywhere in the Muslim West. In front of you is a huge open platform. At one end rises a single great tower. At the other end, under a green pyramid roof, stands a modern royal tomb.

    Two eras of Moroccan history meet here. One is the late twelfth century and the Almohad caliphs. The other is the twentieth century, and the reign in which Morocco won back its independence.

    The caliph Yaqub al-Mansur began the mosque. UNESCO and Morocco's World Heritage dossier both date the start of work to 1184, just after he came to the throne.

    A heritage catalogue notes another view. Some authors, among them the seventeenth-century chronicler Mármol, link the project to his victory at Alarcos, in Spain, in 1195.

    The same catalogue adds a small mystery. The name Hasan appears on the monument. Perhaps it belonged to the architect, but nothing else is known about him.

    The ground you are standing on is a single plan, more than eight hundred years old, which was never completed. Walk out across it slowly, and let its size sink in. Notice how quiet it is, in the middle of a capital city, and how the wind comes up off the river across the open stone.
