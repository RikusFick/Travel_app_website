    The Roman Theatre

    Begin at the terraced stone seating of Augustus's Roman Theatre uncovered beneath the hillside in 1951.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    These curved rows of stone seats, cut partly into the hillside below the walls, are the Roman theatre of Málaga, the main surviving monument of the Roman town of Malaca.

    The town was far older than Rome's rule. The Phoenicians founded it as Malaka, and by the sixth century BC it was one of the leading ports of the western Mediterranean. The theatre was built in the early years of the Empire, in the first century AD. Its lower seats rest on the slope of the hill, Greek-fashion, while the rest stand on built foundations. Seating followed rank: magistrates and priests on marble seats in the semicircular orchestra, knights in the lower rows, and slaves and women at the top. A marble inscription from the Flavian period runs between the orchestra and the stage.

    By the end of the second century the theatre was hardly used, and in the third it was abandoned. Fish-salting vats were dug into it to make garum, the Roman fish sauce, and some can still be seen; later the site became a cemetery.

    It lay forgotten until 1951, when work on a garden uncovered what was first taken for a gate in the city wall. It was the theatre's main entrance, and the rest ran under the Casa de la Cultura, a library and archive building. In the 1990s the city decided to demolish it to uncover the theatre. Its glass interpretation centre opened in 2010, its walls printed with the Lex Flavia Malacitana, the town's Roman charter.
