    Welcome to Ephesus: the Upper Gate

    Read along

    Welcome to Ephesus. You are at the Upper Gate, looking down the hill. Everything you can see, the white marble, the broken columns, the paved road curving down towards the trees, was once one of the great cities of the Roman world.

    This tour follows the same route an ancient visitor would have taken: in through the upper city, where the politicians and priests worked, down a street lined with fountains and statues, past the homes of the rich, to the famous Library of Celsus, and finally into a theatre that held more than twenty thousand people.

    A little history to set the scene. According to legend, Ephesus was founded by Androklos, a prince from Athens, who was told by an oracle to build his city where would show him the spot. His story is carved in stone on the porch frieze of the Temple of Hadrian, on Curetes Street. The city moved more than once over the centuries, and this site was laid out around three hundred years before Christ, by Lysimachus, one of Alexander the Great's generals.

    Then came Rome. In 133 BC, the last king of Pergamon left his kingdom to the Romans, and Ephesus became part of their new province of Asia, the richest province in the empire. Ephesus grew into its leading city, and by the time of the emperor Augustus it was firmly the capital, the seat of the Roman governor. Ships came and went from its harbour, pilgrims came to worship the goddess Artemis, and money poured in.

    How many people lived here? Nobody knows for sure. Modern studies based on the size of the city suggest somewhere between thirty and seventy thousand people, while older estimates went as high as two hundred thousand or more. Either way, this was one of the great cities of the Roman East.

    Ephesus was a port city, but the sea later retreated several kilometres away. The river slowly filled the harbour with silt, and that, as much as earthquakes or invaders, is what ended this city's story. The result is plain to see on Harbour Street.

    The city's ancient gate on this side, the Magnesian Gate, stands a short way up the road, outside the entrance. It takes its name from the road that ran from it to the city of Magnesia on the Maeander. The main path leads down to the large open square on the right.

    Ephesus has been listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site since 2015.
