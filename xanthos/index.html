    Read along

    Welcome to Xanthos, which UNESCO describes as the capital of ancient Lycia. The city was built on two hills overlooking the plain, near the village of Kınık. In inscriptions written in the Lycian language, its name appears as Arnna.

    Homer tells of Xanthians fighting at Troy under Sarpedon.

    Yet the ministry and Antalya's culture directorate both say that finds from the excavations do not take settlement here back before the eighth century BC. Elsewhere, the ministry traces the city's history back to around three thousand BC.

    On the right of the ramp up to the ruins, only the foundations of the Nereid Monument remain. The British Museum dates the monument to around three hundred and ninety to three hundred and eighty BC. It says the tomb was probably built for Erbinna, known in Greek as Arbinas, ruler of Xanthos.

    It resembled a Greek temple of the Ionic order, raised on a high podium in the local tradition for important burials. Between its columns stood statues most likely of Nereids, sea nymphs who were said to help sailors in fierce storms.

    Its friezes showed sieges and battles, either legendary or drawn from the ruler's own life. One panel shows a seated ruler in a Persian headdress, with an attendant holding a parasol behind him. As a Lycian ruler, the museum notes, he was ultimately answerable to the Great King of Persia.

    UNESCO says the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, was directly influenced by this monument.

    Antalya's culture directorate dates the discovery of Xanthos by Charles Fellows to eighteen thirty-eight. The British Museum places his two expeditions in the early eighteen-forties, when he found this monument lying in ruins.

    With Ottoman permission and British Museum funding he removed it, and the Royal Navy carried the stones down the river to a ship. The museum noted in twenty twenty that the monument's exact design was still debated.
