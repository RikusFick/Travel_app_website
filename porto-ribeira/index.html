    Avenida dos Aliados

    On the broad central walkway of the Avenida dos Aliados, looking up towards the City Hall tower.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Avenida dos Aliados is the great granite-paved avenue of Porto, climbing gently to the tower of the City Hall at its head.

    Its name means the Avenue of the Allies, after the nations on whose side Portugal fought in the First World War. Work began on 1 February 1916, when the old City Hall and a tangle of short streets and alleys were pulled down to give the city a new civic centre. The plan began with the English planner Barry Parker in 1915, and local architects including José Marques da Silva pushed back. The buildings that line the avenue follow the French Beaux-Arts taste, many of them crowned with domes, lanterns and small spires.

    Porto itself is far older. The Romans called the place Portus, the port, and the city has been bound to its river ever since. From here the old city falls away steeply to the quays where it began.

    In 1996 UNESCO listed the historic centre, together with the Luís I Bridge and the monastery of Serra do Pilar across the river, as a World Heritage site.

    São Bento Station

    Inside the entrance hall of São Bento railway station, surrounded by blue and white tile panels.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The entrance hall of São Bento station is lined from floor to ceiling with blue and white tiles, more than twenty-two thousand of them.

    The station stands on the site of a Benedictine convent, São Bento da Avé Maria, begun in 1518 on the orders of King Manuel the First; Bento is Portuguese for Benedict. After the convent was gone, the Porto architect José Marques da Silva designed the station, with a granite façade and a grand hall, in a style influenced by the French Beaux-Arts school. A wooden passenger shed opened here in 1896, work on the new station began in 1903, and the building was inaugurated on 5 October 1916.

    The tiles are the work of the painter Jorge Colaço, and the railway's heritage service dates them to between 1905 and 1908. The great panels show episodes from Portuguese history: the clash at Arcos de Valdevez, remembered both as a battle and as a tournament, and the knight Egas Moniz presenting himself and his sons to a king of León and Castile. Other panels show the history of transport, country work and local customs. They were fired at the Sacavém pottery factory near Lisbon, in the nationalist, history-loving taste of the early twentieth century.

    Porto Cathedral

    On the terrace in front of Porto Cathedral, high above the old city.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Porto Cathedral stands on a hill above the river, a fortress-like church of granite with two square towers, battlements and a great round rose window.

    Its Romanesque core dates from the twelfth century, the age when Portugal was becoming a kingdom. Its battlements and bare walls give it the look of a fortified church. Inside, the narrow central nave is covered by a stone barrel vault, held up by flying buttresses; some writers count it among the first churches in Portugal to use them.

    It is also a church of royal memory. In 1387 King João the First married the English princess Philippa of Lancaster here, a marriage that joined the royal houses of England and Portugal. One of their sons would be Henry the Navigator.

    Later centuries added their own layers: a Gothic cloister, a well-known silver altar, and, on the side façade, an elegant Baroque loggia of 1736 by Nicolau Nasoni, an Italian architect who worked in Porto in the 1700s.

  4. 4

    Luís I Bridge

    On the upper deck of the Luís I Bridge, high above the Douro, between Porto and Vila Nova de Gaia.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Luís I Bridge leaps the Douro in a single arch of iron, 172 metres across, carrying two decks: one high up at the level of the hilltops, the other down at the level of the quays.

    When it opened, it was the longest wrought-iron arch in the world. Its designer was the Belgian engineer Théophile Seyrig, who had earlier worked with Gustave Eiffel. The upper deck was inaugurated on 31 October 1886, and the lower deck was finished in 1888. They replaced an earlier suspension bridge on the same crossing.

    Porto and Gaia both rise steeply from the water, so the bridge serves two levels. The upper deck links the high town with the high bank, and the lower deck links the two quays.

    Below, on the Gaia bank, are the port wine lodges; above them stands the monastery of Serra do Pilar, part of the same World Heritage site.

    Each half of the arch was built out over the river from its own bank, section by section, until the two met in the middle.

    Praça da Ribeira

    In the Praça da Ribeira, the riverside square, among the tall narrow houses facing the Douro.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Praça da Ribeira opens onto the river, lined with tall, narrow houses painted and tiled in many colours, their balconies stacked above the quay.

    This is one of the oldest quarters of Porto. The square is documented from 1389, when a royal letter of King João the First ordered fresh fish to be brought here before it was sold. In the fourteenth century Porto spread along the riverbank as trade grew, and the waterfront filled with merchants, boatmen, coopers and sailors. The great Fernandine walls, begun under King Afonso the Fourth and finished in the 1370s under King Fernando, were built to take in this busy harbour district as well as the old town around the cathedral.

    Much of the square's present look comes from the eighteenth century, when it was remodelled under the influence of John Whitehead, the English consul in Porto. Only part of that plan was ever carried out.

    Casa do Infante

    In the Rua da Alfândega, in front of the stone façade of the Casa do Infante.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Casa do Infante, the Prince's House, is a solid medieval stone building a few steps from the river, and one of the oldest in Porto.

    It began as the royal customs house of Porto, raised in the fourteenth century under King Afonso the Fourth. Cargo arriving by river was brought here and taxed, and the royal mint later worked in the same complex. Today the building houses the city's historical archive and a site of the city museum.

    Tradition holds that Prince Henry, the son of King João the First and Philippa of Lancaster, was born here in 1394. The city archive keeps a parchment of 1394 recording what Porto spent on celebrating his baptism, four days after the birth; it is described as the oldest known written record of his life. Henry would become known as the Navigator, the patron of the voyages down the coast of Africa that opened the age of the Portuguese discoveries.

    Church of São Francisco

    In front of the Gothic Church of São Francisco, beside the Stock Exchange Palace, just above the river.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Church of São Francisco is a sober Gothic church of granite on the outside, with a great rose window, and inside a Baroque interior famous for its gold.

    The Franciscans, the order founded by Saint Francis of Assisi on a vow of poverty, came to Porto in 1223. The present church was begun in 1383, the year King Fernando the First died, and finished in the early 1400s, in the simple Gothic suited to a mendicant order. Its structure has changed little since then.

    Then, from the 1600s into the middle of the 1700s, carvers transformed the interior inside that Gothic shell. Walls, pillars, chapels and ceilings were covered with talha dourada, carved wood covered in gold leaf, so dense that the medieval stone almost disappears. Among the altarpieces is a Tree of Jesse, the family tree of Christ, with the kings of Judah rising on golden branches; this carved altarpiece was reworked in the early 1700s.

    In the early 1830s, during the siege of Porto, a violent fire hit the convent, and its cloister was later demolished. The Stock Exchange Palace was built next door, on that site.
