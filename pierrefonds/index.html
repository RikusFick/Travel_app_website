    The castle of Pierrefonds looks like the perfect medieval fortress, but most of what you see was built in the nineteenth century.

    Its enclosure has eight great towers, and each holds in a niche a statue of one of the Nine Worthies, the heroes of chivalry. The Worthies were a favourite theme of the late Middle Ages: three heroes of pagan antiquity, three of the Hebrew Bible, and three Christian kings, among them Charlemagne. Together they stood for the courage of the ideal knight. Along the top run two wall-walks, one above the other: the lower one roofed and resting on machicolations, the upper one open, with battlements.

    The first castle here was begun in the 1390s by Louis of Orléans, brother of King Charles VI, on a site where a castle had already stood since the twelfth century. In 1392 the king had made the county of Valois, which included Pierrefonds, into a duchy for Louis.

    The works were directed by Jean le Noir, and the castle was built in less than ten years, with money from the dowry of Louis's wife, Valentina Visconti. Louis was murdered in Paris in 1407. His death left the castle at the centre of a long quarrel between the crown and the house of Orléans. In 1413, before handing Pierrefonds back to Louis's son, the royal side set fire to its roofs.

    The fortress was fought over again in the wars of the late sixteenth century, and in 1595 it was even seized in the name of King Philip II of Spain. In 1617 King Louis XIII had Pierrefonds besieged and dismantled, and it stood as a romantic ruin for more than two centuries.

    The reason was politics. In the troubled early years of the reign, the castle's owner, François-Annibal d'Estrées, had joined the party of discontented nobles led by the Prince of Condé, and in March 1617 troops sent by Richelieu besieged and took the castle. A bombardment opened the way in. Afterwards the great towers were breached with explosive mines, and the floors and roof timbers were burned. The demolition was never finished, because the task was simply too big.

    Napoleon I later bought the ruin for a very small sum. Romantic taste then made it famous. In August 1832 King Louis-Philippe gave a banquet here for the marriage of his daughter Louise to Leopold the First, the first king of the Belgians, and the painter Camille Corot painted the ruins many times between 1834 and 1866. In 1848 Pierrefonds was listed as a historic monument.
