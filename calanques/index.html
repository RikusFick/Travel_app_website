    The Calanques National Park stretches along about eighty kilometres of coast between Marseille, Cassis and La Ciotat.

    A calanque is a narrow inlet with steep limestone walls, cut by rivers long ago and later flooded by the rising sea. The park was created in 2012, the tenth national park in France, and it is the only one in Europe that lies on the edge of a big city and covers both land and sea. More than four fifths of its heart lies at sea.

    The white rock around you is a limestone called Urgonian, laid down more than a hundred million years ago in a warm, shallow tropical sea. Look closely at a broken surface and you may see the shells of rudists, extinct molluscs, distant cousins of oysters, that lived on that seabed and were turned to stone with the mud that buried them. The rock is so cracked and porous that rainwater sinks straight through it, hollowing caves and feeding springs that rise under the sea near Cassis, while the surface stays almost without water.

    Shepherds, fishermen and farmers used these hills for centuries. From the late nineteenth century walkers took them over too: hiking societies such as the Excursionnistes marseillais marked out their own routes, and their members were among the first to fight to protect the massif. In summer the risk of fire is extreme, and access to the hills can be restricted or closed on windy, dry days, so the park and the prefecture publish the day's rules each morning.

    People have also worked the rock itself. Quarries here supplied stone for Roman roads and buildings, while chemical works making soda, lead and sulphur were built along the shore in the early nineteenth century. A committee to defend the Calanques was formed in 1923 to stop industrial development at En-Vau. In spite of the dry ground, the hills hold more than nine hundred plant species, and the rare Bonelli's eagle nests on the cliffs. Visitors number close to three million a year, on land and at sea.

    The calanque of Sugiton is a small cove of pale rock and clear water, one of the closest to Marseille's university campus at Luminy.

    Just offshore sits a rocky islet that locals call the Torpilleur, the torpedo boat, for its shape. The hillsides around you look dry, but they shelter rare plants and animals adapted to one of the windiest, most arid corners of France, and the park protects around two hundred rare species.

    This little cove became a victim of its own success. On summer days Sugiton has seen up to two thousand five hundred visitors, and the constant trampling has made the soil slide down towards the beach, exposing the roots of the pines and stopping young plants from growing. In recent summers the park has limited numbers on the busiest summer days: access is then by a free booking made in advance, a rare step for a French natural site, taken to give the slope time to recover.

    Morgiou and the Cosquer Cave

    Play0:00 / –:––

    Morgiou is a calanque with a tiny fishing harbour and its little cabins, and nearby lies one of the most extraordinary caves in the world.

    The entrance of the Cosquer Cave lies thirty-seven metres under the sea, near the cape of Morgiou. It was found by the diver Henri Cosquer in 1985 and officially declared in 1991. Inside, beyond a long flooded tunnel, its walls carry hand stencils and paintings of horses and bison, made around thirty-three thousand and nineteen thousand years ago, when the sea was far lower. The cave is closed to visitors; a full-size replica can be seen in Marseille.

    The cave is a sanctuary, not a home. People came into its dark halls only to draw, engrave and leave their marks. More than fifty hands have been found, painted in outline around the hand or pressed on in colour, and on one group the fingers appear shortened, a puzzle that has never been solved. Among some hundred and eighty animals, horses and ibex are the most common, alongside bison, aurochs, deer, chamois and a big cat. What makes Cosquer unique is the sea creatures: seals, fish, signs that may be jellyfish, and great auks, flightless seabirds of the cold north , among the rare Ice Age images of these birds.

    Those animals tell of a different world. During the last Ice Age the Mediterranean lay around one hundred and twenty metres lower than it does today, and the shore was several kilometres from the cave's mouth. Hunters crossed a wide plain of grass where herds grazed, and climbed the same limestone heights you see now, where ibex and chamois lived. When the ice melted and the sea rose, the entrance drowned, sealing the paintings in a pocket of air at the end of a tunnel about a hundred and seventy-five metres long.

    The calanque of Sormiou is the largest of the Marseille calanques, with a sandy beach, a small harbour and fishermen's cabins.

    The road down is closed to most cars in summer, to protect the site and limit crowds.

    Sormiou and Morgiou, with Callelongue further west, are the inhabited calanques, reached by a road. Their small harbours began as fishing hamlets. As small-scale fishing declined from the 1950s, the fishermen's huts, the cabanons, became simple holiday homes, passed down through Marseille families, with a boat moored below and a terrace on the rocks. The cabanon, with its long Sunday lunches by the water, is part of the city's identity.

    Sormiou is framed by two rocky ridges, the crête de Sormiou and the crête de Morgiou, and holds about a hundred cabanons; Magdalenian stone tools were found in the rock shelter of the Puits de Sormiou.

    The Calanque of En-Vau

    On the cliff path above the calanque of En-Vau.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The calanque of En-Vau is often called the most beautiful of all, a deep, narrow inlet closed in by tall white cliffs and rock needles.

    Its tiny pebble beach can be reached only by a steep path or from the sea. Climbers come from all over Europe to scale these walls.

    En-Vau owes its survival to a public outcry. Industry valued the limestone of these cliffs. In 1896 the Belgian industrialist Ernest Solvay bought land at Port-Miou, the next calanque towards Cassis, and opened a quarry. The crushed rock was shipped to soda works at Marseille and Salins-de-Giraud, soda being an ingredient of Marseille soap, and over about three quarters of a century some six to seven million tonnes of stone were taken from its sides, giving Port-Miou its present shape. On the thirteenth of March 1910 about two thousand people gathered there to oppose an extension of the quarry, a protest remembered as one of the first environmental demonstrations in France.

    The stone of Cassis is that same Urgonian limestone. Hard and pale, it was prized for paving, for quays and for monuments, and the walls of En-Vau, sharpened by erosion into ridges and needles, are made of it. Climbers began scaling these cliffs in the late nineteenth century.

    After the First World War, industrialists turned to En-Vau and its neighbour Port-Pin. Opposition from the public and the press was fierce, and in 1936 both calanques were protected as listed natural sites. The quarry at Port-Miou was never extended, and it finally closed in the early 1980s.
