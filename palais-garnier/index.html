    The façade of the Palais Garnier faces the Place de l'Opéra, crowned by two gilded groups, Harmony and Poetry.

    Emperor Napoleon III wanted a grand new opera house for Paris. In 1861 a young architect, Charles Garnier, won the design competition against about one hundred and seventy entries. Between the columns stand gilded busts of composers such as Mozart, Beethoven and Rossini. The building opened on the fifth of January 1875, after the fall of the Empire.

    Garnier built for colour and theatre. The façade uses some seventeen different materials: marbles of many colours, porphyry, other stones and gilded bronze, arranged in friezes, columns and statues. At the base, four large sculpted groups flank the arches; the most famous is The Dance by Jean-Baptiste Carpeaux, whose whirling nude figures shocked many Parisians. The building became the model of what is called the Second Empire or Napoleon III style, borrowing freely from the Renaissance. The story goes that Empress Eugénie, whose own favourite had lost the competition, asked Garnier what style it was meant to be, and he answered that it was Napoleon III style.

    Garnier was an outsider in that competition. In the first round his design came only fifth, and he went through as one of seven finalists; the favourites, among them Viollet-le-Duc, supported by the Empress, missed out. Once he had won, Garnier chose the artists himself: fourteen painters, the mosaicists and seventy-three sculptors. The composers' busts are bronze, gilded by electroplating, a then-modern technique. High above, on the gable over the stage, stands Aimé Millet's bronze group of Apollo raising his golden lyre between Poetry and Music, with a winged horse at each end by Eugène-Louis Lequesne. Millet's Apollo, about seven and a half metres tall, also serves as a lightning conductor.
