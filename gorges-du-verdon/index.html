    Lake Sainte-Croix and the Galetas Bridge

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Lake Sainte-Croix spreads out below the Galetas bridge, where the river Verdon leaves its gorge.

    The lake is man-made. A dam was built downstream, and the valley was flooded in the winter of 1973 to 1974, drowning farms, fields and the old village of Les Salles-sur-Verdon, which was rebuilt higher up. The lake covers between about two thousand and three thousand hectares, depending on the source. Its famous turquoise colour comes from fine particles of limestone and minerals held in the water.

    The lake is one of a chain. Between 1929 and 1975 five dams were built on the Verdon, turning stretches of the river into reservoirs for hydro-electric power. Its water marks the border between two départements, the Var to the south and the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence to the north, and the gorge you are about to follow forms the same border for much of its length. Two other villages stand on the shore, Bauduen and Sainte-Croix-du-Verdon. In summer you may see firefighting planes skim low over the water: the lake is one of the places where they scoop up loads to drop on wildfires in the hills.

    Moustiers-Sainte-Marie

    The next story is about Moustiers-Sainte-Marie.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Moustiers-Sainte-Marie clings to the foot of a cliff, and high above it a golden star hangs on a chain stretched between two rock faces. Some sources give its length as one hundred and thirty-five metres, others as more than two hundred.

    Legend says a knight, back from the Crusades, hung the first star here to keep a vow made in captivity, though no one knows for certain. In the version made famous by the Provençal poet Frédéric Mistral, the knight was a member of the Blacas family who promised that if he ever came home he would hang a star over his village. The star you see is not the original; it has been replaced several times over the centuries. The one you see was made in 1957.

    The village is also famous for its fine glazed pottery, or faience. Its golden age began in the late seventeenth century, when the Clérissy family made white-glazed tableware that was sought after across France. Pierre Clérissy is recorded as running a faience works here by around 1679. His workshop used cobalt blue on a white ground. Just below the star, the chapel of Notre-Dame-de-Beauvoir, with its Romanesque porch, looks out over the valley and the Valensole plateau.

    The Point Sublime

    Here the guide turns to The Point Sublime.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Point Sublime looks down into the mouth of the Grand Canyon du Verdon, one of the deepest gorges in Europe.

    Over millions of years the river cut through the limestone of the Provence Prealps, carving a canyon about twenty-five kilometres long. Its cliffs fall between two hundred and fifty and seven hundred metres, while at the bottom the river can be only a few metres wide. At water level the gorge is between six and a hundred metres across. At the top, from one rim to the other, it is between two hundred metres and a kilometre and a half.

    The rock tells a long story. In the Triassic period, this part of Provence sank beneath the sea, and thick layers of limestone settled on the sea floor. In the Jurassic period a warm, shallow sea covered the area and corals flourished, adding more limestone. Much later, when the Alps were pushed up, the Verdon began to trace its course, and as the land rose the river kept cutting down, leaving the walls you see today.

    The first printed descriptions date from 1782 and 1804, and the gorge only began to appear in French guidebooks in the second half of the nineteenth century.

    The gorge was long neglected: it could be reached only by mule tracks, so early travellers saw little more than a cleft and moved on. The explorer Édouard-Alfred Martel came in 1905 and carried out careful geological surveys of the river; he is credited with calling the gorge a canyon. The Touring Club de France later improved the road and, in 1929 and 1930, laid out viewpoints, among them the Point Sublime.

    The Route des Crêtes

    A short story at this point covers The Route des Crêtes.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This railing at the Trescaïre viewpoint is one of the first belvederes of the Route des Crêtes, a loop of about twenty-three kilometres that runs along the right bank of the Grand Canyon.

    About fifteen viewpoints line the road, each looking straight down into the gorge. The road was the wish of local elected officials, among them the mayor of La Palud-sur-Verdon, and it took more than twenty years to build. Work began in 1949. By 1953 the road reached the Chalet de la Maline, the mountain refuge where the famous Blanc-Martel footpath begins. It later climbed to the Dent d'Aire viewpoint, and in 1972 the loop was finally closed. The chief engineer, Gaston Armanet, has a belvedere named after him.

    The central section is narrow, steep and dizzying, so it is one-way and must be driven clockwise, and in winter that stretch is closed because of frost.

    Exploring the Depths

    This stop is given to Exploring the Depths.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The gorge depths below this rim were long a mystery, known only in part to local woodcutters.

    A first crossing of the canyon on foot and by boat came in August 1905, led by the explorer and cave scientist Édouard-Alfred Martel, with the local schoolteacher Isidore Blanc. The team set out on the eleventh of August and included Armand Janet, a fellow explorer. After several days at the bottom of the gorge they finally came out at the Pas de Galetas, near today's lake. Their account made the gorge famous. Martel came back with part of his team the following year.

    One great obstacle is the Imbut, where the river vanishes beneath a chaos of enormous fallen blocks before reappearing further down. Upstream lies another narrow section called the Styx, after the river of the underworld in Greek mythology, a canyon within the canyon. It was Robert de Joly who made the first full crossing of the gorge, the Imbut included, in 1928. That same year the Touring Club de France began laying out a footpath along the bottom, although some sources date its first path to 1906, and in 1930 it was named the Sentier Martel.

    Climbers came last. Today the limestone walls hold more than fifteen hundred climbing routes, and some French counts put it over two thousand five hundred; the gorge is one of the best-known climbing destinations in Europe, as well as a place for kayaking, rafting and fly fishing. Since 1997 the area has been protected as part of the Verdon Regional Natural Park.

    The name Imbut comes from the Provençal word for a funnel.

    The Artuby Bridge

    Play0:00 / –:––

    The Artuby bridge leaps across the canyon of the river Artuby in a single concrete arch, just before it joins the Verdon. The place where the two rivers meet is called La Mescla.

    The deck stands high above the river: some sources say about one hundred and eighty metres, others closer to one hundred and forty, and the bridge has become known for bungee jumping. The southern road along the rim, the Corniche Sublime, offers some of the most dramatic views of the whole canyon.

    The bridge is built of reinforced concrete, was put up between 1938 and 1940 by a Nice firm to the designs of the engineers Pelnard-Considère and Caquot, and is about 142 metres long. It carries the road between Aiguines and Trigance.
