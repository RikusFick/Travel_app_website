    This long granite building is the Sabatini Building, the historic heart of the Museo Reina Sofía, Spain's national museum of twentieth-century art.

    It was built as the General Hospital of Madrid, on the site where King Philip II had gathered the court's scattered hospitals in the sixteenth century. In the eighteenth century those buildings were no longer enough, and a new hospital was designed by José de Hermosilla and then by Francesco Sabatini, the Italian architect of King Charles III, who did most of the work. The plan was enormous, and only about a third of it was ever finished. Sources differ on when it first took in patients, between 1788 and 1805, and it went on treating the sick until it closed in the 1960s.

    Saved from demolition, the building was converted into an arts centre in the 1980s. It was declared a national museum in 1988 and inaugurated as the Museo Reina Sofía, named after Queen Sofía, on 10 September 1992. The three glass-and-steel towers on the façade, added in 1989 by the British architect Ian Ritchie, carry the lifts, so the thick old walls inside did not have to be cut.

    Together with the Prado and the Thyssen-Bornemisza along the Paseo del Prado, the museum forms Madrid's "Golden Triangle of Art". The Prado's story ends around 1900; this is where Spanish art continues, with Picasso, Dalí and Miró.
