    Read along

    The Royal Portal is the group of three carved doorways on the west front, with tall, slender statues of kings and queens from the Bible.

    It dates from the twelfth century and is one of the few parts of the older cathedral to survive a great fire in 1194. The two spires above do not match. The plain south spire, the Old Bell Tower, dates from the 1160s; the lacy north spire, in the Flamboyant Gothic style, was finished in the early sixteenth century after a wooden belfry burned.

    Each doorway shows a different part of Christ's story. Over the right door, the Virgin sits enthroned with the Child on her lap, above scenes of the Nativity, and the arches around her carry the Seven Liberal Arts and the classical authors linked with them, a reminder of the famous cathedral school of Chartres. In the centre, Christ in glory sits in an almond-shaped frame, surrounded by the symbols of the four Evangelists, with the Twelve Apostles below and the twenty-four Elders of the Apocalypse in the arches. Over the left door, Christ stands on a cloud between angels, usually read as the Ascension, framed by the signs of the zodiac and the labours of the months.

    Look along the tops of the columns, just above the statues' heads. The carved capitals form a continuous frieze telling the life of the Virgin and of Christ, some thirty-six small scenes.

    The doors were used differently. The two side doors were the usual way in for most visitors, as they still are, while the central door opened only for processions on great feast days, above all the installation of a new bishop. The statues were once mistaken for early kings of France, which made them targets during the Revolution, but they almost certainly represent the kings and queens of the Old Testament.

    This triple portal was hugely influential, copied at Le Mans, Angers and Bourges, and probably carved by the same sculptors as the portal of Saint-Denis.
