    Al-Hussein Mosque and the square

    In Al-Hussein Square, facing the mosque, with the Khan el-Khalili bazaar behind you and al-Azhar Mosque a short walk to the south-west. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Read along

    This is the mosque of Sayyidna al-Husayn, which means our master Husayn. For many Egyptians it is the mosque closest to their hearts.

    Husayn was a grandson of the Prophet Muhammad. His father was Ali, the Prophet's son-in-law. In the year 680 he died in battle at Karbala, in Iraq.

    Shi'a Muslims honour him as the supreme martyr. Sunni Muslims love him as the Prophet's grandson. Both come here to ask for his intercession.

    Tradition holds that his head rests in this shrine. In 1153 a head said to be his reached Cairo.

    Where to house it was debated. The vizier Tala'i ibn Ruzzik is said to have built a new mosque for it. But the caliph refused to move it. He wanted it kept within the Fatimid palace, and so the shrine rose here, on palace ground.

    Find the old gateway on the mosque's southern corner. It is called Bab al-Akhdar, the Green Gate. Its lower part may be all that is left of the Fatimid shrine.

    The minaret above it is Ayyubid, from 1237. Its stucco panels are carved with fine arabesques.

    The rest of the mosque is much younger. Khedive Isma'il had it rebuilt and enlarged in the nineteenth century. The work is said to have taken about ten years. It was finished around 1873. His builders added the Turkish-style minarets.

    In 1953 the government enlarged the mosque again, to about three thousand three hundred and forty square metres.

    Now look at the tall poles in the square. On Fridays they open into giant umbrellas. They shade the crowds who pray outside when the mosque is full.

    In the twelfth century the traveller Ibn Jubayr watched pilgrims here. He saw them kiss the tomb, crowd around it, and pray with tears.
