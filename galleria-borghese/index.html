    Read along

    You're now looking at the Villa Borghese's gallery building. This elegant building was never meant to be lived in. It was a villa at the edge of Rome, built for parties and for showing off a collection.

    The architect was Flaminio Ponzio, and after his death in 1613 the Flemish architect Giovanni Vasanzio completed it. Set into the portico are ancient Roman relief panels showing battle scenes.

    When it was finished, in the 1610s, the outside looked far busier. The façades were crowded with niches, ancient statues and busts, and some 144 ancient reliefs. Most of them were sold to Napoleon. In the early nineteenth century the architect Luigi Canina redesigned the stripped façades in a plainer neoclassical style, which is largely what you see.

    Its owner was Cardinal Scipione Borghese, nephew of Pope Paul V. With his uncle on the throne, Scipione had money, power and an eye for talent. He collected ancient statues, paintings, and one young sculptor in particular: Gian Lorenzo Bernini.

    The Borghese family kept the villa for almost three hundred years. In 1902 the Italian state bought it, with its park and collection.

    Not everything stayed. In 1807 Prince Camillo Borghese, married to Napoleon's sister, had to sell many ancient statues to Napoleon. They're in the Louvre in Paris.
