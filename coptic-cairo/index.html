  1. 1

    The Roman fortress of Babylon

    On the open ground inside the compound, among the surviving stonework of the Roman fortress. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Before there was a Cairo, there was a fortress on this bank of the Nile. It was called Babylon.

    The name is old. Around 50 AD, the writer Diodorus Siculus told how captives from Babylonia seized a strong position on the river bank. When they were pardoned, they founded a settlement and named it after their homeland.

    Who built the fortress is told in different ways. The American Research Center in Egypt says the emperor Diocletian built it in 302. The Cairo governorate says it was commissioned by the emperor Trajan, in the second century. The Greek Orthodox Patriarchate says three Roman emperors renovated it, Trajan among them.

    The governorate says stone from older Pharaonic temples went into its walls. It also says that, of the original fortress, only the southern gate and its two large towers remain.

    The old Roman streets lie three to five metres below the modern ground. Archaeologists found them during a project to lower the groundwater here, between 2000 and 2006. They also found Roman walls, barracks and harbour works.

    In 641, Arab armies took Egypt. They besieged this fortress for seven months. Then they camped to the east, at a place called al-Fustat.

    The fortress also has an Arabic name, Qasr al-Sham. The governorate translates it as the Wax Palace. The Patriarchate gives the Palace of the Torches.

    Inside the old walls stand churches, a convent, a synagogue and a museum. The American Research Center says these sites show the earliest years of urban Cairo.
