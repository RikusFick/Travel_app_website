    On this rocky hill stood a famous oracle of the ancient world. People crossed hundreds of kilometres of desert to put a question to its god.

    Siwa lies far out in the Western Desert. Estimates put it some five hundred to seven hundred kilometres west of the Nile, depending on the route.

    The god here began as a Libyan deity. The Egyptians saw in him their own great god Amun. They gave him a title: Amun of Siwa, the lord of good counsel. Because the place was so hard to reach, an answer from this god felt rare, and so it felt reliable.

    The first Greeks to come here were from Cyrene, a Greek city on the Libyan coast. They knew the oasis through the caravan trade. They called the god Zeus Ammon. The Greek word ammos means sand. So, to Greek ears, he was something like Sandy Zeus.

    How did the oracle begin? Herodotus heard two stories in the fifth century BC. Priests at Thebes told him that Phoenicians had carried off two priestesses. One was sold in Libya and one in Greece, and each founded an oracle. The priestesses of Dodona, in Greece, told it another way. Two black doves flew from Thebes. One landed at Dodona. The other reached Libya and told the people to found an oracle of Ammon.

    The hill itself is part rock and part dried mud, about thirty metres high. The Greek historian Diodorus describes a fortress at the heart of the oasis, with three rings of walls. The innermost held the palace of the old rulers. The middle ring held the families, the guards, and the sanctuary of the god with its sacred spring. The outer ring held the barracks of the royal guard.
