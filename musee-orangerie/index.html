    The Orangery

    This part of the route looks at The Orangery.

    Free sample: stop 1

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The orangery was built in 1852 to shelter the orange trees of the Tuileries Garden through the winter, with a glass wall on the south side to catch the sun.

    The architect Bourgeois designed it like a greenhouse: the glazed south front faces the Seine, while the north front is blind, to shield the trees from the north winds.

    Before this building existed, the trees spent the winter indoors at the Louvre. Look at the doorways at the east and west ends: they were finished by Bourgeois's successor, Louis Visconti, the architect of the new Louvre. Each door is framed by columns under a carved pediment, and the carvings show horns of plenty, plants and ears of maize, a reminder that this was built as a working garden building.

    After the fall of the Second Empire in 1870 and the burning of the Tuileries Palace the following year, the orangery passed to the State. It still sheltered the trees in winter, but it also hosted concerts, art exhibitions, competitions and even dog shows, until 1922.

    After the First World War its fate changed completely. The prime minister, Georges Clemenceau, a close friend of Claude Monet, proposed installing here the vast series of water lily paintings that Monet had offered to the nation.

    In 1921 the building had been handed to the fine arts administration, together with its twin across the garden, the Jeu de Paume, to show the work of living artists. At first the Water Lilies were meant for a pavilion at the new Musée Rodin. Clemenceau pushed for this orangery instead, and Monet chose it. The orange trees moved out.

    The idea of the gift was older. On the twelfth of November 1918, the day after the armistice, Monet wrote to Clemenceau that he was about to finish two decorative panels he wanted to sign on the day of victory, and asked him to offer them to the State. It was meant as a monument to peace. Clemenceau persuaded him to give the whole decoration instead, and the deed was signed before a notary in April 1922.
