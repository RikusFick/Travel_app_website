Travel tips

What to do in Istanbul

This page is in English.

Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque and the Hippodrome, the Basilica Cistern, Topkapı Palace, the Grand Bazaar, the Süleymaniye, Galata Tower and the Bosphorus.

These are the places the Earwise guides cover in Istanbul: Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque, the Basilica Cistern and Topkapı Palace in the old city, the Grand Bazaar and the Süleymaniye, Galata across the Golden Horn, and a boat trip on the Bosphorus.

Hagia Sophia

Hagia Sophia, Greek for “Holy Wisdom”, was built as a Christian church for the Byzantine emperor Justinian and finished in the year 537. It has been a cathedral, a mosque, a museum, and since 2020 a mosque again. The dome rises about fifty-five and a half metres above the floor and is about thirty-one metres across. Many of the mosaics were hidden under plaster in the Ottoman period, and that plaster helped to protect them; they were uncovered from the 1930s onwards. The Earwise guide follows the visitor route up to the upper galleries and around the mosaics. How access is arranged can change, so check before you go.

The Hippodrome and the Blue Mosque

Sultanahmet Square was once the Hippodrome of Constantinople, the great chariot-racing stadium of the Byzantine capital. Its pink granite Egyptian obelisk was made for Pharaoh Thutmose the Third in the fifteenth century BC and first stood at the temple of Karnak. Beside the square rise the six minarets of the Sultan Ahmed Mosque, built between 1609 and 1617 for Sultan Ahmed the First. Inside, the lower walls are covered in more than twenty thousand İznik tiles, in over fifty different designs, and the blue of the tiles and paintwork gave the Blue Mosque its nickname. This is a place of worship: shoes come off, and visitors dress modestly.

The Basilica Cistern

Fifty-two stone steps lead down into a cool, dim hall built in the sixth century under Justinian to store water for the Great Palace of the emperors. Three hundred and thirty-six columns, in twelve rows of twenty-eight, each about nine metres tall, hold up its brick vaults. In the far corner, two huge marble Medusa heads serve as the bases of two columns, one upside down and one on its side; no record tells us why.

Topkapı Palace

For nearly four hundred years, the Ottoman sultans ruled from behind the Imperial Gate. Mehmed the Second began the palace around 1460, a few years after he conquered the city, on the headland where the Bosphorus meets the Sea of Marmara and the Golden Horn. The guide walks through the four courtyards, past the palace kitchens, the Imperial Treasury and the Chamber of the Sacred Relics, the most sacred part of the palace, with the Harem as an optional section.

The Grand Bazaar and the Süleymaniye

The Grand Bazaar, the Kapalıçarşı, has more than sixty streets under one roof. It grew up around a single domed market hall that Mehmed the Second ordered a few years after he conquered the city in 1453; its rents went to support Hagia Sophia. The guide walks it east to west, from the Nuruosmaniye Gate to the Beyazıt Gate, with an optional stop at the Spice Bazaar. Uphill, the Süleymaniye Mosque was built for Sultan Süleyman the Lawgiver by his chief architect, Sinan. Its four minarets carry ten balconies, which tradition links to Süleyman as the tenth Ottoman sultan, and the guide’s loop around the complex passes the tombs of Süleyman and Hürrem and Sinan’s own tomb.

Galata Tower

Across the Golden Horn, the Galata Tower was built by Italians: merchants from the Republic of Genoa, who had their own colony here. The tower dates from 1348 and stood at the highest point of the colony’s walls. Its balcony, just over fifty-one metres up, gives a full 360-degree view of Istanbul.

On the water: the Bosphorus

The Bosphorus divides Europe from Asia. It is about thirty kilometres long and links the Black Sea with the Sea of Marmara. On the surface the water flows south, while deep below a saltier current flows north. The Earwise guide is written for a northbound boat trip from Eminönü along the European shore to the Fatih Sultan Mehmet Bridge, then back along the Asian shore, and each stop works on its own, in whatever order the boat passes.

We do not give current opening hours or ticket prices here, because those change; check before you go.
