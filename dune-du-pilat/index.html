    The forest edge is where the pine woods of the Landes meet a wall of sand, the land side of the highest dune in Europe.

    The dune lies at the southern entrance to Arcachon Bay, about sixty kilometres south-west of Bordeaux. It belongs in large part to public bodies such as the Conservatoire du littoral, which protects it as a natural site that anyone can visit on foot.

    The pines around you are not all alike. The plantations behind the dune, and the state forest further south, were sown in the nineteenth century to pin down the sand blown inland by the wind. But the old forest that runs along the dune's eastern foot, the forêt usagère of La Teste-de-Buch, is much older: a mixed wood of maritime pines and oaks, grown on a relief of ancient dunes and sheltered from the west wind by the great dune itself.

    In July 2022 this forest went through fire. A blaze that broke out on the twelfth of July in a heatwave swept through the forêt usagère, and the five campsites at the foot of the dune were destroyed, their visitors evacuated in haste. The burnt trunks you may see are the marks of that summer.

    Its name means the forest of usage. For about six centuries the inhabitants of the old Captalat de Buch have held a right to take dead wood for heating and, under conditions, living timber to build their homes, while the landowners alone may harvest the resin. Because the trees serve the community, the parcels cannot be fenced, and the forest has never been worked for timber production; trees are left to grow old and renew themselves naturally.

    The landscape is protected by law. On the fifth of June 1943 part of the dune and of this forest became a listed site, and in 1994, under pressure from crowds and building, the listing was widened to take in a much larger area. Close to two million people a year climb the dune, which makes it one of the most visited natural sites in France.

    The buried forest is the line of pines that the dune is slowly swallowing on its land side.

    The wind blows sand up from the beach and over the crest, so the dune creeps inland by a few metres a year. Trees, roads and even old buildings at its foot have disappeared under the sand.

    The sand you are climbing has buried a forest before. From around the sixteenth century, strong winds built a new generation of coastal dunes that covered older dunes, fields and even villages. The great dune that formed here was known as the dune de la Grave.

    This land-side slope is the steep one. The wind pushes grains up the gentle ocean face, and at the crest they tumble over and settle on the sheltered side, which is why the face you are on is so hard to climb and why the dune keeps rolling inland like a slow wave.

    To stop the sands of the Landes from advancing, the French state launched a vast campaign of sowing maritime pines along the coast, under a decree of 1801 and the guidance of the engineer Nicolas Brémontier. The dune de la Grave was sown with pines during the nineteenth century and became a cultivated forest, tapped for resin. Then the sea took its revenge. As the coastline retreated, around 1860 the vegetation on the dune's seaward side disappeared, the sand began to move again, and a new dune rose on top of the old one: the Dune du Pilat. What you see swallowing the pines in front of you is the continuation of that movement.

    The summit of the dune rises a little over one hundred metres above the sea, and its height changes from year to year with the wind and storms.

    The dune is about two point seven to two point nine kilometres long, depending on the source, and around five hundred metres wide, holding some fifty-five to sixty million cubic metres of sand. To the east the forest stretches to the horizon; to the north lie the entrance to Arcachon Bay and the tip of Cap Ferret.

    Two big forces meet at this viewpoint. Out at sea, a current runs southward along the Aquitaine coast, carrying sand with it. The mouth of Arcachon Bay interrupts that current: every tide, the bay fills and empties, and the sand stops and piles up at its entrance in banks such as the Banc d'Arguin, just offshore. At low tide the bank dries out, and the strong west winds lift its grains and carry them onto the coast in front of it. The dune is, in effect, the Banc d'Arguin's sand blown ashore and heaped up.

    The name Pilat comes from the Gascon word for a heap or pile of sand.

    Old maps show places called Pilat to the south and out to sea, because the dune has moved over the centuries. Until the 1930s this sand hill was known as Les Sabloneys, and it took the name Dune du Pilat after a property developer named his new resort here. The dune also hides traces of the past: Iron Age pottery and old layers of soil have been found in its sand.

    People were here long before the dune. Archaeologists have found traces of short-lived camps from late prehistory linked to making salt from seawater. On the last day of 2013 a walker found at the foot of the dune a funerary urn and a small vase from the Iron Age, about eight hundred years before Christ, and in 2014 a team of volunteer archaeologists spent two weeks excavating the spot.

    The resort below the dune's northern end has its own story. Around 1913 the developer Daniel Meller obtained land from the state between Le Moulleau and the dune and named his new resort Pyla-sur-Mer, after the dune, with the aim of building a town in the forest. Pyla is the resort; Pilat, with a t, is the official name of the dune.

    The ocean side is the long slope down to the Atlantic, where waves and tides wear away the foot of the dune.

    Look along the face below the crest. The dark, wavy lines running through the sand are not oil but old soils, called palaeosols, the surfaces of earlier dunes and forests that the sand later buried. Scientists count four main levels. The lowest is the old soil of the Landes plateau, with stumps of a prehistoric pine forest; another holds a settlement of the early Iron Age; and the highest is the forest of Brémontier's nineteenth-century plantations, where fragments of the clay pots used to collect resin have been found. Dated by carbon-14 and by the objects they contain, these bands make the dune an archive of four thousand years of climate on this coast: cold, dry and windy periods when the sand moved, alternating with warmer, wetter ones when forest took hold.

    Offshore lies the Banc d'Arguin, a nature reserve of shifting sandbanks where seabirds nest. On the beach below, concrete bunkers of the German Atlantic Wall from the Second World War have tumbled down the slope as the sand moved.

    Those bunkers are a measure of how fast this coast changes: as the shoreline retreated and the dune moved inland, the concrete was left behind, and some blocks now lie on the sand or under the waves, all within a single lifetime.

    The same steady sea wind that builds the dune makes it a favourite place for paragliders, who launch from the crest and soar along the ocean face.
