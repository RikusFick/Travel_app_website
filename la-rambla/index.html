Follow Barcelona's best-known boulevard from its upper fountain down to the harbour. Originally a seasonal stream outside the medieval walls, La Rambla now links Baroque churches, La Boqueria market, the Liceu opera house, and early Gaudí architecture.

  1. 1

    Font de Canaletes

    Begin at the lamppost fountain near Plaça de Catalunya, traditional gathering point for Barça celebrations.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Font de Canaletes, the ornate fountain crowned with a lamp, marks the top of La Rambla, Barcelona's most famous street.

    La Rambla runs for about 1.2 kilometres from here down to the sea. Its name comes from the Arabic word ramla, meaning sand: this was once a sandy stream bed outside the city walls, dry most of the year and a torrent after heavy rain. In the fifteenth century the stream was diverted, and slowly the bed became a street. It is really five streets in a row, each with its own name, which is why locals also say "les Rambles".

    This fountain dates from 1892. Its name recalls the water channels, the canaletes, that once ran through the old city wall here.

    It is said that anyone who drinks from the Font de Canaletes will come back to Barcelona. And when FC Barcelona wins a big trophy, this is where fans gather to celebrate.

  2. 2

    Església de Betlem

    Pause outside the austere stone façade of the seventeenth-century Jesuit church on Rambla dels Estudis.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Església de Betlem, the church of Bethlehem, is a Baroque church built for Barcelona's Jesuits.

    It belonged to a Jesuit college founded here in the sixteenth century. That college gave this stretch its name, the Rambla dels Estudis, the "Rambla of Studies". The first church burned down in 1671. The church you see was begun in 1680 and blessed in 1729.

    Inside, the plan is broad and simple: a single wide nave, with side chapels set between the buttresses, a layout that follows the old Catalan Gothic tradition rather than Roman models. The domes over the chapels, with their small lanterns, can be seen from outside.

    When the Jesuits were expelled from Spain in 1767, the church closed and later became a parish church.

  3. 3

    Palau de la Virreina

    Hear the story of the eighteenth-century palace built for a former viceroy of Peru and his young widow.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Palau de la Virreina, the "Vicereine's Palace", is one of the finest eighteenth-century houses in Barcelona.

    It was built between 1772 and 1778 for Manuel d'Amat i de Junyent, a Catalan who had made his fortune as Viceroy of Peru. He sent the plans from Lima. Amat died in 1782, only a few years after moving in, and his widow lived on here, which is how the palace got its name.

    The style sits between Baroque and Rococo. The sculptor Carles Grau worked on the stone decoration, including the window surrounds and the vases along the top.

    Today the palace belongs to the city and is used for exhibitions and cultural events.

  4. 4

    La Boqueria

    Enter beneath the iron-and-glass arch of Barcelona's oldest and liveliest fresh-food market.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    La Boqueria, officially the Mercat de Sant Josep, is Barcelona's most famous food market.

    Farmers sold their produce here, outside the old city gate, for centuries before any market hall existed. The site itself was a Carmelite convent dedicated to Saint Joseph, which is where the official name comes from. In 1835 the convent was burned in riots against the religious orders, and the city decided to give the stalls a permanent home on the empty site.

    Records of that trade go back to the thirteenth century, when tables near the city gate sold meat. Selling outside the walls spared traders the tax charged on goods entering the city.

    Work on the market began on Saint Joseph's Day in 1840, to a design by the city architect Josep Mas i Vila. It started as an open Neoclassical square ringed by a portico of Ionic columns, so traders had shade at the edges while the middle stayed open to the sky. Iron changed that. Like other Barcelona markets of the time, the Boqueria was covered with a frame of iron and glass, light enough to span the whole square on slender columns while letting daylight and air through. Earlier projects had been drawn up by the engineer Miquel de Bergué, but the roof that was built went up during the 1910s under the architect Antoni de Falguera and the engineer Joan Torras; the metal roof dates from 1914.

    Inside are more than 300 stalls under one roof, selling fish, meat, fruit and vegetables, making it one of the largest markets in Catalonia.

    The name Boqueria is thought to come from boc, the Catalan word for a goat: a place where goat meat was sold.

  5. 5

    The Miró pavement

    Spot Joan Miró's colourful round pavement mosaic set directly into the pedestrian walkway.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Miró pavement, a large round mosaic in bold colours, is set into the ground of the Rambla here at the Pla de la Boqueria.

    It was designed by Joan Miró, one of Barcelona's greatest artists. The city asked him for a work to greet visitors arriving by air. Miró offered three: one for those arriving by air, a mural at the airport; one for those arriving by road, a sculpture; and this one for those arriving by sea, walking up from the port. The pavement was unveiled at the end of 1976.

    Around it runs the Rambla's wavy paving, a pattern that recalls the water that once flowed down this stream bed.

  6. 6

    Gran Teatre del Liceu

    Look up at the Gran Teatre del Liceu and learn how the opera house twice rose from devastating fires.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Gran Teatre del Liceu, Barcelona's opera house, opened on this spot on 4 April 1847.

    Unlike most great opera houses in Europe, it was not built by a king. Barcelona's citizens paid for it themselves, buying boxes and seats in advance, and when it opened it was one of the largest opera houses in Europe by capacity, with seats for more than 2,000 people.

    Its history has been dramatic. Fire destroyed the auditorium in 1861, and it was rebuilt within a year. On 7 November 1893, on the opening night of the season, an anarchist threw two bombs into the stalls, killing about twenty people. And in 1994 a fire gutted the hall again. It was rebuilt as before and opened once more in October 1999.

    The façade on the Rambla survived all of this; it is still the original of 1847.

  7. 7

    Plaça Reial

    Step just off the avenue into a palm-shaded square with early iron streetlamps designed by Antoni Gaudí.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Plaça Reial, the "Royal Square", is a closed square of arcades and tall palm trees, just off the Rambla.

    A Capuchin convent stood here until the riots of 1835, when the religious houses of the Rambla were attacked. The city then held a competition for a new square, won by the architect Francesc Daniel Molina. His design is uniform and elegant, with matching arcaded buildings closing the square on every side.

    The ornate lamp posts were designed by Antoni Gaudí, long before his fame came with the Sagrada Família.

  8. 8

    Palau Güell

    Admire the dark stone and wrought-iron entrance of the townhouse Gaudí built for patron Eusebi Güell.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Palau Güell is the town house Antoni Gaudí designed for the industrialist Eusebi Güell, his greatest patron, in the late 1880s.

    Güell's father had made a fortune in Cuba, and the family built it up with textile mills. He wanted a palace for receiving high society, and he gave the young Gaudí a free hand. The result is a stone façade, almost severe, pierced by two gates shaped as parabolic arches, filled with forged ironwork. Through those gates, horse-drawn carriages drove straight into the house, and the horses went down a ramp to the stables in the basement.

    Since 1984 the palace has been part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Works of Antoni Gaudí".

  9. 9

    The Columbus Monument

    End at the harbour column marking the seafront terminus of Barcelona's grandest promenade.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Columbus Monument rises about 60 metres above the harbour at the foot of the Rambla.

    It was built for the Universal Exhibition of 1888, and opened on 1 June that year, in memory of Columbus reporting back to Ferdinand and Isabella in Barcelona after his first voyage across the Atlantic. The architect was Gaietà Buïgas.

    At the top, a bronze statue of Columbus, about 7 metres tall, by Rafael Atché, stretches out one arm. It is often said he points to the Americas; in fact he points out to sea, roughly towards North Africa. Below him, a Corinthian column stands on a base decorated with lions, figures of the kingdoms of Spain and bronze reliefs of the voyage.

    A small lift inside the column carries visitors up to a viewing platform just below the statue.
