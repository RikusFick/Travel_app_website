    The walls and towers of the Château d'Angers make one of the mightiest fortresses of medieval France.

    There are seventeen round towers, striped with dark schist and pale limestone. The site on its rocky promontory above the river Maine has been occupied since prehistoric times, and in 851, to resist the Vikings, King Charles the Bald installed a count here. The counts of Anjou founded the Plantagenet dynasty, who became kings of England in the twelfth century. When the French king took Anjou back in 1206, it became a frontier, and in the 1230s the regent Blanche of Castile, mother of the future Saint Louis, had this colossal fortress built, razing a whole district of the city. The towers were once much taller.

    Even cut down, each tower still stands around thirty metres high and measures some eleven to thirteen metres across.

    Look at what the walls stand on. The fortress sits on a promontory of slaty schist, the same dark stone used in its stripes, and in the Middle Ages quarrying made the drop to the river even steeper. Before this castle, Count Fulk III, famous for building dozens of castles, had already fortified the site. Blanche of Castile's builders worked fast, from about 1230 to 1242, because she needed an outpost against rebellious barons in the west. Later the moats were dug out properly in 1485 and widened again. In 1585, in the Wars of Religion, Catholics and Protestants fought over the castle, and King Henry III ordered it razed so neither side could use it against him; only the tops of the towers were taken down.

    The fortress covers about two and a half hectares. At the end of the eighteenth century its garrison held out when the thick walls withstood heavy cannon fire from the Vendée army.
