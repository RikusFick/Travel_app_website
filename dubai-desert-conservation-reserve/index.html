    A reserve in the desert

    Read along

    Look out across the sand. What you see is protected land: the Dubai Desert Conservation Reserve, about 225 square kilometres of dunes and gravel plains in the south-east of the emirate of Dubai. That is close to five percent of the whole emirate.

    It began with a hotel. In 1999 the Al Maha desert resort opened inside a fenced area of 27 square kilometres, with a mission to give guests the real desert, with its native plants and animals. In its first year the team released Arabian oryx and planted some 6,000 native trees and shrubs. The land had been heavily grazed by camels and goats, and once the grazing stopped, surveys found that both the number and the variety of plants rose.

    Success led to something bigger. In 2002 the resort's managers carried out an environmental audit of the wider area: the threats to endangered species, the loss of desert habitat, and unregulated off-road driving. They proposed a formal national park. A board was formed under the chairmanship of Sheikh Ahmed bin Saeed Al Maktoum, who also chairs the Emirates airline. The Ruler of Dubai created the reserve by decree, and by 2003 it covered its full 225 square kilometres. At that time, by the reserve's own account, 17 tour operators were driving freely over these dunes; the number was cut to a few approved companies, who must keep to set routes. In 2004, 41 Arabian oryx were moved from the original Al Maha area into the north of the new reserve. All domestic livestock was removed in 2008, and in 2011 the old fence around the first 27 square kilometres came down, so the animals could range over the whole reserve.

    The reserve is divided into zones. There is a wilderness zone, a zone for rehabilitating plants and wildlife, an ecotourism zone for low-impact activities such as nature walks and camel rides, and a zone for development and recreation, where the approved operators run their drives and camps. In the wilderness zone, no one goes in at all except researchers, and then only on foot. The approved operators share a handful of camps, used mainly for evening meals and limited overnight stays. Keeping vehicles to set areas means the animals always have quiet ground to retreat to.

    The results show in the counts. The reserve recorded about 60 bird species in 2003; it now lists around 140, along with 74 plants, 18 mammals, 26 reptiles and some 300 kinds of insects, spiders and other arthropods. Since the founding years, rangers have also counted things they did not plant: acacia trees and calligonum shrubs, seeding themselves across the sand.
