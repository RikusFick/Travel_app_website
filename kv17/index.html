    Belzoni's discovery

    At the top of the entrance stairs, in front of the tomb's doorway in the eastern branch of the valley. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are standing at the doorway of the tomb of Seti the First, a king of Egypt's Nineteenth Dynasty. Scholars usually date his reign to roughly 1294 to 1279 BC, though some place it a decade or so earlier. His son was Ramesses the Great. Among the Valley of the Kings' monuments, KV17 stands out for its vast scale and near-total painted relief work.

    For more than three thousand years almost nobody walked into it. Then, in October 1817, an Italian explorer named Giovanni Belzoni began to dig here. Belzoni had been a circus strongman and a hydraulic engineer before he turned to antiquities. He was collecting pieces for the British consul-general, Henry Salt. He quickly found the entrance to what he called a magnificent tomb, and three days after he started, on the nineteenth, he reached the king's sarcophagus.

    Over the past two centuries the tomb picked up three names: KV17 in modern numbering, Belzoni's Tomb, and , from an unexpected find , the Tomb of Apis. Belzoni wrote that the paintings seemed more perfect the further in he went, and that they kept a gloss, like a varnish, over the colours.

    Look at the stairs and the walls around the entrance. In the year after Belzoni's dig, rain water flooded the lower chambers, because the protective dykes he had begun outside the entrance were not finished. James Burton completed them in 1825, and the tomb has not flooded since. In 2001, work began to alter the modern walls around the entrance to reduce the risk further. Water, as much as people, has been this tomb's enemy.
