    Praça do Comércio

    In the middle of the Praça do Comércio, near the bronze statue of the king on horseback, with the river behind you.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Praça do Comércio opens wide to the river, a vast square framed on three sides by yellow arcaded buildings. Many people in Lisbon still call it by its older name, the Terreiro do Paço, the Palace Yard.

    The palace that gave it that name is gone. In the early 1500s King Manuel the First moved his court down from the castle to a new riverside palace here, the Ribeira Palace. Here, beside the docks where the ships of the empire came in, the royal court sat until the earthquake.

    On the first of November 1755 a great earthquake struck Lisbon, followed by a tsunami and fire. The palace was destroyed. The king's chief minister, Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, later the Marquis of Pombal, led the rebuilding. A plan drawn up by the engineer Manuel da Maia and the architects Eugénio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel gave the square its U shape, open towards the river, and its new name, the Square of Commerce, for a city that would live by trade.

    In the centre stands King José the First on horseback, the king who reigned at the time of the earthquake, modelled by the sculptor Machado de Castro, cast in bronze and unveiled in 1775. On the river side, the marble steps of the Cais das Colunas were planned as a grand entrance to the city from the water.

    The square has seen darker days too. On the first of February 1908, King Carlos was shot dead in his carriage as it crossed this square, and his heir, Prince Luís Filipe, died of his wounds soon afterwards. Two years later Portugal became a republic.

    The Rua Augusta Arch

    On the north side of the square, facing the great triumphal arch.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Rua Augusta Arch fills the north side of the square, a triumphal gateway of pale stone leading from the river into the heart of the rebuilt city.

    A triumphal arch was part of the plan for rebuilding Lisbon after the great earthquake of 1755, to celebrate the city rising again. But it was not finished until the 1870s, more than a century later.

    At the very top, a figure of Glory holds out crowns to two others, Valour and Genius, the work of the French sculptor Célestin Anatole Calmels. Below them stand four great figures from Portugal's past: Viriatus, a leader of resistance against Rome; Nuno Álvares Pereira, the medieval commander; Vasco da Gama, who reached India by sea; and the Marquis of Pombal himself, who rebuilt the city. Lying at either side are two figures for Portugal's great rivers, the Tagus and the Douro.

    The Latin inscription reads "Virtutibus maiorum ut sit omnibus documento": to the virtues of the ancestors, as a lesson for all. Through the arch runs the Rua Augusta, the main street of the new Lisbon.

    Rua Augusta

    Halfway along the Rua Augusta, the pedestrian street between the arch and the Rossio.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Rua Augusta runs straight as a ruler through the Baixa, the low town, between rows of buildings of almost exactly the same height and design.

    That sameness is the point. Before 1755 this was a maze of medieval lanes. After the earthquake and the fire, the planners working for Pombal, the king's chief minister, swept it away and laid out a grid of straight streets, lined with buildings of one uniform design, so the city could be rebuilt in an orderly way.

    They were also built to stand up to the next earthquake. Inside the walls of these buildings is a hidden frame of wood, a lattice of crossed timbers known as the Pombaline cage, designed to spread the force of the shaking through the whole building. The Baixa is often described as one of the first districts in the world built with earthquakes in mind.

    The frame is thought to owe something to shipbuilding: like a ship's hull, timber can bend under strain without breaking, while the masonry around it resists fire. The carpenters raised the timber frame first, and the masons then filled the walls around it. Accounts tell that a model of the frame was tested by soldiers marching around it to imitate a quake, though how far this was a true test, and how far a demonstration, is still debated.

    The Rossio

    In the Rossio square, near the tall column in the centre, facing the theatre at the north end.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Rossio has been one of Lisbon's main squares since the Middle Ages, and its wave-patterned pavement is one of its trademarks.

    Its official name is the Praça de D. Pedro IV, after the king whose statue stands on top of the tall column in the centre, put up in the 1870s. But the people of Lisbon never took to the new name, and it is still the Rossio.

    The Rossio was the stage for festivals and bullfights, revolts and public executions. At the north end, where the theatre stands now, there was once the Estaus Palace, built in the 1400s to lodge visiting nobles and ambassadors. In 1571 it became the seat of the Inquisition, the Church court that tried people for heresy.

    The old palace burned down in 1836, and in its place rose the National Theatre of Dona Maria the Second, which opened in the 1840s. Around the middle of the 1800s the square was paved with black and white stone in rolling waves, a pattern that later spread all over Lisbon.

    The pavement is laid by hand by specialist pavers, the calceteiros, who set small pieces of white limestone and dark stone side by side to form the pattern.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Santa Justa Lift rises forty-five metres above the street, a tower of iron lace that carries passengers straight up from the Baixa to the higher ground of the Carmo.

    Lisbon is a city of steep hills, and getting from the low town to the upper districts has always been hard work. The engineer Mesnier du Ponsard, who also built funiculars and lifts elsewhere in Portugal, designed this one.

    The tower is made of iron, still a modern material at the time, and dressed in neo-Gothic arches and tracery. Inside, two wooden cabins with mirrors and windows ride up and down. At the top, an iron walkway crosses over the rooftops to the Largo do Carmo. The lift opened in 1902.

    The Carmo Convent

    In the Largo do Carmo, facing the ruined Gothic church of the Carmo Convent.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Carmo Convent stands above the square with a church that has had no roof since the earthquake of 1755, its tall arches open to the sky.

    It was founded in 1389 by Nuno Álvares Pereira, the Constable of Portugal, supreme commander of the king's armies. In his later years he gave up his wealth and, in 1423, entered this convent himself as a Carmelite brother.

    The church was a grand Gothic building, and it was said to rival Lisbon's cathedral. The earthquake of 1755 badly damaged it, and the fire that followed destroyed almost everything inside. A rebuilding in a new Gothic style began the year after, but it was stopped in 1834, and in the middle of the 1800s it was decided to leave the nave open to the sky. Today the ruin stands as a memorial to the earthquake.

    The ruins also house the Carmo Archaeological Museum, founded in 1864. Among the items currently displayed there is the tomb of King Fernando the First, a medieval king of Portugal.

    Café A Brasileira

    On the Rua Garrett in the Chiado, outside the Café A Brasileira, beside the bronze statue of a man seated at a table.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Café A Brasileira has stood on this street, the Rua Garrett, since November 1905.

    It was opened by Adriano Telles, a Portuguese businessman who had worked in Brazil, to sell Brazilian coffee to Lisbon. It is linked with the "bica", the small, strong cup of coffee that Lisbon still drinks today. At first the shop sold coffee to take home; in 1908 it was remodelled to open a café where coffee could be drunk on the spot.

    In the early twentieth century the café became a meeting place for writers and artists. Among them was Fernando Pessoa, Portugal's great modern poet. Pessoa and his friends, among them the writer Mário de Sá-Carneiro and the artist Almada Negreiros, were the group behind Orpheu, a literary magazine of 1915. It ran to only two issues, but it brought the modernist movement into Portuguese literature.

    The bronze figure at the table outside is Pessoa, made by the sculptor Lagoa Henriques and placed here in 1988, a hundred years after the poet's birth. The figure is seated at one of the tables of the café terrace.
