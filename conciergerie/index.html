    The Clock Tower on the corner of the old royal palace carries the oldest public clock in Paris.

    King Jean le Bon built the square tower in the mid-fourteenth century as a lookout for the palace. Around 1370, under his son Charles V, it received the first public clock in the city, made by Henri de Vic, a clockmaker from Lorraine. In 1418 the townspeople asked for an outside dial, so that they could regulate their business by day and by night.

    The dial you see dates from 1585, under King Henri III. Its blue background is scattered with gold fleurs-de-lis, and the sculptor Germain Pilon framed it with two figures standing for the Law and Justice. Damaged during the Revolution, they were restored in the nineteenth century, and the dates of the repairs are carved below the clock.

    Further along the river front stand the palace's other towers. The crenellated one at the far end is the oldest, called Bonbec.

    The palace has burned again and again: the Great Hall in 1618, the Chamber of Accounts in 1737, and much of the palace in January 1776. An older tower, lost after that last fire, had held some of the most notorious prisoners of the old monarchy, including Ravaillac, the assassin of King Henri the Fourth.

    The building's name goes back to the same reign. When Charles the Fifth moved the royal household to the Louvre and the Hôtel Saint-Pol, the old palace was left in the care of its concierge, the royal steward, and a conciergerie was simply the concierge's lodging. The concierge was given wide powers over the lesser courts, and cells were gradually added in the lower parts of the building, until the name came to mean the prison itself. Above, the Parlement of Paris, the kingdom's highest court, went on sitting in the palace for centuries.
