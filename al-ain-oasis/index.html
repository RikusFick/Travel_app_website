  1. 1

    The West Gate

    On the plaza in front of the West Gate. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Welcome to Al Ain Oasis, the largest of the old oases of Al Ain. The name of the city means "the spring", and the whole city grew up around gardens like this one, watered from the mountains you can see to the east.

    Look at the gate in front of you. Its thick, earth-coloured walls look old, but the gate is modern: a concrete structure finished in clay, built to echo the gates that once led into the oasis. Low, glass-walled pavilions stand on either side of it. One holds the ticket office, the other a permanent exhibition about the oasis and its water. The glass is there so that, even before you enter, you look straight into the palm grove.

    For most of its history, this was not a park but a private farming landscape, a maze of walled family gardens linked by narrow lanes and water channels. Many plots are still working farms, owned and tended by Al Ain families. The public paths, benches and gates were added when the oasis was opened to visitors as a heritage site in 2016, with shaded paths winding through the palms.

    In 2011, this oasis became part of the first World Heritage Site in the United Arab Emirates: the Cultural Sites of Al Ain. UNESCO listed it together with the Bronze Age tombs of Jebel Hafit, the ancient settlement at Hili, the site of Bidaa Bint Saud and the other oases of the city. Together they show people settling and farming in this desert since the Neolithic period.

    Visitor information gives the oasis an area of about 1,200 hectares and more than 147,000 date palms. A water survey of Al Ain gives a much smaller figure for the oasis's irrigated gardens, so the larger number may describe the whole protected oasis area rather than the farmed plots alone.

    The exhibition beside the gate traces the falaj, the water system you will walk beside. Visitor material calls it a system that has worked here for thousands of years. It is worth a look before you set off, because without that water, nothing around you would grow.

    The Eco-Centre and the Miniature Oasis

    Outside the Eco-Centre, near the scale model of the oasis. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The low building here is the Eco-Centre, built as an environmentally friendly visitor centre. Abu Dhabi's culture department describes it as a time machine: its interactive screens take you from today back through the oasis's past, and show how the delicate oasis ecosystem is being protected.

    Nearby is the Miniature Oasis, a scale model of Al Ain Oasis. It is the best place to understand how and why the oasis was laid out as it is. Follow the water on the model. It enters along the main channel, then splits again and again into smaller channels that reach every walled garden. Around the edge, the model shows the forts that were built to protect the oasis.

    Those forts tell you what the oasis meant. In a land with very little farmland and even less reliable water, a working oasis was the most valuable thing a community could hold. It fed families, it fed their animals, and it produced dates that could be stored and traded. Whoever controlled the water and the palms controlled Al Ain, and so towers and fortified houses grew up around the gardens. Several forts and towers still stand around the edges of the oasis today.

    Al Ain sits at the foot of the Hajar Mountains, near the border with Oman. Rain that falls on the mountains soaks into the gravel and flows slowly underground towards the plain. Studies of old wells here show that the climate has grown drier over the last four and a half thousand years, and that water tables fell slowly over that time. By the end of the Bronze Age, farming that relied on rain alone could no longer be sustained. The answer, from the Iron Age on, was to go underground and bring the water in by hand-dug channels.

  3. 3

    The falaj

    Beside an open stretch of falaj channel. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Look down at the channel beside the path. This is a falaj, the irrigation system that has kept this oasis alive. The plural is aflaj.

    There are three kinds of falaj in this region. Some start at a natural spring. Some divert water from the bed of a wadi, a seasonal river. The aflaj of Al Ain are mostly the third kind, called Dawoodi: underground channels fed by a mother well.

    Here is how a Dawoodi falaj was built. Diggers first sank a well, by hand, down to the water table, often several kilometres away towards the mountains. From there they dug a tunnel back towards the fields, with a slope so gentle that the water would flow by gravity alone, never fast enough to wash the tunnel away. Along the route they sank vertical shafts from the surface, each called a thaqba. The shafts let workers climb down, haul the loose earth up in buckets on ropes, let in air and light, and later come back to clean and repair the tunnel. From the air, a line of these shafts looks like a row of small craters across the gravel plain. Archaeologists working under the modern city have recorded falaj tunnels as deep as fifteen metres below today's streets.

    Visitor information names two main aflaj for this oasis, Al Aini and Al Dawoodi. A survey of Al Ain's aflaj describes the Dawoodi as about seven kilometres long, from mother wells to the east of the city, and the Aini, also called Sarooj, as about fifteen kilometres long. Its mother well is about twenty metres deep.

    The oldest known aflaj in the Al Ain area, at Hili and nearby, are around three thousand years old. Those Iron Age channels have long since silted up. The ones that still run are mostly from the Islamic period, kept going for centuries by regular cleaning. Since the 1970s and 80s, falling groundwater has dried the old mother wells, and today the Dawoodi is topped up by pumped wells and desalinated water. Some reports estimate that about a third of its flow is still natural groundwater.

    So the water may come and go with the seasons. If the channel is dry when you visit, look at its shape and lining instead: it was built to carry water at exactly the right pace, and to be cleaned by hand.

    Sharing the water

    At a point where channels divide. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Notice how the channel splits. At junctions like this, water could be turned one way or the other by blocking one branch, traditionally with stones, earth or a board, and opening another.

    A falaj never stops flowing, so its water has to be shared all day and all night. In the aflaj of this region, water was not divided by volume but by time. Each owner held the right to the whole flow for a set period, and the turns went round in a fixed cycle that could last several days.

    Studies of aflaj in neighbouring Oman describe the system in detail, and the same falaj culture runs along both sides of these mountains. The basic unit there is the athar. Each day is split into a daytime and a night-time period, each divided into twenty-four athars, so one athar is on average about half an hour. A farmer's share might be several athars, or a half or a quarter of one.

    Before clocks, how did anyone know when their half-hour was up? By day, with a sundial. A typical falaj sundial was a vertical pole in a flat, open space, with rows of stones marking where the shadow of its tip would fall. Some had separate rows of stones for summer, winter, and spring and autumn, because the sun's path changes with the seasons. At night, time was told by the stars. The falaj manager knew which stars rose at which point in the night, and each star marked the end of one share and the start of the next.

    Rights to water could be inherited, sold or rented separately from the land, so a water share was one of the most valuable things a family owned. Longer turns, such as those at dawn and dusk, could be worth more. Running all this needed trust and rules. Someone responsible for the falaj kept the turns, settled disputes and organised the cleaning, and in a place where water meant life, these rules mattered as much as any law.

    The date palms

    On a shaded lane between walled palm gardens. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Look up. The oasis holds more than 147,000 date palms, of up to a hundred varieties. The variety matters because each ripens at its own time. Farmers in Al Ain group their palms into very early varieties, ready in late May, through early and mid-season types, to late varieties that can still be picked in the winter. By planting a mix, a family had fresh dates for much of the year.

    Dates are eaten at different stages, and local Arabic has a name for each. Kimri is the hard green fruit. Khalal is the fruit at full size and full colour, yellow or red, crunchy and still a little astringent. Rutab is the soft, brown, half-ripe date, and tamr is the fully ripe, dry date that keeps for months. Tamr was the reserve that carried families and travellers through the year.

    A date palm needs people at every stage. Palms are either male or female, and only females bear fruit. Although wind and insects can pollinate them, farmers here pollinate by hand to make sure the fruit sets, using pollen collected from a few male palms kept in each garden. After pollination, the heavy bunches are tied to the leaf stalks to support their weight, and some fruit may be thinned so the rest grows larger. All of this means climbing to the crown, which on an old palm can be very high. Climbing is still practised in Al Ain, though mechanical lifts are becoming more common.

    A female palm starts bearing at four to six years old and reaches full production at around fifteen to twenty. Accounts of a single palm's yearly harvest vary widely, from around forty kilos to around a hundred, depending on the variety, the age of the tree and how well it is watered. Some palms in these oases are thought to be several hundred years old.

    Notice the low mud walls around the gardens. Each walled plot is a family holding, and many have been passed down through generations.

    A tree for everything

    Look at the fences and screens along the lanes, and at the fallen fronds under the trees. For the people of this region, the date palm was close to a complete livelihood, and almost nothing of it was wasted.

    Trunks were split and used as beams and posts. The leaves were made into fences that shield young crops from the wind, and woven into mats, screens, baskets and crates. Fronds were burned as fuel, and turned into brooms, walking sticks and fishing gear. The leftovers of the yearly pruning, such as frond bases, fruit stalks and leaflets, were fed to sheep and goats.

    The palm also gave people their summer houses. The arish was a house made from palm materials alone: palm trunks for the frame, dried fronds for the walls and roof, and rope twisted from the fibres at the top of the trunk to tie it all together. The open weave let the breeze through, which made it far cooler in summer than a solid wall. An FAO report on these oases notes that in the 1970s such palm houses still made up most homes in the region, before modern building took over.

    Even the climbing gear came from the palm. To reach the crown, a farmer used the habool, a broad climbing belt of palm fibre looped around his back and the trunk. Hitched around both the climber and the trunk, it let him lean back and work with his hands free. Climbing is how pollen is tied in, bunches are supported and the harvest is brought down, and on a palm more than twenty metres tall it takes real skill.

    Nothing of the fruit was wasted either. Oral histories recorded by the UAE's National Library describe how, at the end of the season, families pressed the dates for their syrup and stored dates and syrup to cook with through the winter, and how even the stones could be roasted to make a coffee. In 2019 the date palm, and the knowledge, skills and traditions around it, were added to UNESCO's list of the world's intangible heritage, shared by the UAE and other Arab countries.

    Dates themselves shaped the history of this land. Archaeologists have found charred date stones on Dalma Island, off the coast of Abu Dhabi, from around seven thousand years ago. Without dates, no large population could have lived in the inland desert. Along with the camel, the date palm made it possible to live in this land, and caravans carried dates along the trade routes for centuries.

    Under the palms

    The narration at this pin is about Under the palms.

    Play0:00 / –:––

    Look below the palm crowns. In the shade, farmers grow other crops: fruit trees such as mango, orange, banana, fig and jujube, and lower down, vegetables and fodder for animals. Alfalfa for livestock is still one of the main crops of the Al Ain oases.

    This is the oasis working as its farmers designed it, in layers. The palms take the full force of the sun. The fruit trees grow in their filtered light. The ground crops grow in the coolest, dampest layer of all, watered by the same channels. Fallen leaves and animal dung feed the soil. In a land where summer temperatures pass forty-five degrees, this layered garden makes a cooler, damper world under the trees.

    The running water of the falaj is what made this possible. In Liwa, the great sand oasis to the south-west, the wells were few and often brackish, and little could grow beside the palms. Here, steady water from the mountains let farmers plant crops in the palms' shade. One study suggests that it roughly doubled the date harvest as well, and let families keep herds of sheep and goats.

    The flood irrigation you may see, where a whole basin around a palm is filled with water, is the oldest form of watering here and is still used. Newer farms elsewhere often use sprinklers or drip pipes instead.

    Farmers here also read the sky. Old farmers of the Abu Dhabi oases have described a calendar of the stars, handed down by word of mouth. The rise of Suhail, the bright star Canopus, in mid-August signalled the end of the worst heat, and with it the best time to dig up the young offshoots that grow at the foot of a mother palm and plant them out. Offshoots are the usual way to make new palms, because it is quicker than growing a palm from a stone. A young palm planted this way starts to bear in a few years.

    As the dates swell, many farmers tie nets around the bunches to keep birds off. Some, one farm manager in Al Ain told a reporter, leave a few bunches uncovered on purpose, for the birds.

    Inside the visitor area, the Oasis Garden is laid out in three sections to show the kinds of plants grown in traditional oasis farms. It is a useful place to put names to what you see growing here.

    The oasis and the city

    In a quiet shaded lane, away from the gate. This is stop 8 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Take a moment in the shade and listen. The water, the birds and the rustle of the palms are the sounds that travellers crossing the desert once longed to hear.

    Around Al Ain, people have farmed and settled for thousands of years: from the Bronze Age villages at Hili to the gardens around you. Al Ain grew into a city because of its oases. Today the gardens sit in the middle of the modern city, and UNESCO counts that as part of their value: farming that has gone on for generations, still woven into the streets around it.

    Sheikh Zayed, the founder of the United Arab Emirates, spent twenty years here, from 1946 to 1966, as the ruler's representative in Al Ain before he became ruler of Abu Dhabi. In those years he is remembered for repairing and sharing out the aflaj, and the protection of these oases stayed a priority for him all his life. A falaj survey of Al Ain notes the great importance he placed on preserving the oases as part of the heritage of the area. He is remembered for saying: give me agriculture, and I will guarantee you civilisation. His love of greening the desert, planting trees and protecting farms was shaped in this landscape, and it is one reason Al Ain is still called the Garden City.

    For generations, summer was the season of the oasis. It was called the qeidh, or in local speech the mqeez. When the coast grew too hot and humid, families from Abu Dhabi travelled inland by camel, and later by Land Rover, to spend the summer in Al Ain and in the oases of Al Dhafra. The season began, elders recall, when the first half-ripe dates appeared, a moment the old star calendars tie to the Pleiades. The poet Ousha bint Khalifa Al Suwaidi wrote of exactly that moment, when the first ripe dates show and, two weeks later, friends start giving them to one another. Varieties with names such as Khinaizi, Hilali and Fard were picked through the summer, along with mangoes.

    The oasis is not a museum piece. Families still own these plots, harvest these palms in summer and take their turns at the water, as their grandparents did. When you walk out, look back at the gardens: a living farm, and one of the oldest ways of living in the desert, in the middle of a modern city.
