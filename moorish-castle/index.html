    The Moorish Castle runs along the top of one of the peaks of the Sintra Hills, its walls winding over the granite rocks above the town.

    People lived on this rock long before the castle. Archaeologists have found Neolithic pottery and flint tools beside the church inside the walls, and traces of a settlement of the late Bronze Age, roughly three thousand years ago.

    For centuries this has been called a Moorish castle. Muslim families certainly lived on this hill, and the excavations show it. A recent study of the stonework, though, suggests that the walls standing today were raised in the twelfth century, after the Christian conquest.

    The castle stood at the centre of a farming district and guarded the roads below and the approaches to Lisbon. From up here the garrison could watch the land and the sea at once.

    It has two rings of walls, an outer one and an inner one, and they follow the shape of the rock rather than a plan, about 450 metres around. The inner wall is crowned with pointed, pyramid-shaped merlons and strengthened by towers, square and round.

    The builders worked with the mountain rather than against it. The walls are made of granite blocks fitted between the boulders and the cliffs, so that the bare rock becomes part of the defences, and the wall turns wherever the rock does.

    The Norse sagas tell of Sigurd, the king of Norway, who attacked a castle at Sintra on his way to the Holy Land, in the early twelfth century. The Parks of Sintra link that visit to this castle.

    In 1147 Afonso Henriques, the first king of Portugal, took Lisbon, and the castle here surrendered to him without a fight. In 1154 he gave Sintra a charter to bring settlers and keep the place defended.
