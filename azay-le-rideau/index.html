    The water mirror gives the Château d'Azay-le-Rideau its famous double, floating on the river Indre.

    The château stands on a small island between two arms of the river. It was built in the early sixteenth century, under King Francis I, for Gilles Berthelot, a royal financier and mayor of Tours. It replaced a medieval fortress that had guarded the crossing of the Indre on the road from Tours to Chinon. During the Hundred Years' War, in 1418, the future King Charles VII had that fortress stormed and burned.

    Building on an island was not easy. The ground was soft and wet, and part of the new château had to be raised on wooden piles driven into the bed of the river. Berthelot's duties kept him away, so the works were run by his wife, Philippa Lesbahy. The novelist Honoré de Balzac, who knew the area well, called it a faceted diamond set in the Indre. Yet the perfect reflection is partly modern: in the nineteenth century a terrace ran along the south wing, and only in 1950 was the river arm widened so that the water touched the walls.
