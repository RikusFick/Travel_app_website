  1. 1

    Hadrian's Gate

    In front of the white marble triple archway on Atatürk Caddesi, at the eastern edge of the old town. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are standing in front of Hadrian's Gate, the white marble triple archway into the old town. Welcome to Kaleiçi , the walled heart of Antalya. In Turkish the name itself means "inside the castle". This quarter was walled twice over, with an inner ring and an outer one, by the various peoples who lived here over some two millennia; sections of those walls remained standing.

    The city was founded around the harbour. Its founder, Pergamon's King Attalos the Second, gave it his own name: Attaleia. That founding came soon after he took the throne, probably around 158 BC.

    The gate's other name, Üçkapılar, means "the Three Gates", after the three arches side by side in front of you. Step closer and the carved and relief ornament stands out; everything but the columns is white marble. The gate once rose two storeys and carried a Latin inscription; only the lower storey has survived.

    Now look at the towers on either side. Neither belongs to the gate's own building work. Julia Sancta, as the southern tower is known, also dates from Hadrian's reign; it was raised separately, though, and left plain. The northern tower shows two eras in one wall: old masonry at its base, Seljuk work on top.

    Attaleia raised this gate in Hadrian's honour when the emperor came to the city, around AD 130 , some accounts say 131. It fitted a pattern of his reign: many cities far from Rome marked his arrival with monumental buildings.

    It was built purely for display, with no role in the city's defences, on the road towards cities such as Perge, Aspendos and Side. For many years, though, it was hidden from view. It came back to light only in 1882, when the walls built against its front and back were pulled down. Those walls went up during wars in the Byzantine period, when the gate was sealed for defence.

  2. 2

    The Fluted Minaret

    Near the Clock Tower and Kalekapısı Square, looking at the tall brick minaret with grooved sides. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The tall brick tower ahead of you is called the Yivli Minare , in English, the Fluted Minaret. It has long been treated as a symbol of Antalya.

    The minaret is attributed to Alaeddin Keykubad the First, the Seljuk sultan whose reign ran from 1220 until 1237. One account points to an inscription that names him as the builder; another treats the attribution as a matter of style. The niches on the west face hold a building inscription.

    Accounts of its height differ: about thirty metres, or about thirty-eight. Tilework in dark blue and turquoise once covered the flutes that run up the brickwork; there are eight in all, each semi-circular. The shaft rises from a square stone base. One account gives that base as about five and a half metres wide and about six and a half metres high.

    Beside the minaret stands a plain, rectangular prayer hall, its outer walls of hewn stone and its six domes finished with tiles on the outside. Mehmet Bey of the Hamidoglu family had the hall rebuilt with those domes in 1373. It replaced a mosque first dated here to 1230, which was wrecked in the 1300s , one account ties the damage to a spell of Lusignan rule. An older church on the same spot was said to have supplied the walls of that first mosque.

    What grew up around the minaret over time was a külliye, a religious complex. Beside it rises the Clock Tower, built on the bastion of the Kale Kapısı, the old city gate, in the first decades of the twentieth century.

    Conservation work was carried out on the minaret in 1953 and 1961. Work in 1973 renewed the minaret's spire.

  3. 3

    The old harbour

    Here the guide turns to The old harbour.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are at the old harbour , the little bay that is the reason Antalya exists. King Attalos set his harbour-town around it. It is a natural harbour, sheltered by the land. From antiquity this bay was used as a place of harbour settlement.

    Look out through the gap between the breakwaters. This stretch of the harbour went by the name Merdivenli İskele, the Stairs Landing. The name came from a flight of steps that ran down through the city wall and later linked the walled quarter to this quay.

    For much of the 1300s, after the Seljuk line ended, Antalya was held by the Hamidoglu family, and under them its sea trade grew stronger. The Seljuks had made the harbour their base on this coast after their sultan Gıyaseddin Keyhüsrev took the city in 1207.

    The Book of Acts tells how Paul and Barnabas, after preaching in Perge, came down to Attaleia and sailed from the city back to Antioch.

    Look back up at the town walls above you, and the old houses peering over them. Many of them are traditional two- or three-storey homes, which turn just one side to the street and keep the others for a garden. Beside the water stands a small mosque. A pyramid-shaped tiled roof covers its six-sided plan. The building is dated 1903.

  4. 4

    The Broken Minaret

    A short story at this point covers The Broken Minaret.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    In front of you is the Kesik Minare, the Broken Minaret , a name earned after fire took the cap of its tower. Its walls hold many periods of the city at once.

    Nearly all the building material in these walls was taken from older Hellenistic and Roman buildings. With it, a church of the Panagia was raised on the site of the city agora of Hellenistic and Roman times, probably in the later 400s or the early 500s. In the Byzantine period the church served as the city's episcopal centre, and from the middle Byzantine period it was called the most sacred place in Pamphylia.

    For roughly four hundred years, until the fire, the building served as a mosque; under the Ottomans it was also known as the New Mosque. That use probably began in the time of Prince Korkut, son of Sultan Bayezid the Second , or, in another account, on Bayezid's own order. Before the change, Christian worship had continued here into the fifteen-hundreds.

    Fire badly damaged the building in the eighteen-nineties, and that damage gave it the name Kesik Minare. One record puts the fire in 1895, another in 1896. For well over a hundred years the building then stood damaged, with only some strengthening of certain sections in 1974.

    Restoration work was launched in 2017, and one report dates the start of comprehensive work to 2018. In 2019 the minaret got back the cap it had lost in the fire. By March 2025 the restoration was complete, and the building was open for worship and for visits.

  5. 5

    Hıdırlık Tower

    At the round stone tower on the south-west corner of the old town walls, above the sea. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    In front of you is Hıdırlık Tower, about fourteen metres high, at the south-west angle of the old walls. Its use as a tower dates from the Byzantine period, when it was joined into the city's defences.

    Before that it is thought to have been a tomb: a Roman funerary monument of the first or second century. Seen from outside, it has two storeys, a round drum resting on a square podium.

    On both sides of the main doorway, look for twelve carved fasces, the Roman bundles of rods. They probably mark the tomb's owner as a man of consular rank.

    Hesapçı Sokak, the lane that links this tower with Hadrian's Gate, was in antiquity the Platae, Attaleia's main street. The city's shops stood on it, and so did the buildings from which Attaleia was governed.

    Look out over the sea and the bay, and the Taurus Mountains beyond.
