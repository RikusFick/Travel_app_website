  1. 1

    Khafre's pyramid: the one with the cap

    At the foot of the north face of Khafre's pyramid, near the entrances. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Look up. The top of this pyramid still wears a white cap. That is the last of its original casing, fine Tura limestone, smooth and sloping, and it shows how the whole pyramid once looked. Its builders called this king Khafre, and the Greeks called him Chephren. This is his tomb, the middle one of the three great pyramids of Giza.

    It looks taller than the Great Pyramid next to it, but it is not. Khafre was a son of Khufu, and his pyramid is a little smaller. It sits on bedrock about ten metres higher, and its sides are steeper, at about fifty-three degrees. Together that gives it its commanding look. The base measures about two hundred and fifteen metres on each side. Its original height is given as anywhere from about one hundred and thirty-six to about one hundred and forty-eight metres, depending on the source.

    Now look at the bottom. The first course of casing here was pink granite from Aswan, hundreds of kilometres to the south, and above it came the white limestone. Where the casing has gone, you can see the rough core, and how the stones get smaller as the pyramid rises. The builders used the natural rock of the plateau as part of the structure. On the north-west corner the rock was cut away, and on the south-east the ground was built up to level it.

    Look closely at the surviving casing. The outer faces of the blocks are often staggered by a few millimetres rather than flush. That may mean the blocks were dressed to shape before they were set in place.

    Here on the north side are two entrances, both on this face. One is at ground level, and the other is about eleven and a half metres up. Why two? Nobody knows for certain, and the usual explanation is a change of plan during building.
