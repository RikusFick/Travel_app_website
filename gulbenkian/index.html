    The Calouste Gulbenkian Museum holds one of the most important private art collections in the world, gathered by a single man over a lifetime.

    Calouste Sarkis Gulbenkian was born in March 1869 in Üsküdar, on the Asian shore of Constantinople, today Istanbul, the son of an Armenian trader and banker. He studied engineering and applied sciences at King's College in London, and he became one of the great negotiators of the early oil industry in the Middle East. In the early 1900s he worked closely with Henri Deterding, the head of Royal Dutch, later Royal Dutch Shell, one of the largest oil companies in the world. In 1928 a famous agreement shared out the Turkish Petroleum Company among the giant oil firms. It was later renamed the Iraq Petroleum Company. The agreement kept five per cent of the shares for Gulbenkian himself. That share gave him his nickname, Mister Five Per Cent, and the fortune behind this collection.

    In April 1942, during the Second World War, Gulbenkian came to Lisbon, and he never left. He lived at the Aviz Hotel, a small and exclusive hotel long since demolished, until his death in July 1955, at the age of eighty-six. In his will he left most of his fortune to a foundation based in Portugal, with artistic, charitable, scientific and educational aims, and he left his collection to be shown here.

    The museum opened in October 1969, together with the Foundation's headquarters and the garden around them. The architects were Alberto Pessoa, Pedro Cid and Ruy Jervis d'Athouguia, who won an invited competition between three teams of architects. The headquarters was planned as a cultural centre, with auditoriums and exhibition spaces alongside the museum.

    The complex is one of the landmarks of modern Portuguese architecture of the 1960s. It won the Valmor Prize, Lisbon's architecture award, in 1975, and in 2010 the headquarters building was classified a National Monument.

    The architects built in raw, exposed concrete, together with granite, glass and bronze. Reinforced and prestressed concrete allowed long spans inside, and the buildings were kept low and horizontal, with large windows that open the rooms onto the garden.

    The collection numbers about six thousand pieces, of which around a thousand are displayed in the galleries. Displays change from time to time.
