    The south portal of the Jerónimos Monastery is a carved stone doorway about thirty-two metres high and more than twelve metres wide, rising through both storeys of the façade.

    The monastery stands on the old beach of Restelo, where ships left for Africa, India and Brazil. In the middle of the 1400s Prince Henry the Navigator had a small chapel here built or enlarged, and he gave it to the Order of Christ. It became the chapel of Santa Maria de Belém, Saint Mary of Bethlehem. Tradition says that Vasco da Gama and his crews kept a night of prayer in it before sailing for India in 1497.

    King Manuel the First chose this spot for something far larger: a royal monastery for the monks of the Order of Saint Jerome. They were to pray for the king's soul and give spiritual help to the sailors leaving from this shore. Building began in the first years of the 1500s and went on for about a hundred years. Much of the money came from a levy of about five per cent on the trade with Africa and the East.

    The whole building is of lioz, a fine limestone quarried near Lisbon. Fresh, it is pale, almost white; with age it warms to the honey colour of this front.

    The portal was built between 1516 and 1518 by João de Castilho and his workshop. It is a crowd of carved figures standing under lace-like stone canopies, climbing towards the top. On the pillar between the two doors stands Prince Henry the Navigator, shown as a knight in armour. He stood behind the chapel that began this place, and he backed the first voyages of discovery. Higher up is Our Lady of Belém, to whom the church is dedicated; and right at the summit stands an archangel. The two small scenes over the doors come from the life of Saint Jerome. In one, dressed as a cardinal, he pulls a thorn from a lion's paw, a story told about him since the Middle Ages, and the lion became his constant companion in art. In the other he does penance in the desert.

    For all its splendour, this was never the church's main entrance. That is the west portal, at the other end of the building.
