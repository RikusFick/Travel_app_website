    Read along

    You are standing where the main entrance of the Temple of Seti I once rose. A great pylon, a gateway flanked by two massive towers, stood here. It has gone, and so has much of the first courtyard behind it. The king who began this temple was Seti the First.

    Seti ruled from around 1290 BC until about 1279 BC, though some chronologies start his reign a few years earlier. He began this temple on fresh ground, and it was unfinished when he died. His son Ramesses the Second completed it, and his cartouches appear in parts of the building. The plan is unusual. Instead of one straight axis, the temple is L-shaped, with a wing turning off behind the main halls. It is built mainly of limestone, with some sandstone.

    Why build here? Abydos was the burial ground of Egypt's earliest kings. From the end of the Old Kingdom, it became the great cult centre of Osiris, god of the dead. Egyptians travelled here on pilgrimage. Seti's temple was both a memorial to himself and a place of reverence for the early pharaohs, and it was part of the Osiris festival.

    Even after its priesthood faded, the complex remained active. By the seventh century BC, quarriers were already stripping parts of it for limestone. Yet in Greco-Roman times it hosted an oracle of Bes, and early Christians later sheltered inside.

    UNESCO records that the French traveller Granger brought it back to scholarly notice in 1731, before Auguste Mariette excavated the sand-buried courts in the 1860s.
