  1. 1

    The park

    Inside Hili Archaeological Park, on the path towards the Grand Tomb. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Read along

    You are standing in one of the most important groups of Bronze Age sites in the United Arab Emirates, and indeed in the whole Arabian Peninsula. Around five thousand years ago, this area on the northern edge of today's Al Ain was farmland and village: round mudbrick towers, houses, fields, palm groves, and great stone tombs for the dead.

    It is hard to picture among lawns and paths, but the landscape explains why people came. Al Ain lies on a narrow, fertile plain. To the west are the sands of the Empty Quarter; to the east rise the Hajar Mountains. When storms break over the mountains, the run-off soaks into the gravels and is stored underground, where it can be reached by wells and, later, by channels called aflaj. The same mountains hold copper. Water and metal together made this a place where people could settle, farm and trade, very early in the history of Arabia.

    Archaeology here began with an invitation. In the early 1960s Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan, then ruler's representative in Al Ain and later the first President of the UAE, invited a Danish team to dig. They uncovered a tower and the Grand Tomb, both in this park. Local archaeologists surveyed the area through the 1970s, French teams worked here from the late 1970s, and the Department of Culture and Tourism Abu Dhabi excavates here today. The finds number in the thousands.

    Within this garden are the Grand Tomb, two more tombs called E and N, and two settlements known as Hili 1 and Hili 10. Other sites lie scattered just outside. Since 2011 the Bronze Age sites of Hili have formed part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site called the Cultural Sites of Al Ain, alongside Jebel Hafit, Bidaa Bint Saud and the oases. Many of the objects found here are in Al Ain Museum, which reopened in October 2025 after a long renovation.
