    Read along

    In front of you is Al Hisn, the fort of Sharjah. For a century and a half it was both the home of the ruling Al Qasimi family and the seat of government, and when it was built it was the largest and most important building in the town. Most accounts date it to 1820, in the time of Sheikh Sultan bin Saqr Al Qasimi; the museum's own description gives 1823. Either way, it belongs to the early 1820s.

    That was a turning point for this coast. The Al Qasimi had been a major sea power in the lower Gulf, with fleets based here and at Ras Al Khaimah. After British naval expeditions against them, the rulers of the coast signed the General Maritime Treaty with Britain in 1820. With the age of the fleets ending, the fort became the centre of a ruler governing his town on land: a residence, a court, a treasury and a refuge for the people in times of danger.

    Look at the walls. They are built of coral stone, cut from the reefs of the Gulf, laid in mortar and covered with a light brown plaster. The builders also used sea sand and pebbles from the island of Abu Musa. The doors are of teak, and the ceilings, as you will see inside, are of mangrove poles and stripped palm ribs. Not everything was local: teak and other hardwoods came by sea from India, and some timber from East Africa, so the fort itself is a record of Sharjah's trade.

    It had three towers. The round one, Al Kabs, is the only tower that survived when the fort was pulled down.

    In January 1970, during a short and troubled reign, most of the fort was demolished; some accounts put the start of the work in late 1969. Sultan bin Muhammad Al Qasimi, then a student in Cairo, hurried home to stop it. He was too late to save more than the tower and two damaged walls, but he kept the fort's main doors, among them the main gate, Al Sabah gate and Al Khazna gate, the treasury gate. In January 1972 he became Ruler of Sharjah. About a quarter of a century later, in 1996, he had the fort rebuilt on its old foundations, using old photographs and the memories of older residents, and the saved doors were hung again. It opened as a museum in 1997, and reopened after a further restoration in 2015.
