  1. 1

    Crystal Mountain

    Beside the low ridge and natural arch of Crystal Mountain, just off the road from Bahariya to Farafra. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Driving south from Bahariya, most trips stop here. It is a low, pale ridge on the side of the road. It is neither tall nor wide. What it does is catch the sun.

    The local name is Gebel el-Izzaz. That is roughly the mountain of glass. Look for the natural arch. It stands about as tall as a person. Its inner faces are lined with blades of crystal.

    What are they? Postcards often say quartz. Accounts disagree. Some say the crystals are barite and calcite, and others add quartz. A study of the park's rocks, in a geology journal, says calcite crystals are well exposed here. So calcite is the one name they all share.

    The difference matters for one reason. Barite and calcite are soft. They rate about three to three and a half on the hardness scale geologists use. Quartz rates seven. A steel blade would scratch these.

    How did they grow? The basic story is simple. Hot water, rich in dissolved minerals, moved through the rock underground. As it cooled, it left crystals in cracks and hollows. Wind and weather later stripped away the softer stone around them.

    The geology study fits this ridge into a bigger picture. It names Crystal Mountain as one of the karst depressions on the plateau between the two oases. Karst is rock that water has slowly dissolved. Elsewhere in the area, the study says, calcite crystals can reach twenty centimetres. Some grow in branching, curved or spiral shapes.

    This ridge has paid for its looks. It is said to have been cut open when the road was built, and part of it to have gone into the roadbed. Local accounts speak of years of quarrying after that. Visitors did the rest. Visitors taking crystals are said to have stripped most of the larger ones already.

    Egypt protects its natural areas under Law 102 of 1983. That law forbids damaging or removing natural features in a protected area. So look closely, take a photograph, and leave every crystal where it is.
