    The Giralda

    Look up at the Giralda, an Almohad minaret crowned with a Renaissance belfry and rotating weathervane.

    Free sample: stop 1

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This great brick tower is the Giralda, the bell tower of Seville Cathedral and the symbol of the city. Its lower part was the minaret of the city's great mosque, begun in 1184 by the architect Ahmad ibn Baso for the Almohad rulers, and finished on 10 March 1198, when four gilded bronze spheres were set on top. The minaret stands on a square base about 13.6 metres a side, and its main shaft rises some fifty metres.

    Inside, instead of stairs, thirty-five ramps wind up around the core. It is said they were made wide enough for a rider on horseback. After the Christian conquest of 1248 the minaret became a bell tower. In the sixteenth century the architect Hernán Ruiz the Younger added the Renaissance belfry, and in 1568 a bronze statue of Faith, the Giraldillo, was raised to the very top. Figures for the tower's height range from about 95 to about 104 metres, depending on how it is measured and whether the statue is counted.

    The Almohad builders worked almost entirely in brick, on a base of reused stone blocks taken from older walls. The upper faces of the minaret are covered with a raised lattice of brick, a net of interlocking diamond shapes known as sebka. The original four spheres did not last: they are recorded as falling in an earthquake in 1356.

    That statue turns in the wind as a weather vane, and in Spanish a "giralda" is a figure that turns. The name passed from the vane to the whole tower, and the statue is now called the Giraldillo.
