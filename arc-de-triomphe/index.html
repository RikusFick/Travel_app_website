    The Arc de Triomphe

    This part of the route looks at The Arc de Triomphe.

    Free sample: stop 1

    Read along

    The Arc de Triomphe rises about fifty metres above the busiest roundabout in Paris. It is about forty-five metres wide and twenty-two metres deep, and two hundred and eighty-four steps climb inside it to the roof terrace.

    The day after his victory at Austerlitz in December 1805, Napoleon ordered a triumphal arch to honour his Grande Armée, and he signed the decree in 1806. The architect Jean-François Chalgrin drew the plans, inspired by ancient Roman arches. His chief model was the Arch of Titus in Rome. The foundations alone took two years. In 1810, when Napoleon married the Archduchess Marie-Louise of Austria and she made her entry into Paris from the west, the arch was still only a stump, so a full-size mock-up was built of timber and painted canvas for her to pass beneath. Chalgrin died in 1811, and his pupil Louis-Robert Goust took over.

    Napoleon fell long before it was done. Work stopped and started for thirty years, and the arch was finally inaugurated in 1836 under King Louis-Philippe, dedicated to the armies of the Revolution and the Empire. The names of battles and generals are carved on its walls.

    Many plans were made to crown the top with a great sculpture, and none survived. From 1882 to 1886 a full-size plaster chariot by Alexandre Falguière, titled the Triumph of the Revolution, stood on the summit to judge the effect, until the weather began to break it apart and it was taken down. The arch has stayed bare ever since.

    It has seen many processions. In December 1840 the cortège bringing Napoleon's remains back from Saint Helena passed beneath it. For that day the architect Guillaume-Abel Blouet set a provisional sculpture of Napoleon on the summit. On 7 August 1919 the pilot Charles Godefroy flew a Nieuport biplane straight through the main arch.

    The arch was meant to glorify the army. Napoleon is said to have told his soldiers after Austerlitz that they would return home only beneath arches of triumph. A site at the Bastille was considered, but this hilltop on the western edge of Paris was chosen for its long views. The square around it was later called the Étoile, the star, because twelve avenues, laid out in the nineteenth century under Napoleon III and Baron Haussmann, radiate from it. The arch is built of a hard limestone from Château-Landon.
