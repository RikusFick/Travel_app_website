  1. 1

    A house built by Zayed bin Khalifa

    In the square outside the main entrance of Al Jahili Fort. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    In front of you is one of the largest traditional forts in the United Arab Emirates. Al Jahili Fort was begun in 1891 by Sheikh Zayed bin Khalifa, known as Zayed the First, who ruled Abu Dhabi from 1855 to 1909 and was the grandfather of Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan, the founder of the UAE. Work went on for years: the official account says the fort was completed in 1898, the year 1316 in the Islamic calendar.

    That date was carved on a wooden panel at the entrance, together with two lines of verse. In translation they say: "A door of goodness opens in this glorious chapter, where joy and happiness reside. The blessings of honour said, mark this house, a house of high standing built by Zayed bin Khalifa." It is a proud inscription, and it tells you what the fort was for. It was a show of power, a way of controlling the tribes around the oasis and protecting the date gardens and their water channels, and it was also a summer home. Each summer Abu Dhabi's rulers left the damp heat of the coast for Al Ain, where the air is drier and there is fresh water and shade.

    The grand gateway in front of you, with its two big towers, is not from Zayed's time. It was added during a restoration between 1986 and 1989, and built in the old way, with mudbrick walls and palm-log roofs, so that it would match the older parts.

    Look at the walls. Mudbrick is earth mixed with water, pressed into moulds and dried in the sun. Over the bricks goes a coat of mud plaster, which has to be renewed again and again, because rain slowly washes it off. Restorers describe the walls as about ninety centimetres thick. Walls that thick soak up the heat of the day slowly and give it off at night, keeping the rooms behind them cooler.

    The outer wall is about eight metres high. The fort is said to be about 118 metres long, although that figure comes from a single survey.

    When Zayed the First died in 1909, his eldest son, Sheikh Khalifa bin Zayed, chose not to become ruler. He stayed in Al Ain instead and lived here in the fort with his family.

  2. 2

    The round tower

    At the foot of the tall, tiered round tower. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This tower is the symbol of Al Jahili, and of Al Ain. It rises in rings, like drums stacked on top of each other, with each ring a little narrower than the one below. Most descriptions say it has four tiers and is about fourteen metres high. Look at the top of each ring and you will see a fringe of triangular crenellations, the pointed tops typical of Al Ain's mud towers.

    The tower is older than the fort around it. Archaeologists working for the Abu Dhabi culture department link it with the falaj, the water channel, that ran past it. Together they probably belonged to the date plantations here in the eighteenth century, before the fort was built. It began as a watchtower guarding the water. Whoever controlled the falaj controlled the gardens, so a tower like this was a guard post for the whole oasis economy. In raids, a lookout up here could see riders coming and raise the alarm.

    The shape also makes sense for building in earth. A mud tower has to carry its own heavy weight, so it is wide at the bottom. Making it narrower as it rises means each ring is lighter than the one under it. The small openings in the walls let defenders look out and shoot while staying hidden.

    The tower has changed since then. The 1950s were its busiest years. In the 1950s British-officered troops took over the fort. The official history says the tower was first a sentry post, and that in 1957 it was turned into officers' quarters and an extra storey was added. The top room, the highest point of the fort and the one that caught the coolest breeze, was kept for the commanding officer. Not every source agrees on this. Another official description treats four tiers as the tower's original form, so the extra storey may have been a rebuilding of one level rather than a new one.

    Photographs of the tower from those years became famous. The fort appears on the back of the UAE's fifty-dirham banknote.

  3. 3

    The old fort

    In front of the square inner fort, by its arched gate. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This square block is the old fort, the heart of what Zayed the First built in the 1890s. It is roughly thirty-five metres on each side. Its high walls have walkways along the top, behind crenellations, where defenders could move around the whole circuit. The arched gate in its wall is the original kind of entrance, and this one has survived.

    Look at the corners. The official survey describes tall round towers at two opposite corners, the northwest and the southeast, and says the southwest corner originally had a square tower. The fourth corner, in the northeast, seems to have held the living quarters from the start. Official descriptions say the sheikh's majlis was there, the reception room where he received guests and did official business. There is some disagreement about the towers. One official text says three round towers and one square one, and another gives the square tower's place differently. The basic plan is the same in every account: towers at three corners, and the rooms of the household at the fourth.

    This plan was not new. The archaeologists point out that the old fort is almost the same size and layout as the old inner fort at Qasr Al Hosn in Abu Dhabi, built about a century earlier, and as the later Sultan Fort in Al Ain Oasis. In other words, the ruling family built its forts to one tested design, from the late 1700s into the early 1900s.

    Inside, rooms were built against the walls around a small courtyard. The roofs were made of palm trunks laid across the walls, covered with mats of palm fronds and then a thick layer of earth. Nearly everything came from the oasis: earth, water and the date palm, which gave beams, fronds and fibre. When a building like this was abandoned it simply went back to the soil. That is one reason restorers today describe these materials as natural and recyclable.

  4. 4

    The great courtyard and the Scouts

    In the large open courtyard. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Stand in the open and look around. Long ago, a single enclosure wall joined the old fort and the older watchtower, and created a rectangular yard about ninety by sixty metres. The archaeologists compare it to an older Al Ain plan called the sur, where a tall stronghold stood in one corner of a walled compound. In a raid, people and their animals could shelter safely inside walls like these. The entrance was an arched gate in the north wall. That gate was still standing in 1948. It has since been rebuilt, in work completed around 2013, based on excavation and old photographs.

    By the 1950s the fort had fallen into disrepair. In 1955 Sheikh Shakhbut bin Sultan, then ruler of Abu Dhabi, offered it to the Trucial Oman Levies, renamed the Trucial Oman Scouts in 1956. This was a British-officered force that kept order across what were then the Trucial States. The Scouts built an administrative compound in the western part, with barracks and stores in two matching blocks. They added a tin-roofed barracks inside the old fort and a cookhouse against the walls. One block held the armoury and the radio post, and traces of both were found when the Thesiger exhibition was installed. The Scouts' camel patrols carried first aid and basic medicines out to remote villages, and they formed a military band here in 1957. The army kept using the fort until it was handed to the new Union Defence Force in 1971.

    The single large courtyard you are standing in dates from the 1986 to 1989 restoration. Then, in 2007 and 2008, the Abu Dhabi culture authority turned the newer wings into an information centre and exhibition halls. The rooms around the arcaded gallery are cooled by a network of chilled-water pipes laid under the mud plaster, on almost every wall and ceiling. The restorers took down damaged earth, soaked it and used it again. They treated old palm lintels and reused them, and quietly added modern supports to help the walls withstand earthquakes. The project won the Terra Award for earthen architecture in Lyon in 2016.

  5. 5

    Mubarak bin London

    Inside the permanent exhibition on Wilfred Thesiger. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This exhibition is called "Mubarak bin London: Wilfred Thesiger and the Freedom of the Desert". Thesiger was a British explorer, writer and photographer. In the late 1940s he crossed the Rub' al Khali, the Empty Quarter, twice, riding camels with Bedouin companions. His best-known companions were two young men, Salim bin Kabina and Salim bin Ghabaisha. The Bedouin gave him the name Mubarak bin London.

    His second crossing brought him to Abu Dhabi, where the ruling Al Nahyan family welcomed him. In Al Ain he was the guest of Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan, then the ruler's representative here, and he joined him in his daily majlis. Accounts describe Thesiger staying for about a month and riding out with Zayed on hunts with falcons and saluki dogs. The two men became friends through their shared love of the desert, and Thesiger later wrote about Zayed with great admiration.

    Look closely at the black-and-white photographs. They are among the best records of desert life just before oil changed everything: camels at wells, camps, sand ridges stretching to the horizon, faces lined by sun and wind. Thesiger took pictures as he travelled, of his companions as much as of the land.

    He was in no hurry to write about it. His friends had to persuade him, and his first book, "Arabian Sands", came out in 1959, about ten years after his last crossing. It became a classic of travel writing.

    Look at the rooms themselves as well. The armoury and the radio post of the Trucial Oman Scouts once stood in this part of the fort, and traces of both were found when this exhibition was fitted out. The photographs hang in cool rooms with thick earth walls, the same kind of building Thesiger would have rested in on his journeys.

  6. 6

    The mosque outside the walls

    Outside the walls, near the small mosque. This is stop 6 on the route.

    Read along

    Outside the walls, to the south-west, is a small mosque. The official account says it was probably built at the same time as the old fort, at the end of the nineteenth century. The original building survives beneath a new coat of mud plaster added when it was conserved. If you go up to it, please do so quietly, as at any mosque, and remember that clothing covering the shoulders and knees is expected. If people are praying, wait or watch from a respectful distance.

    Its plan is simple. There is a single prayer room with an open arcade along its eastern side, and you enter through a walled courtyard. In the courtyard is a low platform from which the call to prayer was made. Beside it is a small room that was probably used for wudu, the washing before prayer.

    This simplicity is typical of the old mosques of Al Ain. Like the fort, the mosque is built of mudbrick and roofed with palm trunks and fronds. Its decoration is almost entirely the way the light falls through the arcade and across the earth walls. The building needed nothing more to do its work: to give the soldiers, the household and the farmers of the gardens around it a clean, shaded place to pray five times a day.

    Look at the ground too. Remains of a falaj, a water channel, are recorded near the fort. Archaeologists connect it with the old round tower inside. The water, the gardens and the fort worked as one system. The channel watered the palms, the palms fed the people, and the fort and its tower guarded both. In a place where a missed season of water could mean hunger, guarding the falaj was worth an army.

  7. 7

    Al Ain Palace: Sheikh Zayed's home

    Outside the walls of Al Ain Palace Museum. This is stop 7 on the route.

    Read along

    Behind these walls is the home where Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan, the founder of the United Arab Emirates, lived with his family for almost thirty years. The palace was built in 1937 on the western edge of Al Ain Oasis. Zayed lived here during his two decades as the ruler's representative in Al Ain, the eastern region of Abu Dhabi. He left in 1966, when he became ruler of Abu Dhabi. Five years later, in 1971, he became the first President of the UAE.

    The palace was also where the region was run. In its rooms, councils met to issue directives on public services for the eastern region, and from here Zayed set off to dig wells, repair the aflaj and plant trees. That work earned him his reputation long before he led the country.

    Look at the walls. They are built in the traditional way of Al Ain, from local, natural materials: clay, adobe bricks and plaster stones. The roofs, ceilings, doors and windows were made from parts of the palm tree, and teak was brought in later during renovations. The plaster has a warm red-orange colour, and one newspaper description gives the whole complex an area of about 15,600 square metres, with around thirty-seven rooms.

    The towers at the main entrance were added during the restoration that began in 1998, deliberately designed to look like those of Al Jahili Fort. Zayed himself ordered that restoration, carried out by his own team. He wanted the palace kept as an honest picture of life before oil and before the union. It opened as a museum in 2001.

    At the time of writing, the palace has been closed for renovation since 1 July 2024, with no reopening date announced.

  8. 8

    Inside the palace walls

    This segment focuses on Inside the palace walls.

    Read along

    You cannot go inside at the moment, but you can picture the place. Behind these walls the palace is a series of courtyards, and accounts describe four parts. There was the family's private home, a court for the relatives of Sheikha Fatima bint Mubarak, Zayed's wife, a section for official guests and delegations, and a public section that anyone could enter.

    Above the main entrance was the barza, an upper majlis where important guests were received and where people could sit in the shade on hot days. An inner gate decorated with verses from the Holy Quran leads to the guest rooms and the council rooms. The oldest reception room in the palace is called the Majlis of Bu Da'un, named after the palm trunks and fronds it was built from. Accounts describe the majlis rooms with cushions all around the walls, seating more than twenty-five people. Each had a coffee room next to it, and from the main coffee room in the outer yard, Arabic coffee was carried all over the palace.

    Sheikh Zayed and Sheikha Fatima's own house had two floors. Sheikha Fatima received women visitors on the ground floor, and the upper floor belonged to the family. In the outer courtyard there was a well, the tawi, with rooms around it for her relatives, as well as the kitchen, a storeroom and quarters for the staff.

    There was also a classroom where Zayed's sons studied, still with its original desks and blackboard, and another room where the household learned the Holy Quran and the Hadith, the sayings of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. In the grounds stood a large tent like the one Zayed used to receive guests in winter, and in the inner courtyard there was a Land Rover like the ones he drove across the desert to visit people in their villages.

    For a ruler's home, it was modest: earth walls, palm roofs, a well and a garden next to the oasis. That modesty is a large part of what it means to people here today.
