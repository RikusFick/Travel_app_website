Stops

  1. 1

    Al Fahidi Fort

    Outside the main gate of Al Fahidi Fort, on its eastern wall. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Read along

    You are looking at the oldest building still standing in Dubai. Al Fahidi Fort is usually dated to 1787, when Dubai was a small settlement of fishermen, pearl divers and traders on the edge of the Creek. Some accounts give 1799 instead. The likeliest way both can be true is that the fort grew in stages: its oldest tower is thought to go back to the 1780s, and the walls and rooms were added and enlarged over the following decades.

    Look at its shape. It is a near-square enclosure, reported as about forty-one metres by thirty-three, with towers at three of its corners. Accounts describe two of them as round towers, called burj, and one as a rectangular tower, called murabba. From their tops, guards could watch every approach. The fort's job was to protect the town from the land side, from the desert, while the Creek guarded the other side. When it was built, it stood on the edge of the settlement, facing outward.

    Now look at the walls. They are built of coral and shell stone taken from the sea and the shore, set in a lime-based mortar. Thick walls like these kept attackers out, and they also held off the fierce summer heat. Ceilings inside are described as palm trunks laid side by side. The main door is reported to be solid teak, studded with large iron nails.

    Over two centuries the fort has been many things: the home and office of the ruler, the seat of government and justice, an arsenal for weapons, and at times a prison. In 1969, Dubai asked Kuwait to send a museum expert, and in 1971, the same year the United Arab Emirates was founded, the fort opened as the Dubai Museum. In the 1990s it was restored and an underground extension was added beneath the courtyard, where life-size scenes of the souq, the desert and the sea recreated the time before oil.

    The fort closed in 2021 for a long rehabilitation. The new museum plans six main galleries, an underground extension, and access to the old walls, watchtowers, gates and courtyard.

  2. 2

    The lanes of Al Fahidi

    In one of the narrow lanes of the Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Read along

    Step into the shade of this lane, and notice how narrow it is. These walkways are called sikkas, and they are rarely more than two or three metres wide. That is deliberate. In a town without electricity, a narrow lane between high walls stays in shadow for most of the day. Air moving through it speeds up, so even a faint breeze off the Creek feels cooler here than in the open.

    This quarter was once called Al Bastakiya. It was built from around the 1890s by merchants from southern Persia, from the area of Bastak, who were drawn to Dubai by its growing trade and the welcome it gave to traders. They brought their own way of building with them. Accounts describe around sixty houses at the quarter's height. The protected district today runs about three hundred metres along the Creek and about two hundred metres inland, and the houses are low, no more than two storeys, so the wind towers stand clear above them.

    Look at the walls around you. Before about 1900, most homes along the Creek were built of palm fronds, a type of house called areesh. Historians link the change to a fire in 1894 that swept through the Deira side of the Creek and destroyed most of the market and the palm-frond homes around it. People turned to a material that could not burn. The houses here are built of sea stone and coral from the Creek, stacked like bricks and set in a mixture of mud, sand and gypsum, sometimes with lime. The coral was dried before use. The pale finish reflected the sun, and the walls were then coated with a layer of sand and mud for insulation. Timber for doors, roof beams and window screens came largely by dhow, teak among it, and roofs were laid on palm-wood beams.

    A house like this was a sign of wealth. Its outer walls were mostly blank, with only small, high openings, and life turned inward to a central courtyard. A short stretch of the old town wall, thought to date from around 1800 and made of coral stone and gypsum, still survives inside the quarter.

  3. 3

    The wind towers

    Below one of the square wind towers rising above the houses. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Read along

    Look up at the square tower rising above the rooftops. This is a barjeel, a wind tower, and it is one of the oldest ways of cooling a building without power. Towers like it were built across Persia and the Gulf, and the idea reaches back thousands of years.

    It works with simple physics. The tower is open at the top on all four sides, and divided inside by cross walls into shafts. Breezes high above the street, cooler and steadier than the air at ground level, are caught by whichever side faces the wind and pushed down into the room below. At the same time, warm air inside the house rises and escapes up the other shafts. The result is a steady movement of air through the room. Modern studies suggest a working wind tower can lower the temperature inside by several degrees, in good conditions by close to ten.

    The tower was built of the same coral stone and gypsum as the house, and the top was left open. It usually stood over one room, often the room where the family slept or received guests in the hottest months. The house was the cooling system: the narrow lanes shaded the walls, the courtyard drew cooler air down at night, and wooden lattice screens called mashrabiya let air through while blocking the sun and the eyes of passers-by.

    Owning a wind tower was a mark of status. In the early twentieth century, only a few buildings in Dubai had them.

    The quarter came close to disappearing. In the 1980s, about half of it was demolished for new offices, and by 1989 the rest was set to go. A British architect living in one of the houses, Rayner Otter, campaigned to save it and wrote to Prince Charles, who was due to visit Dubai that year. The prince walked through the district and urged that it be preserved, and the demolition was called off. Restoration followed. Today more than fifty restored houses stand here, used as galleries, cafés, small museums and cultural centres.

  4. 4

    A house open to visitors

    Outside the Sheikh Mohammed Centre for Cultural Understanding. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Read along

    This restored courtyard house is home to the Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum Centre for Cultural Understanding. It was founded in 1998 as a non-profit centre to help visitors and residents understand Emirati culture, customs and Islam, under the motto "Open Doors. Open Minds." It has been based in this neighbourhood since 2004.

    The centre runs guided walks through the district and shared meals, where visitors sit on the floor with Emirati hosts, eat traditional dishes and are invited to ask anything they like about local life and faith.

    Look at the doorway, and the courtyard beyond if it is open, because this is how such a house worked. The street side gave little away: a heavy wooden door, plain walls, small windows set high. Inside, rooms opened onto a shaded central courtyard, which gave the family privacy, light and air. In many homes, sources say, the family kept chickens or goats there, and a tree for shade; more than a dozen old trees still grow in the quarter's courtyards.

    The courtyard also worked as part of the cooling. At night, cooler air settled into it and spread into the rooms around it. By day its high walls and deep, shaded edges kept the sun off the living spaces and kept out dust and sand.

    The layout reflected the importance of privacy and hospitality. The majlis, the room where the men of the house received guests, was often placed near the entrance, so that visitors never needed to pass through the family's private rooms. In larger houses walls separated men's and women's quarters, and the washroom was set apart in a corner. Wealthier houses had two storeys, with arches and carved wooden columns around the courtyard, and a wind tower over the best room.

    The people who lived here were mostly merchants, traders and judges. One local guide has described the quarter as the most sought-after address in Dubai in its day. Life in houses like these carried on until about the early 1970s, when families moved to newer homes with electricity and air conditioning.

  5. 5

    The Coffee Museum

    This stop is given to The Coffee Museum.

    Read along

    Coffee is the drink of welcome in the Gulf, and this old house in the lanes of Al Fahidi is given over entirely to it. The Coffee Museum opened in September 2014, timed to International Coffee Day, and it is described as the first museum in the UAE dedicated to coffee. It sits in Villa 44, one of the restored houses of the quarter.

    The museum tells the story from the beginning. Its displays start with the legend of Kaldi, an Ethiopian goatherd, whose goats are said to have eaten the berries of an unknown tree and stayed lively all night. From the highlands of Ethiopia the drink spread to Arabia, and from Arabia, as the museum tells it, around the world.

    The museum has two floors. On the ground floor, several rooms hold coffee equipment from the Arab world and far beyond: ornate dallahs, the beaked coffee pots of the Gulf, along with grinders, roasters, urns and cups in silver, brass and other metals. Look for the Bedouin coffee tools in the recreated majlis next to the entrance, and for a centrepiece made for the museum, a large silver charcoal burner in the Egyptian style. Among the most surprising pieces are coffee grinders from the First World War, made from the metal of melted-down bullets. There are brewing pots from Egypt, Yemen and Ethiopia, and in the international rooms pieces from Europe and Japan, such as a single-wheel coffee mill from Germany.

    The museum is also a place where coffee is made, not only shown. Staff demonstrate how beans are roasted and brewed in the styles of different countries, so the smell of roasting often reaches the lane. Upstairs there are books on the history of coffee, documentary films in a media room, and a small lounge.

    Arabic coffee has a special standing here. In 2015, the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman and Qatar together had it inscribed by UNESCO as part of the intangible cultural heritage of humanity, and Jordan joined the listing in 2024. UNESCO describes the etiquette: older people and guests are served first, the coffee is poured with the right hand, and a guest who has had enough gives the small cup a gentle shake.

  6. 6

    The Bur Dubai Grand Mosque

    In the street beside the Bur Dubai Grand Mosque, next to Al Fahidi Fort. This is stop 6 on the route.

    Read along

    Look up at the minaret. At about seventy metres, it is described as the tallest minaret in Dubai, a slim tower that has served as a landmark for boats on the Creek, much like a lighthouse.

    A place of faith and learning has stood here for well over a century. Around 1900, this site was a kuttab, a school where children learned to read and recite the Quran by heart. At that time a kuttab was how most children in Dubai received their schooling, sitting with a teacher and repeating the verses until they knew them. The building was replaced by a mosque in 1960, made slightly smaller to make room for the market around it. The present mosque dates from the 1990s, when it was rebuilt in a style meant to echo the original.

    Notice the roof. Instead of one great dome, it carries many: sources count forty-five small domes and nine larger ones, rising in groups over the prayer hall. Inside, large chandeliers hang from the main dome. The walls are a plain sandy grey, which ties the building to the coral-and-gypsum houses of the old quarter. Above the five columns of the entrance runs a large inscription from the Quran, and the wooden windows are fitted with handmade stained glass.

    The prayer hall holds up to about twelve hundred worshippers. Inside, more than fifty pillars are reported to be shaped like old wind towers, part of the way the hall is kept cool, and tall arched windows let in the light. Beyond the prayer hall lies an open area with the place for ablution, the washing before prayer.

    This is a working mosque, and the call to prayer is broadcast from the minaret five times a day. Friday is the day of the main congregational prayer, when the hall and the streets around it fill. A local charity has run guided visits for non-Muslims on weekday mornings, explaining the prayer, the pulpit where the Friday sermon is given, and the five pillars of Islam; check current times. Please do not enter during prayer times, and dress modestly if you walk around it.

  7. 7

    The textile souq

    Under the wooden roof of the Bur Dubai textile souq. This is stop 7 on the route.

    Read along

    You are in one of the oldest market streets of Bur Dubai, part of the area long known as the Souq Al Kabir, the great souq, which grew up along the Creek between the old town and the water. Before the malls, this was one of Dubai's main bazaars. Here, goods unloaded from dhows went straight into shops, and divers and fishermen spent what the pearling season had brought in.

    The market you see today has been restored in the old style. Look up at the arched wooden roof. It shades the lane, so the market stays walkable even in the heat of the day, and in the evening traditional lamps light the shopfronts. The buildings are sand-coloured, and many shops still close behind wooden gates.

    Shops here sell bolts of cloth, embroidered fabrics, scarves, pashminas and ready-made clothes, along with souvenirs. Look for the bright Indian silks and cottons. Much of this trade has long been run by merchants from the Indian subcontinent. Their ties to Dubai go back to the dhow trade, when boats from India came up the Creek with timber, spices and cloth. One shopkeeper who opened here in 1984 recalls that the souq then sold mostly cotton and silk; today it also sells satins, wools and laces from East Asia. The souq is also a wholesale stop, where traders buy cloth to sell on to markets in other Gulf countries.

    The streets behind the souq are often called Meena Bazaar, a name that comes from an old textile shop rather than any official district.

    Listen as you walk. You may hear Arabic, Hindi, Urdu, Malayalam, Persian and English, sometimes all at once. Bargaining is normal here, and traders will often call out to you; a smile and a polite "no, thank you" is enough.

  8. 8

    The Creek

    On the waterfront at the Bur Dubai abra station. This is stop 8 on the route.

    Read along

    This stretch of water made Dubai. The Creek is a natural saltwater inlet of the Gulf, reaching far inland, and narrowing and widening along its length.

    For generations it was the only harbour the town had. In the 1950s Dubai was a town of perhaps twenty thousand people, in three districts, Bur Dubai, Deira and Shindagha, all facing this water. The pearling fleet returned here from the summer diving season, and big two-masted sailing dhows called booms came in from India and East Africa, carrying timber and spices. Pearling was the heart of the local economy until cultured pearls from Japan undercut the trade in the 1930s.

    But the Creek had a problem. By the 1950s silt had built up across its mouth, and larger ships could no longer get in. Captains had to anchor offshore while small boats called lighters ferried cargo to the quay. That cost time and money, and storms damaged the lighters and their cargo, which pushed up insurance rates.

    In the mid-1950s the British firm Sir William Halcrow and Partners was asked to study the problem. Its report advised cutting a new channel from the bend of the Creek out to sea, more than nine hundred metres long, and building a stone groyne and walls to stop the sand coming back and protect the banks from erosion. One history records that merchants bought what were called Creek bonds to pay for the survey. Sheikh Rashid, who became ruler in 1958, raised the rest through the merchants, customs revenue and loans, the largest of them from Kuwait. An Austrian dredging company, Overseas AST, began work in 1959, and the first phase was finished by the early 1960s. The sand dug out of the channel was used to raise the low ground behind Shindagha.

    Before the work, boats of around two or three hundred tonnes could anchor in the Creek; afterwards, ships of around five hundred tonnes could reach the quay. Engineers later had to come back and do more, but the gamble paid off. Dubai overtook Sharjah as the leading trading port of the coast, and the Creek set the town on the path to the city you see today.

  9. 9

    Across by abra

    On an abra crossing the Creek. This is stop 9 on the route.

    Read along

    The little wooden boat you are sitting on is an abra, a water taxi that has carried people between the two halves of Dubai for generations. The name is linked to the Arabic verb meaning "to cross", and that is all it does, back and forth, every few minutes.

    On one side is Bur Dubai, where the Al Maktoum family settled and from where it has ruled Dubai since the nineteenth century. On the other is Deira, the old market side. Until 1963, when the first bridge, the Al Maktoum Bridge, opened further down the Creek, a boat was the only way across.

    Look at the boat itself. Abras are still built by hand from wood. The newest are made of African teak, about ten and a half metres long and about three metres wide, and carry up to about twenty passengers on the bench that runs down the middle, sitting back to back and facing the water. They run on diesel engines; today's boats carry engines of around seventy-eight horsepower, where older ones had about thirty. In 2020 the fleet was updated with life jackets under the seats, space for wheelchairs, GPS and card payment, but the shape has barely changed. About a hundred and fifty boats work the crossings.

    As you cross, look along the Deira bank. You will often see wooden dhows moored side by side, loading cargo stacked on the quay: tyres, fridges, sacks of rice, crates of every kind. Small trading ships still sail from here to ports around the Gulf, Iran, East Africa and the Indian subcontinent, though the big container trade moved long ago to Port Rashid and then to the deep-water port at Jebel Ali.

  10. 10

    The Deira spice souq

    This stop introduces The Deira spice souq.

    Read along

    Breathe in. The lanes of the spice souq are lined with open sacks: dried limes, cardamom, saffron, cinnamon bark, frankincense, rose buds, chillies and heaps of coloured powders. Many of these goods arrive by the same dhow routes you have just watched on the Creek, from Iran, India and beyond.

    Spice trading on this side of the Creek goes back a long way; accounts trace a spice market here to at least the nineteenth century. Yet for a long time it stayed small. Two traders who arrived in 1967 both recall finding only about six shops selling spices and herbs, in a town still without mains electricity or running water. Some sellers brought their spices on bicycles and sold them at the kerb. The trade grew fast after the United Arab Emirates was founded in 1971, as merchants from Iran, India, Afghanistan and elsewhere settled here, drawn by low customs duties. Today traders count around two hundred or more shops in and around these lanes, and the market supplies buyers far beyond the city.

    Saffron is the most precious thing here. It is the dried stigma of a crocus flower, picked by hand, and traders say most of what is sold in this souq comes from Iran.

    Dried black lime, called loomi, is a classic of Gulf cooking, used to give a sour depth to rice and stews. Frankincense, the hardened resin of a tree that grows in southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa, has been traded across this region for thousands of years. It is still burned in homes to scent rooms and clothes, often alongside oud, a fragrant wood.

    The souq is a working market, not a museum. Many shops here have been run by the same families for decades, some starting out as porters before opening stalls of their own. Bargaining is expected.

  11. 11

    The gold souq

    In the main covered lane of the Deira Gold Souq. This is stop 11 on the route.

    Read along

    Look along the windows. Necklaces, bangles, earrings and wedding sets glitter on every side, in yellow, white and rose gold. This lane and the alleys around it are packed with jewellery shops, and together they make the Deira Gold Souq one of the best-known gold markets in the world.

    It began in the early twentieth century, when a handful of merchants set up shop near the Creek, drawn by Dubai's low taxes and open trade. The dhows that worked the Creek carried gold in and out along with everything else. One trader's son remembers the market, in his childhood, as a few rickety stalls along the water. Accounts describe how, around the middle of the century, merchants from Iran and India came in growing numbers, and the market grew with them. Thousands of Indian goldsmiths, traders and craftsmen followed in the later twentieth century.

    Today the souq has hundreds of shops; counts range from more than three hundred retailers to more than four hundred shops, depending on whether wholesalers are included. Look up at the roof. The main lanes are covered by a wooden canopy that shades the shopfronts. Dubai itself has become a major centre of the world's gold trade, and this street is its shop window.

    Gold here is sold mostly by weight, with a charge added for the workmanship, and its purity is marked in carats: 18, 21, 22 and 24. In the Gulf, gold has long been an important part of a bride's wedding gifts and many of the heavy traditional pieces you see are made for weddings.

    Look out for the souq's record-breaker: a gold ring called Najmat Taiba, the Star of Taiba. It weighs about sixty-four kilograms, made of 21-carat gold set with precious stones, and was completed in 2000. It is far too large to wear, and has been shown in one of the shop windows here.
