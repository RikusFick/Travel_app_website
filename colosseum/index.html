    Read along

    You're now at the Colosseum. Before you look up at one of the most famous ruins in the world, look down. The low square of old stone near your feet is the base of a giant, and that giant probably gave the Colosseum its name.

    Here's the story. In the year 64, a great fire swept through Rome. Afterwards the emperor Nero took a huge slice of the burned city for himself and built a pleasure palace, the Domus Aurea, the Golden House. Where the Colosseum stands now, he had an artificial lake dug, and near the entrance to his palace stood an enormous bronze statue: the Colossus.

    Nero's reign ended badly, and the new emperor, Vespasian, made a clever political move. He drained Nero's private lake and built on it a vast amphitheatre for the people of Rome. The message was clear: what the tyrant took for himself, I give back to you.

    The statue survived. About sixty years later the emperor Hadrian wanted its spot for a new temple, so he had the Colossus moved here, standing upright the whole way. According to the ancient , the job needed twenty-four elephants. Hadrian also had Nero's features removed and dedicated the statue to the Sun god.

    In Roman times the building behind you was simply called "the amphitheatre". Only in the Middle Ages, long after the statue was gone, did people start calling it the Colosseum. Most scholars think the name came from its giant neighbour; others think it simply describes the building's colossal size.

    The name "Flavian Amphitheatre", which you'll often see, isn't ancient either. Scholars invented it later, after the family name of Vespasian and his sons, the Flavians.
