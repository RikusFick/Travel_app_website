What to do in Paris

This page is in English.

The Eiffel Tower, the Louvre, Notre-Dame and the Sainte-Chapelle, the Musée d’Orsay, Montmartre, the Arc de Triomphe, and a full day at Versailles.

These are the places the Earwise guides cover in Paris, from the Eiffel Tower and the Louvre to Notre-Dame and Montmartre, plus a full day at Versailles.

The Eiffel Tower

The tower was built for the World’s Fair of 1889, which marked one hundred years since the French Revolution, and it was the tallest structure built by people anywhere until 1930. Start on the Trocadéro terrace across the river for the classic view, then cross the Pont d’Iéna to the foot of the tower. That is the best place to see how it is made: about eighteen thousand iron parts joined by some two and a half million rivets. The first floor, fifty-seven metres up, has a glass floor; the second, at a hundred and fifteen metres, gives what many visitors find the best view of Paris. Not everyone loved it at first: in 1887 a group of artists and writers, among them Guy de Maupassant, signed a protest against this “useless and monstrous” tower. Allow two to three hours.

The Louvre

I. M. Pei’s glass and steel pyramid opened in 1989 as the museum’s new main entrance, and many Parisians hated the idea at first. Below ground you can still see the moat and the base of the walls of the first Louvre, a fortress built by King Philip Augustus around 1190. Upstairs, the Earwise route takes in the Venus de Milo, the Winged Victory of Samothrace, the Mona Lisa, Veronese’s Wedding Feast at Cana, the largest painting in the museum, and Géricault’s Raft of the Medusa. Plan on three to four hours.

Notre-Dame and the Sainte-Chapelle

The Île de la Cité is where Paris began. On the evening of 15 April 2019, fire swept through the roof of Notre-Dame; over the following five years, craftspeople rebuilt the oak roof and a new spire to the same design, using traditional methods. In the square out front, a small bronze star marks Point Zero, the spot from which road distances from Paris are measured. Above the central doorway, carved in the early thirteenth century as a sermon in stone for people who could not read, Saint Michael weighs souls at the Last Judgement.

A short walk away, King Louis IX built the Sainte-Chapelle inside his palace as a giant reliquary for the Crown of Thorns, which he bought in 1239. The upper chapel is almost a cage of glass: fifteen windows about fifteen metres high, showing 1,113 scenes from the Bible, and about two-thirds of the glass is original thirteenth-century work. A visit takes about forty-five minutes.

The Musée d’Orsay

Across the river from the Louvre, the Musée d’Orsay fills a railway station that opened on 14 July 1900, in time for the World’s Fair. Its frame contains more metal than the iron of the Eiffel Tower, and the great clock still keeps time above the main hall. The museum holds art from about 1848 to 1914, and the guide visits Millet’s Angelus, Manet’s Luncheon on the Grass, Monet, Renoir, Degas, Cézanne, Van Gogh and Gauguin. Give it two to three hours.

Sacré-Cœur and Montmartre

Sacré-Cœur crowns Montmartre, the highest natural point in Paris. Work began in 1875 and was finished in 1914, but the basilica was only consecrated in 1919, after the First World War. Its stone comes from Château-Landon, south of Paris, and when it rains it releases a mineral that keeps the walls white. The guide then carries on through the old village: Saint-Pierre de Montmartre, one of the oldest churches in Paris, Place du Tertre, where painters have set up their easels for generations, the Clos Montmartre vineyard, the Moulin de la Galette and the Bateau-Lavoir.

The Arc de Triomphe

Napoleon ordered the arch after his victory at Austerlitz in December 1805, but it was only inaugurated in 1836, long after his fall. Beneath it lies the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, a French soldier of the First World War chosen at Verdun to stand for all who died. Two hundred and eighty-four steps climb to the roof terrace, which looks down on the twelve avenues of the Étoile and straight along the Champs-Élysées towards the Louvre. Allow about an hour.

A full day: Versailles

Louis XIV’s palace needs a day of its own. The Hall of Mirrors stretches about seventy-three metres, with three hundred and fifty-seven mirrors facing seventeen windows. In the King’s Bedchamber, at the exact centre of the palace, the king’s rising and going to bed were public ceremonies watched by the court. Outside, André Le Nôtre’s gardens are laid out along the path of the sun, from the palace in the east to the Grand Canal in the west, and further on are the Trianons and the Queen’s Hamlet, a make-believe village built for Marie-Antoinette. The Earwise guide is written for a full-day visit.
