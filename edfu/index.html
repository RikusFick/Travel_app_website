    The birth house

    In front of the low ruined temple on the open ground before the great gateway, the birth house. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are looking at a small temple built for a baby god. The great temple behind it belonged to Horus, the falcon god of Edfu. His divine wife was Hathor of Dendera, and their child was Harsomtus. This little temple is a mammisi, or birth house.

    Yale University's Tell Edfu Project explains the idea. Each year, the birth and crowning of the divine child were acted out here. By extension, the festival also renewed the power of the king. Singers and dancers performed in the open courtyard, where ordinary people could watch.

    The temple is Ptolemaic. Its inscriptions name Ptolemy the Eighth and his son Ptolemy the Ninth.

    Look at the columns. They are shaped like bundles of papyrus, marsh plants and flowers. The project links them to the myth of Isis, who gave birth to Horus and hid him in the marshes of the Nile Delta. At the top of each column is a carved figure of Bes. He was the god who protected mothers and newborn babies.

    The building faces east, because the divine child was linked to the rising sun. Walls of the inner sanctuary were once covered with sheets of gold, to catch the light. Only scant traces of that gold have been found, on the western wall.

    The project counts about two hundred ritual scenes in this small building. The most important show the child god being born, fed and crowned. Protective gods and spells cover the walls, to keep mother and child safe.
