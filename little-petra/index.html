    Read along

    Welcome to Siq al-Barid, the cold canyon. Step into the shade of these high sandstone walls and you'll feel why it got its name: the canyon's direction and its tall sides keep most of the sun out.

    Visitors call it Little Petra, and the reason is clear. Like the great city six kilometres to the south, it is entered through a narrow cleft in the rock. Here too the Nabataeans, the Arab people who grew rich on the caravan trade two thousand years ago, carved halls and chambers straight into the sandstone cliffs. Yet this complex is far smaller, with open courtyards linked by a tight gorge. It belongs to Petra's World Heritage listing and the Petra Archaeological Park.

    Before you step through the cleft, look to the right for Tomb 846, and notice the narrow entry itself, where a hinged door once closed off the canyon. Tomb 846 carries classical Nabataean capitals and a Greek-style frieze under a flat arch topped by an urn, yet its inner chamber was left unfinished and never held a burial. Archaeologists view Siq al-Barid as a northern trading suburb of Petra, built around the first century when Nabataean power was at its height. Its many dining halls suggest a station for hospitality, where merchants welcomed arriving caravans. Watch for channels and cisterns cut into the rock: every drop of rain had to be saved, and as late as 1929 caravans of three hundred camels watered at a large Nabataean cistern nearby.
