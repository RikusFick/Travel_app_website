    This vast half-circle of brick, tile and marble is the Plaza de España, the largest building of the Ibero-American Exposition, the world's fair that Seville hosted from May 1929 to June 1930.

    The architect Aníbal González began it in 1914, mixing Renaissance, Baroque and Neo-Mudéjar, the Moorish-inspired style of Spain, with plenty of Seville's glazed ceramics. The square covers about fifty thousand square metres and opens west towards María Luisa Park. Its plan has been compared to the villas of the Renaissance architect Andrea Palladio.

    Building it took fifteen years. Builders' strikes and the soaring cost of materials slowed the work, the budget grew from about six hundred thousand pesetas to more than seventeen million, and in 1926 González resigned and left the project, by then settled in all its main lines. The two towers at the ends, seventy metres tall, were opposed by Seville's Academy of Fine Arts, which feared they would rival the Giralda. On 9 May 1929 King Alfonso XIII opened the Exposition here.

    The square's later history is darker. In 1936, at the start of the Civil War, General Queipo de Llano turned the whole complex into state offices, and its basements served as prisons. Today the central building houses an army headquarters, the towers hold offices of Spain's central government, and since 1992 one gallery has been the entrance to Seville's Military History Museum.

    The fountain in the centre is by Vicente Traver, who took over as the Exposition's chief architect. Film-makers love the square: it appears in Lawrence of Arabia, and in Star Wars: Episode II, as a palace on the planet Naboo.
