A free ten-stop walk through Florence's historic streets from Piazza della Signoria across the Ponte Vecchio to Santa Croce. links Medici power, medieval bridges, guild churches, and Italy's pantheon of scholars. Listen here, or get the Earwise app to play as you walk.

  1. 1

    Fire in the square

    Begin in Piazza della Signoria beside Ammanati's Neptune fountain, political stage of the Florentine Republic.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You're now in Piazza della Signoria, the political heart of Florence for over seven hundred years.

    The giant white figure in the fountain is Neptune, god of the sea. Duke Cosimo I de' Medici commissioned the fountain in 1559 to celebrate bringing clean water to the city. It was carved by Bartolomeo Ammannati, and Neptune's face is said to be modelled on Cosimo himself.

    Now look at the paving in front of the fountain for a round marble plaque.

    In 1497, the fiery friar Girolamo Savonarola, who had turned Florence against luxury and the Medici, held his "Bonfire of the Vanities" in this square, burning books, fine clothes and works of art. A year later, Florence turned on him. On the twenty-third of May 1498, he was hanged and burned on this very spot.

    The fountain took about fifteen years to finish, from 1559 into the mid-1570s, and several sculptors worked on it, including the young Giambologna.

  2. 2

    The Old Palace

    Look at the fortress-like Palazzo Vecchio and its slender Arnolfo tower, Florence's seat of civic government for seven centuries.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This is the Palazzo Vecchio, the Old Palace, and for over seven centuries the seat of Florence's government. It's still the town hall today.

    In 1299, the city decided to build a palace strong enough to protect its magistrates. The architect was Arnolfo di Cambio, who also began the cathedral. It was deliberately built on the ruins of the houses of the Uberti, a family of exiled rebels, so they could never return.

    Look up at the battlements. Some of the openings beneath them were for dropping rocks or hot liquids on attackers. This was a town hall built like a castle.

    The tower is ninety-four metres tall. Notice it's off-centre: it was built on top of an older tower that stood here before.

    At the door stands a marble David. This is a copy. Michelangelo's original stood on this spot from 1504 until 1873, when it was moved to the Accademia to protect it.

    The palace has had many names. It became the "Old" Palace when the Medici dukes moved across the river to their new home, the Palazzo Pitti.

  3. 3

    The Loggia and Perseus

    Inspect the open-air Loggia dei Lanzi, housing Benvenuto Cellini's bronze Perseus and Giambologna's marble groups.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You're now looking at the Loggia dei Lanzi. This great open arcade was built between 1376 and 1382 for public ceremonies, such as swearing in the city's leaders. Its name comes later, from the German mercenary guards, the Landsknechts, or "lanzi", whom Duke Cosimo stationed here.

    Today it's an open-air sculpture gallery. Look for the bronze figure holding up a severed head: Perseus, with the head of Medusa. It was made by Benvenuto Cellini and unveiled in 1554.

    In the myth, Medusa's gaze turned men to stone. And look around: she faces a square full of marble men, David, Hercules and Neptune.

    Further back is Giambologna's spiralling marble group, the Rape of the Sabine Women.

    Walk round to the back of Perseus and look at his helmet: Cellini hid a self-portrait there.

  4. 4

    Through the Uffizi

    Walk down the long Uffizi courtyard framed by Vasari's sixteenth-century Medici government offices.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You're walking through the courtyard of the Uffizi, built as offices for the Medici state, and now one of the world's great art galleries.

    Somewhere above you runs part of the Vasari Corridor, a private passage about a kilometre long. Duke Cosimo had it built in 1565, in just five months, so he could walk from the Palazzo Vecchio to his home at the Palazzo Pitti, across the river, without mixing with the public.

    The corridor was built in time for a Medici wedding: Cosimo's son Francesco married Johanna of Austria in 1565.

  5. 5

    The Old Bridge

    Cross the Ponte Vecchio, the medieval stone bridge lined with goldsmiths' shops and crowned by Vasari's hidden corridor.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This is the Ponte Vecchio, the Old Bridge, at the narrowest point of the Arno. There's probably been a crossing here since Roman times.

    Floods have swept bridges away here again and again. After a great flood in 1333, the bridge was rebuilt in stone, in 1345, and that's the bridge you're standing on.

    It stands on three very shallow arches of pietraforte, Florence's brown sandstone, carried by two piers in the river with pointed prows to split the current. The shops that line it were later extended out over the water on slanting wooden brackets.

    For a long time, the shops were butchers'. In 1593, to clean up the bridge below the Medici corridor, Grand Duke Ferdinando I banned them, and since then the bridge has belonged to goldsmiths and jewellers.

    Look up above the shops on one side: that's the Vasari Corridor, crossing the river.

    Now look along the river at the other bridges. Most of them were rebuilt after the Second World War.

    On the fourth of August 1944, as the German army retreated from Florence, it destroyed every bridge across the Arno except this one. Local tradition says Hitler himself ordered the Ponte Vecchio spared. Whatever the reason, it's the only bridge in the city that survived.

    In the middle of the bridge stands a bronze bust of Benvenuto Cellini, the sculptor of Perseus, who was also Florence's most famous goldsmith.

  6. 6

    The New Market and the Porcellino

    Stop under the Market Hall of the Mercato Nuovo beside Pietro Tacca's bronze boar fountain, Il Porcellino.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You're now under the Loggia del Mercato Nuovo, the New Market, and in front of the bronze boar that Florentines call the Porcellino, the little pig.

    Duke Cosimo I had this open loggia built between 1547 and 1551, to a design by Giovanni Battista del Tasso. It was made for the city's richest trades, silk and fine goods, at the heart of the banking and merchant district.

    The boar was cast by Pietro Tacca in 1633, after an ancient marble boar in the Medici collection. The one in front of you is a modern copy; Tacca's original is in the Bardini Museum across the river. Look at its snout, polished gold by countless hands. Tradition says that if you rub it, you will return to Florence, and if a coin you drop from its mouth falls through the grate, you'll have good luck.

  7. 7

    Piazza della Repubblica

    Reach Piazza della Repubblica, cleared in the nineteenth century over the old Roman forum and medieval market quarter.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You're now in Piazza della Repubblica, the big square with the column and the triumphal arch. This was the centre of Roman Florence: the forum stood here, and the column marks the spot where the town's two main Roman streets crossed.

    For centuries afterwards this was the Old Market, a maze of stalls, towers, churches and the walled Jewish ghetto. Then, between 1885 and 1895, the city demolished the whole district to build this square, with wide streets, cafés and grand offices.

    Look up at the arch. Its inscription proudly says that the ancient centre of the city was restored from age-old squalor to new life. Many Florentines at the time saw it as a terrible loss of their medieval heart.

    The column is the Column of Abundance, crowned by a statue of Abundance. It was taken down during the demolition and set back up in 1956.

  8. 8

    Orsanmichele

    Circle Orsanmichele, a former grain market turned guild church whose exterior niches hold Renaissance master sculptures.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You're now in front of Orsanmichele, the tall square building with statues in niches all the way around. It doesn't look like a church, because it wasn't built as one.

    It went up from 1337 as the city's grain market, with an open loggia at street level and storerooms for grain above. Later the arches were walled in and the ground floor became a church.

    The city gave each niche outside to one of the powerful guilds, which paid for a statue of its patron saint. So the guilds competed, hiring the best sculptors of the day. Ghiberti's bronze Saint John the Baptist, for the cloth merchants, was one of the first large bronze statues of the Renaissance. Donatello carved the marble Saint George for the armourers.

    The statues in the niches now are copies. Most originals are kept in the museum upstairs, and Donatello's Saint George is in the Bargello museum.

  9. 9

    Piazza Santa Croce

    Walk into broad Piazza Santa Croce, long used for medieval tournaments, festivals, and calcio storico.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You're now in Piazza Santa Croce, facing the church of the Holy Cross. This great square was once marshland outside the city walls.

    The church in front of you is the largest Franciscan church in the world. Work began in 1294, possibly under Arnolfo di Cambio, the same architect as the Palazzo Vecchio. It was consecrated in the 1440s.

    But the façade isn't medieval. This marble front was only added between 1857 and 1863, in neo-Gothic style. Its architect, Niccolò Matas from Ancona, came from a Jewish family, and a large six-pointed star crowns the design. It's often linked to his faith, though the star is also an old Christian symbol. Matas lies buried under the threshold, honoured with an inscription. The bell tower is younger still: it went up in the 1840s, more than three centuries after the old one collapsed in a storm.

    Beside the steps stands a marble statue of Dante by Enrico Pazzi.

    The square has long been one of the city's great gathering places. Every June it's covered in sand for Calcio Fiorentino, the historic football game, so rough that boxing and wrestling are allowed.

    Tradition holds that the first church here was founded by Saint Francis himself.

  10. 10

    The Temple of Italian Glories

    End before the white-and-green marble façade of Santa Croce, burial church of Michelangelo, Galileo, and Machiavelli.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Behind this façade is Santa Croce, a church simple and vast, as the Franciscans wanted. But its walls hold some of the greatest names in Italian history, which is why it's called the Temple of the Italian Glories.

    In 1966, the Arno burst its banks and flooded Santa Croce with mud and heating oil.
