  1. 1

    The mountain of Moses

    At the entrance to the summit sanctuary, by the memorial stone. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Welcome to Mount Nebo. You are on a ridge of the Abarim mountains, about seven hundred metres above sea level, at the very edge of the high plateau of Moab. Ahead of you, the land falls away more than a kilometre to the valley of the Jordan and the Dead Sea, far below sea level.

    This is one of the great places of the Bible. At the end of the Book of Deuteronomy, after forty years in the wilderness, the people of Israel are camped on the plains of Moab below. God tells Moses, "Go up into these mountains of the Abarim, to Mount Nebo, which is in the land of Moab, opposite Jericho." He tells him to view the land of Canaan, given to Israel (Deuteronomy 32:49).

    And so Moses climbed. Deuteronomy 34 tells us he went up from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, which is opposite Jericho. There, the Lord showed him the whole land. It was here that the man who had led a people out of slavery saw the promise fulfilled, though he himself would not cross over.

    For Jews, Christians and Muslims alike, Moses is among the greatest of the prophets. The peak where you stand is called Siyagha, and since at least the fourth century pilgrims have climbed it to remember his last day. The church ahead keeps that memory.

  2. 2

    A church for Moses

    In front of the Memorial basilica, the modern stone church built over the ancient one. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The building in front of you looks modern, and it is: a new church, finished in 2016, built to shelter something very old. Beneath this roof lie the remains of a Byzantine church first raised in the second half of the fourth century to commemorate the place of Moses' death.

    We have an early eyewitness. A pilgrim woman named Egeria, travelling from the far west of the Roman world, climbed this mountain in the late fourth century. One account dates her visit to around the year 394, and her journal is the first written mention of a church on this summit.

    The first church was small, a chapel with three apses in the shape of a clover leaf. Over the next two centuries it grew into a full basilica, with side chapels, a baptistery, and a monastery all around it. The excavation reports date the rebuild to the year 597. The Franciscans who care for it say the monastic complex was lived in until the ninth century.

    Then came centuries of silence, until 1933. That year, on the thirteenth of July, friars of the Custody of the Holy Land settled here, having bought the land from local Bedouin. Their archaeologists, starting with Father Sylvester Saller and Father Jerome Mihaic, began to dig, and between 1933 and 1938 they uncovered the basilica, its chapels and the monastery. The Franciscans have kept a small community here ever since.

  3. 3

    The great hunting mosaic

    Inside the basilica, on the left as you enter, before the large mosaic floor of the old baptistery. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Look down at this floor. It is one of the finest Byzantine mosaics in Jordan, and it was hidden for most of its life. In 1976, Father Michele Piccirillo, the Franciscan archaeologist, lifted a later, plainer mosaic floor in this hall and found this one lying underneath, its colours almost untouched.

    A Greek inscription tells you when it was made: in the year 530 or 531, depending on how the date is read. It even gives the names of the craftsmen, Soelos, Kaiomos and Elias, and of the bishop of the time, also called Elias.

    Read the floor in bands, from one end to the other. In the first, a shepherd fights off a lion, and a soldier in a pointed cap spears a lioness. In the next, two hunters on horseback, with their dogs, attack a bear and a wild boar. Then the scene grows calm: a shepherd sits on a rock, watching his goats and fat-tailed sheep beneath fruit trees. In the last band, a man leads an ostrich on a rope, and another holds the bridle of a zebra, and a camel whose spotted coat makes it look like a leopard. One writer suggests it may be the artist's idea of a giraffe.

    The room was called the diakonikon, where the clergy kept their vestments and books, and it also served for baptism. At its eastern end, the excavators found a cross-shaped font, where new Christians went down into the water.

  4. 4

    Floors, tombs and those who rest here

    In the nave and presbytery of the basilica. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    All around you are pieces of floors from different centuries. The excavation reports note that the earliest is a panel with a braided cross, set on the south wall near the eastern end. Others show flowers, animals and geometric patterns, the work of generations of craftsmen from the workshops of nearby Madaba.

    According to one account of the excavations, beneath the old mosaic-covered floor of the church lay six tombs cut into the natural rock. The monks of Nebo chose to be buried on the mountain of Moses.

    So did one of its modern guardians. Father Michele Piccirillo, who found the hunting mosaic and gave much of his life to the archaeology of Jordan, died suddenly in October 2008 while the new church was being built. One account says he was laid to rest here, on the mountain. The work went on, and the basilica was reopened on the fifteenth of October 2016.

    When the Franciscan Custos celebrated the community's ninetieth anniversary here in 2023, he recalled an old Jewish story. It says that at the moment of Moses' death on Nebo, God kissed him and took his soul with a kiss. It is a tender image to carry with you as you walk out to the view.

  5. 5

    The serpent and the cross

    On the terrace outside the basilica, beside the tall bronze sculpture. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This bronze sculpture joins two images into one. It is a cross, with a serpent winding around it, made by the Italian artist Gian Paolo Fantoni.

    The serpent comes from the Book of Numbers. During the wandering in the desert, the people spoke against God and against Moses, and venomous serpents came among them. When they repented, the Lord told Moses to make a serpent and set it on a pole. Moses made a serpent of bronze, "and if a serpent bit anyone, he would look at the bronze serpent and live" (Numbers 21:9).

    Centuries later, Jesus himself took up that image. In the Gospel of John, Jesus says: "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up." He adds that whoever believes in him may have eternal life (John 3:14–15). The sculpture holds the two moments together, the healing in the wilderness and the cross.

    Nearby stands an olive tree with its own story. According to one account, Pope John Paul the Second came to this summit on the twentieth of March 2000 during his Holy Land pilgrimage. Beside the old chapel, he planted an olive tree as a sign of peace. Pope Benedict the Sixteenth followed in 2009, and, one account says, looked out from the summit towards Jerusalem.

  6. 6

    What Moses saw

    At the edge of the viewing platform, facing west over the valley. This is stop 6 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Now stand where Moses stood, and look. Below you, the land drops away to the Jordan valley. The green line winding through it is the river. To your left, the Dead Sea shines. Straight ahead, on the far side of the valley, is the oasis of Jericho, which you can usually make out from here. On a very clear day, the hills of Jerusalem appear on the horizon.

    Hear how Deuteronomy describes this moment: "And the Lord showed him all the land, Gilead as far as Dan... and the Valley of Jericho the city of palm trees, as far as Zoar" (Deuteronomy 34:1–3).

    Then the Lord spoke of the land he had sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob to give to their offspring. He told Moses, "I have let you see it with your eyes, but you shall not go over there" (Deuteronomy 34:4).

    So Moses, the servant of the Lord, died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the Lord. He was a hundred and twenty years old, and Scripture says his eye was undimmed and his vigour unabated. He was buried in the valley in the land of Moab, opposite Beth-peor, and, in the Bible's own words, "no one knows the place of his burial to this day" (Deuteronomy 34:5–7). The people wept for him on the plains below for thirty days.

    Deuteronomy closes with his epitaph: "There has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face" (Deuteronomy 34:10). Then Joshua led the people down into that valley and across the river you can see.

    Stay a moment here. Look at the land as Moses did, a whole life's journey laid out before you, and the promise kept.
