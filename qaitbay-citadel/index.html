    The island of Pharos and its lighthouse

    On the approach road or forecourt south of the fort's walls, with the Eastern Harbour on one side. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This fort stands at the eastern tip of what was once an island. Its name was Pharos.

    Egypt's coast is low and flat. Sailors had few landmarks, and sandbanks lay in wait. For Homer, Pharos was the one good harbour on the long way to Egypt.

    In the Odyssey, the Greek king Menelaus was stranded here on his way home from Troy. The island was ruled by Proteus, the shape-shifting Old Man of the Sea.

    The early Ptolemies joined the island to the city with a long causeway. It was called the Heptastadion, because it was seven Greek stades long, about three-quarters of a mile.

    At the island's eastern tip they raised the most famous lighthouse of the ancient world. It took the island's name: the Pharos.

    Its builder was Sostratus of Cnidus. Ancient writers give different dates. A Byzantine encyclopaedia, the Suda, says 297 BC. Jerome puts it in 284 or 283 BC. Other accounts say it was finished around 280 BC, under Ptolemy the Second.

    Estimates put it at more than a hundred and ten metres high. Only the pyramids of Giza would have been taller.

    Ancient sources describe three stages, each leaning slightly inward. The lowest was square, the middle eight-sided, and the top round. A wide ramp wound up to the summit. There, after dark, a fire was kept burning.

    Some accounts say a giant statue crowned it, perhaps Alexander or Ptolemy the First as the sun god.

    The Greek geographer Strabo describes a tower of white marble with many storeys. Its inscription named Sostratus, for the safety of sailors.

    It became the model for every lighthouse since. Yet it only joined the lists of the Seven Wonders in the sixth century AD.
