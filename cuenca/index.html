Explore a cliff-top medieval town balanced on a limestone spur between the Júcar and Huécar river gorges. Five stops follow the cobbled spine from Plaza Mayor and Spain's earliest Gothic cathedral out onto the iron footbridge facing the Hanging Houses.

    Plaza Mayor of Cuenca

    Start in the sloping pastel square at the summit of Cuenca's narrow rocky ridge.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    You are in the Plaza Mayor of Cuenca, at the heart of a town built on a narrow rocky ridge between two deep gorges, carved by the rivers Júcar and Huécar.

    That ridge made Cuenca a natural fortress. Under Muslim rule it was a strong town, and Alfonso VIII of Castile only took it on 21 September 1177, after a siege of nine months. A diocese was created soon after, and work on the cathedral followed.

    The square closes at the far end with the town hall, an eighteenth-century building standing on three arches, which the street passes straight through. Around you, the houses are painted in strong colours, and on the gorge side some of them are many storeys high at the back, clinging to the cliff.

    The town hall was built to a design by the architect Jaime Bort. The plot was small and steep, so a second plot was bought to make the front symmetrical. Its façade is of finely cut stone in three storeys. Below are three arches of different widths, a round one in the centre and slightly pointed ones at the sides, and the pilasters change from Tuscan to Ionic to Corinthian as they rise. An inscription gives the year 1762, in the reign of Charles III, whose coat of arms is carved high on the front beneath a crowned lion.

    UNESCO listed the walled town of Cuenca as a World Heritage Site in 1996, as a medieval fortified city that has kept its setting almost intact.

  2. 2

    Cuenca Cathedral

    Step into the Anglo-Norman Gothic nave of Cuenca Cathedral, begun in the late twelfth century.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Around you is Cuenca Cathedral, one of the earliest Gothic cathedrals in Spain, begun in the late twelfth century and largely finished by 1257.

    It was built at a time when most Spanish churches were still Romanesque. Its style is close to the Gothic of northern France and Anglo-Norman England, with cathedrals such as Laon and Soissons as its relatives. One reason may be Alfonso VIII's queen, Eleanor of England, a daughter of Henry II, whose court had close links with those lands. It is a large church, about 120 metres long, with its central nave rising some 36 metres.

    In all it covers some 10,000 square metres. Its stone ceilings show those northern roots: sexpartite rib vaults, each bay divided by its ribs into six sections. In the fifteenth century the east end was rebuilt with a double ambulatory, two walkways curving around the high altar. In the eighteenth century the architect Ventura Rodríguez added the Transparente, an altar lit from behind through glass, made to rival the famous one in Toledo Cathedral.

    Most of the stone came from the quarries of Arcos de la Cantera, a few kilometres away, a pale limestone formed in ancient lakes. The nave is covered entirely by sexpartite vaults, and the cathedral is described as the only one in Spain vaulted this way throughout. Studies of the vaults show at least four different versions of the pattern, built with arches of different curves, which gave the builders freedom to fit the vaults to bays of different shapes. The ribs were raised first on timber centring, and the thin stone panels between them were then laid over the ribs.

    The cathedral is dedicated to the Virgin Mary and to Saint Julian, Cuenca's second bishop and patron saint.

    The façade looks oddly unfinished. In 1902 the bell tower, known as the Giraldo, collapsed and wrecked the front. It was rebuilt in a neo-Gothic style in 1910, but the towers were never completed.

    Later, new stained-glass windows were made. Four artists linked to Cuenca designed them in an abstract style: Gustavo Torner, Gerardo Rueda, Bonifacio Alfonso and the French master glazier Henri Dechanet. The cathedral chapter set the theme, the story of salvation in three parts, Creation, Redemption and Glorification. The artists drew the designs and oversaw how they were turned into glass, and the windows were made in Cuenca by a glaziers' cooperative led by Dechanet, using glass chosen from the Saint-Just factory in France.

  3. 3

    The Hanging Houses

    View the surviving Casas Colgadas whose wooden balconies overhang the sheer drop of the Huécar gorge.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    In front of you are the Hanging Houses, the Casas Colgadas, Cuenca's most famous sight. Their wooden balconies jut out over the cliff above the Huécar gorge.

    Houses like these once lined much of the eastern edge of the old town, using every centimetre of the narrow ridge. Today only a few survive, the best known a group of three. Their exact origin is uncertain, but they are documented in the fifteenth century, and they have been rebuilt several times, the last time in the 1920s.

    The oldest known image of them is a panorama of Cuenca drawn in 1565 by the Flemish artist Anton van den Wyngaerde, which shows a whole row of houses hanging over the gorge. Three survive today: the House of the Mermaid and the two Houses of the King. Despite the royal name, they were ordinary homes rather than palaces, a way to gain living space on a cramped clifftop, and a document from 1481 already mentions them. In the 1920s the town council bought them and restored them.

    Over the years they have been homes, council buildings and a tavern. Since 1966 one of them has held the Museum of Spanish Abstract Art, created by the artist Fernando Zóbel. It gave Cuenca an important place in modern Spanish art, as painters of the abstract generation settled in the town.

    The way they were built explains how they can hang over the drop. The lowest floors and the foundations are of strong rubble stone, and the upper walls are a timber frame, usually of juniper, filled in and whitewashed. The balconies rest on beams that run out over the void, carried by the inner walls and anchored in the rock, so the weight of the balcony is balanced by the floor behind it. The houses are narrow and tall, with two or three rooms on each floor and up to three or four storeys, and the cellar was dug into the slope below street level. Repair work found the ends of the beams in the rock rotted by damp, and new steel supports were added from the inside so that the outside would look unchanged.

    San Pablo Bridge

    Cross the high iron-and-plank footbridge linking the old town cliff to the San Pablo Parador.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Under your feet is the San Pablo Bridge, an iron footbridge crossing the gorge to the old convent of San Pablo, now a Parador hotel.

    The first bridge here was built of stone in the sixteenth century, so that the Dominican friars of the convent could reach the town. It was a huge work, with several tall arches, but it was finally pulled down. The present iron bridge, made of iron and wood on the old stone piers, was opened in 1903.

    It is over a hundred metres long and stands high above the river.

    From the middle you get the best view of the Hanging Houses and the whole cliff of the old town, with houses growing out of the rock.

  5. 5

    Torre de Mangana

    Finish at the square clock tower standing on the site of Cuenca's Moorish alcázar.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    In front of you is the Torre de Mangana, a clock tower that stands in the area of the old Muslim fortress of Cuenca and has become one of the symbols of the city.

    Its origins are disputed. It may be a remnant of the Muslim fortifications, or a later tower built in the sixteenth century. What is certain is that it has been altered many times, and its present look is the result of a twentieth-century rebuilding.

    The square around it was, after the Christian conquest, part of the Jewish quarter; excavations here have found remains of earlier buildings.

    From the edge of the square there are wide views over the lower, newer town and the Júcar valley.
