    The Clos Normand is the flower garden in front of the house, laid out by Monet himself in beds full of colour. It covers about one hectare of garden.

    Monet first noticed Giverny from the window of the little train that ran along the valley between Vernon and Gasny. It was then a farming village of only about three hundred people, and the house he found was a plain farmhouse with a walled orchard and kitchen garden in front of it. He moved in on the twenty-ninth of April 1883 with his children, and Alice Hoschedé, who would become his second wife, followed with hers.

    He rented the house first and bought it in 1890. He planned the garden like a painting so that something is in bloom from spring to autumn, and climbing roses grow over the iron arches above the central path.

    The orchard and vegetables gave way to flowers. Monet read horticultural magazines and catalogues, built greenhouses, and as his paintings began to sell he could afford help: at the height of the garden he employed a whole team of gardeners. He remained its designer throughout. The beds are laid out on a straight, regular plan, and he filled them with thousands of flowers, choosing them as a painter chooses colours.

    When Monet died the garden slowly declined, and by the 1970s it was overgrown, many trees were dead and the greenhouses had lost their glass. What you see today is a reconstruction, made from 1977 under Gérald Van der Kemp, who had been chief curator at Versailles, with the young head gardener Gilbert Vahé. They used photographs, archives and the memories of people who had known the garden to replant it as closely as they could.
