    You are at the top of the stairs leading down into the Basilica Cistern, one of the strangest and most beautiful places in Istanbul. Above you are busy streets full of cafés and visitors. Below you, down these fifty-two stone steps, is a cool, dim hall the size of a cathedral, built fifteen hundred years ago to hold water.

    The Turks call it Yerebatan Sarnıcı, the Sunken Cistern, or sometimes Yerebatan Sarayı, the Sunken Palace. Once you're inside, you'll see why: hundreds of marble columns rise out of the water like the halls of an underground palace.

    Its other name, the Basilica Cistern, comes from what once stood above it. The cistern was built in the sixth century under the emperor Justinian, the same emperor who built Hagia Sophia, just a short walk away.

    Why build it at all? A great city needs water. Water was carried by aqueducts from the hills and forests to the north and stored in cisterns like this one. This cistern supplied the Great Palace of the emperors and the buildings around it. Written sources say the cistern was fed by the Hadrian aqueduct line.

    The steps down can be damp.

    This is the largest of the ancient cisterns that lie beneath the streets of Istanbul.
