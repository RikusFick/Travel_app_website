    The Imperial Gate

    Read along

    In front of you is the Imperial Gate of Topkapı Palace, the Bâb-ı Hümâyun. Welcome to Topkapı Palace. For nearly four hundred years, the sultans of the Ottoman Empire ruled from behind this gate. From here they governed a vast empire. Its decisions were made, and its treasures stored, within the walls in front of you.

    Mehmed the Second conquered the city in 1453. A few years later, around 1460, he began building a new palace on the tip of this peninsula. The main buildings were finished within about a decade, and the outer walls with this gate were completed in 1478. He chose a superb spot: a headland where the Bosphorus meets the Sea of Marmara and the Golden Horn. It had once been the acropolis, the high citadel, of ancient Byzantion, the Greek city that came before Constantinople. When work began, the ground was known as Zeytinlik, the Olive Grove.

    At first, people simply called it the New Palace. The name Topkapı, meaning "Cannon Gate", came later, from a pavilion by the sea.

    Look up at the gate. It's a two-storey monument, and the calligraphy above the arch carries the signature of a calligrapher called Ali ibn Yahya al-Sufi. The gate itself goes back to 1478, though the marble facing you see here is from the nineteenth century.

    Here's the most important thing to understand about Topkapı. It isn't one big building, like a European palace. It's a series of courtyards, one inside the next, each more private than the last. Ordinary people could enter the first. Only officials went into the second. Very few went into the third. And beyond that, only the sultan and his household.

    So each gate leads deeper into the private world of the sultan. Beyond this one lies the big, tree-lined courtyard.

    On 3 April 1924, shortly after the founding of the Turkish Republic, Topkapı became a museum, the very first museum created by the new Republic.
