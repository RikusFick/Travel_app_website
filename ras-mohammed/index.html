  1. 1

    The Shark Observatory

    On the clifftop terrace known as the Shark Observatory, on the headland at the southern tip of the Sinai Peninsula. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Stay well back from the edge, and look out. You are on a spur of rock about sixty metres above the water, at the southern tip of Sinai. On one side lies the Gulf of Suez, on the other the Gulf of Aqaba. Here they meet the open Red Sea.

    The name means the Cape of Mohammed. The profile of the rock is said to look like a bearded face.

    Look down at the cliff itself. It is not ordinary stone. It is old coral, raised out of the sea. The oldest of it is said to have formed in the Pleistocene, an age that began some 1.8 million years ago and lasted until about ten thousand years ago. In its submission to UNESCO, Egypt listed the raised terraces, the fossil corals and a great fault among its geological sights.

    Why are sharks in the name? This terrace was a lookout over the deep water below. Sharks are said to be rarely seen from the cliff. Come for the view, not for a sure sighting of sharks.

    The meeting of the gulfs matters most underwater. The peninsula narrows to a point, so every tide drives water hard around it. That stream carries plankton and food past the reefs, the fish gather to feed, and the hunters follow. More than a thousand species of fish live here, and more than two hundred and twenty species of coral.

    People noticed. In September 1975, the photographer David Doubilet and Eugenie Clark, known as the Shark Lady, published a magazine cover story that showed the world these reefs. A diving pioneer, Howard Rosenstein, then pressed the Egyptian authorities to protect them.

    In 1983, Egypt made Ras Mohammed its first national park. It began at about ninety-seven square kilometres. In 1988 it grew to about four hundred and eighty, adding the northern coast up to Sharm El Sheikh, plus the two islands of Tiran and Sanafir. Most of that area is sea. Reportedly, visitors may enter only about a tenth of the park. The rest is kept closed for conservation.

    In 2002, Egypt placed Ras Mohammed on UNESCO's tentative list. That is a first step towards World Heritage status, not an inscription.
