  1. 1

    The obelisk that saw Peter

    Begin beside the ancient Egyptian obelisk at the centre of St Peter's Square, moved here in 1586 by Domenico Fontana with forty windlasses.

    Free sample: stop 1

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This is St Peter's Square. You've just stepped across a border. Everything from here to the basilica is Vatican City, the world's smallest country.

    Look up at the obelisk in front of you. It's a single shaft of red Egyptian granite, about twenty-five metres tall, and it has no hieroglyphs on it at all. The emperor Caligula had it shipped from Egypt, around 37 to 40 AD, to decorate his racetrack, a circus that stood right here on the Vatican hill. That circus was later linked to Nero, and it's where Christians were put to death after the great fire of Rome in the year 64. According to tradition, Saint Peter was among them, crucified nearby, and buried in a simple grave on the hillside. So this stone may have been standing when Peter died. Everything around you was built because of that grave.

    For centuries the obelisk stayed where the Romans put it, beside the old basilica. Then, in 1586, Pope Sixtus the Fifth had it moved to the middle of this square. It took the engineer Domenico Fontana hundreds of men, well over a hundred horses and dozens of winches. The story goes that, at the critical moment, with the ropes close to snapping, a sailor in the crowd shouted, "Water on the ropes!", and saved the day.

    It's the only ancient obelisk in Rome that has never fallen.

    In the Middle Ages, people believed the bronze ball that once sat on top held the ashes of Julius Caesar.
