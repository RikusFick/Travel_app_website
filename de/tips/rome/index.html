Travel tips

What to do in Rome

This page is in English.

The Colosseum and the Forum, the Pantheon, a free walk past Piazza Navona, the Trevi Fountain and the Spanish Steps, the Vatican, Castel Sant’Angelo, the Galleria Borghese, and a day trip to Tivoli.

Rome rewards a first visit that stays close to the ancient centre and the Vatican, then leaves time for one quieter gallery or a day in the hills. These are the places the Earwise guides cover in the city, plus a classic outing to Tivoli.

Start with the Colosseum

The Colosseum stands where Nero once had an artificial lake, in the grounds of his Golden House, after the great fire of the year 64. The new emperor, Vespasian, drained the lake and built a vast amphitheatre for the people of Rome. Work began around the year 70 and was finished by his sons, Titus and Domitian. The north façade still stands almost fifty metres high: three rows of eighty arches, and a fourth storey above them. The oval could seat around fifty thousand people. Just beside it stands the Arch of Constantine, the largest Roman honorary arch to survive anywhere. Set aside about an hour and a half to two hours for the visit.

The Forum and the Palatine

From the Arch of Titus, the old Sacred Way runs downhill through the Roman Forum, the heart of ancient Rome, toward the Capitoline Hill. For more than a thousand years this valley was where Romans voted, worshipped, went to court, shopped and gossiped. Along the way you pass the Temple of the Divine Julius, where Caesar’s body was burned, and the Senate House, later saved because it became a church. The walk then climbs the Palatine Hill, where imperial residences looked back down over the valley. Give the Forum and Palatine about two to three hours.

The Pantheon

A short walk away stands the best-preserved building of ancient Rome. The porch still carries Agrippa’s name, though the Pantheon you enter was rebuilt by Hadrian in the second century, who put Agrippa’s inscription back on the front instead of his own. The dome is made of Roman concrete, without any steel reinforcement, and it is still the largest unreinforced dome in the world. Start in Piazza della Rotonda, at the fountain and the small Egyptian obelisk, then step inside to stand under the opening in the dome, the oculus. The temple later became a church, and Raphael is buried here. A visit of about half an hour to forty-five minutes is enough.

A free walk: Navona, Trevi and the Spanish Steps

Between the Pantheon and the Spanish Steps, a free Earwise walk of about two kilometres links Rome’s great Baroque squares and fountains. Piazza Navona still follows the exact outline of Emperor Domitian’s first-century stadium, which held around 30,000 spectators. The walk continues to Bernini’s Fountain of the Four Rivers, past the Temple of Hadrian and the Column of Marcus Aurelius, then to the Trevi Fountain and up to the Spanish Steps. The Pantheon sits on the way, and has its own guide.

The Vatican

St Peter’s Square is already Vatican City, the world’s smallest country. The red Egyptian obelisk at the centre was shipped to Rome by Caligula and moved into the square in 1586; according to tradition, Saint Peter was buried on this hillside, and the basilica was built because of that grave. Inside are Michelangelo’s Pietà, Bernini’s bronze baldacchino, and an optional climb onto the dome. Next door, the Vatican Museums lead through classical sculptures, the Gallery of Maps and Raphael’s Rooms toward the Sistine Chapel. The chapel is a consecrated space, and silence is asked for. Michelangelo painted the ceiling between 1508 and 1512, and later painted the Last Judgement on the altar wall. Give St Peter’s about an hour or two.

Castel Sant’Angelo

A short walk from the square, along the Tiber, Castel Sant’Angelo began as Emperor Hadrian’s drum-shaped tomb. The bridge that leads to it, Ponte Sant’Angelo, was built by Hadrian in AD 134 and later lined with Bernini-designed marble angels. The tomb was later turned into a fortress, and then a papal castle, with a papal apartment and a prison inside, and a terrace at the top. Allow about an hour to an hour and a half.

Galleria Borghese

In the gardens of the Villa Borghese, a smaller villa built in the early 1600s was Cardinal Scipione Borghese’s showcase for his collection. The rooms hold Bernini’s David, Apollo and Daphne and The Rape of Proserpina, as well as paintings by Caravaggio and Raphael. Visits are timed; the Earwise guide is written to fit a two-hour slot.

A longer day: Tivoli

If you have a longer day, Tivoli is a classic outing from Rome. In the second century Hadrian built a sprawling villa in the hills, a palace city in miniature; in the sixteenth century, Cardinal Ippolito d’Este created a garden of fountains just up the hill, and marble and statues were taken from Hadrian’s ruins for it. The Earwise guide covers both estates, and either can come first.
