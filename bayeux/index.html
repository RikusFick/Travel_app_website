    The west front of Notre-Dame de Bayeux, with its two great towers, hides a Romanesque cathedral inside a Gothic one.

    The first great cathedral here was built under Bishop Odo, half-brother of William the Conqueror. It was consecrated on the fourteenth of July 1077, in the presence of William, by then King of England, and his wife Matilda. Odo grew rich from the conquest of England, and he is thought to have commissioned the Bayeux Tapestry, the famous embroidered story of the Norman Conquest, nearly seventy metres long, which for centuries hung in this cathedral. Much of the church was damaged in 1105, during the war between William's sons, and was rebuilt in the Gothic style over the following centuries; the round-arched openings high on the towers survive from the earlier building.

    Inside, the lower level of the nave is still Romanesque, from the mid-twelfth century: round arches covered with zigzags and geometric carving, and between them small stone reliefs of lions, dragons and monsters. In 1855 the central tower over the crossing seemed about to fall and was nearly demolished. Instead the engineer Eugène Flachat, who was building the Paris–Cherbourg railway, held it up on scaffolding and rebuilt the four crossing piers on new foundations reaching solid rock, about nine and a half metres down. A light copper dome was then set on top in the 1860s.

    From about 1180 the bishop and canons rebuilt in the new Gothic style then spreading from the Île-de-France, but they never tore the old church down. They wrapped it instead. The Romanesque towers were strengthened with thick stepped buttresses and crowned with tall Gothic spires, the west front was re-clad with five porches, and the upper nave was rebuilt in the thirteenth century with pointed rib vaults and large windows of stained glass. The stone is a pale local limestone; it is thought to have come from quarries around Creully, a few kilometres east, because the famous Caen stone is not found in this part of Normandy.
