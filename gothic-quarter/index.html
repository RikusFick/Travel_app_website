Uncover two thousand years of Barcelona history hidden behind medieval stone lanes and nineteenth-century Gothic revivals. Eight stops link Roman Barcino's fortified gates and hidden columns with the Cathedral and royal square.

  1. 1

    Plaça Nova and the Roman gate

    Start beside the twin cylindrical towers that once guarded the Roman gate of Barcino.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    These two round towers in Plaça Nova flanked the Porta Praetoria, the main gate of Roman Barcelona, a small colony called Barcino.

    Barcino's wall ran for only about one and a half kilometres, enclosing some ten hectares. From the 250s, raids by Franks and Alemanni pushed the city to rebuild it in the fourth century, much stronger, with around eighty towers; the round ones, like these, guarded the gates. The gate had a wide central arch for carts and two smaller ones for people on foot. The street running in through it, now Carrer del Bisbe, was the Roman decumanus, one of the town's two main axes. Beside the left-hand tower stand the arches of a Roman aqueduct that brought water into the town.

    The right-hand tower is built into the Bishop's Palace, which gave the gate its popular name, the Portal del Bisbe. Until 1823 an arch joined the two towers.

    Through this gate begins the Gothic Quarter, the Barri Gòtic, the oldest part of the city, a maze of lanes and squares between La Rambla and the Via Laietana.

    The modern building across the square, the College of Architects, built between 1958 and 1962, carries a long frieze scratched into its concrete from drawings by Pablo Picasso.

  2. 2

    Barcelona Cathedral

    Visit Barcelona Cathedral, built over earlier basilicas with a peaceful cloister of white geese.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This soaring, pinnacled front belongs to Barcelona Cathedral, the Cathedral of the Holy Cross and Saint Eulalia.

    The first stone was laid on 1 May 1298, and the church was built from the east end westwards; a plain west front was finished in 1417 and the cloister in 1448. The spiky façade seen today is much younger. In the late nineteenth century the Girona banking family paid for a neo-Gothic façade based on a fifteenth-century design by a master named Carlí. The architects Josep Oriol Mestres and August Font built it between 1882 and 1913, together with the central spire. The new front is some 40 metres wide, and the cathedral's two octagonal bell towers each rise about 53 metres.

    Eulalia, the city's co-patron, was a young Christian martyr of Roman times. According to tradition she was rolled down a street in a barrel studded with knives; the lane said to be the place is still called the Baixada de Santa Eulàlia. Her relics lie in the crypt below the high altar. In the cloister, around the Well of the Geese, live thirteen white geese, one, it is said, for each year of her life.

    Inside, the choir stalls bear the coats of arms of the knights of the Golden Fleece, the chivalric order the emperor Charles V gathered here in 1519.

  3. 3

    Plaça de Sant Felip Neri

    Pause in a quiet stone square still scarred by shrapnel from a Spanish Civil War bombing.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This quiet little square, with its fountain and the pitted stone front of the Church of Sant Felip Neri, has one of the saddest stories in Barcelona.

    For centuries the ground here was a cemetery of the cathedral, where the city also buried those executed on the gallows. The Baroque church was built in the middle of the eighteenth century, and the burial ground closed in 1816.

    On 30 January 1938, during the Spanish Civil War, a bomb dropped by aircraft supporting Franco's Nationalists hit the church. Many of the dead were children from the school beside it and refugee children from other parts of Spain who had taken shelter in the basement. The bombing went on while people were digging for survivors in the rubble. Forty-two people were killed. The scars on the church front are from the shrapnel. Under Franco a different story was spread, claiming they were bullet marks from firing squads.

    When the square was rebuilt, the city architect Adolf Florensa reused the Renaissance fronts of two old guild houses, the coppersmiths' and the shoemakers', dismantled elsewhere in the old town, so the square looks older than it is.

    The school still uses the square as its playground.

  4. 4

    The bridge on Carrer del Bisbe

    Look up at the Neo-Gothic skybridge spanning Carrer del Bisbe, added in 1928.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This lacy stone bridge over Carrer del Bisbe looks as medieval as anything in Barcelona, but it was built in 1928.

    It was designed by the architect Joan Rubió i Bellver, a former assistant of Gaudí, to link the Palace of the Generalitat, the seat of the Catalan government, on one side, with the Houses of the Canons on the other. Its Flamboyant Gothic tracery was new work, not salvage.

    The bridge is a small key to the whole Gothic Quarter. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the neighbourhood around the cathedral was transformed from a dark, run-down district into a showpiece, timed for the International Exhibition of 1929. Façades were added in Gothic style, buildings were moved stone by stone, and new neo-Gothic features were built well into the 1960s. Much of the "Gothic" here is therefore a careful twentieth-century creation layered over genuine medieval and Roman fabric.

    The street's own name has changed with politics: it has been called after Saint Eulalia, the poet García Lorca and Bishop Irurita, who was killed in 1936, before the city settled in 1982 on simply Carrer del Bisbe, Bishop's Street. On the Generalitat side, the old Gothic front of the palace bears a medallion of Saint George killing the dragon.

  5. 5

    Plaça de Sant Jaume

    Cross the civic square where Catalonia's regional government and Barcelona's city hall face each other.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This wide square is Plaça de Sant Jaume, the political heart of both Barcelona and Catalonia: the Palace of the Generalitat, the Catalan government, faces Barcelona City Hall across it.

    The spot has been a centre for two thousand years. The Roman town's two main streets, the cardo and the decumanus, crossed near here, and the forum lay close by. In the Middle Ages the church of Sant Jaume, Saint James, stood on the site, and the city council used to meet in its porch. The church was demolished in 1823, when Carrer de Ferran was opened, and the open square was laid out afterwards.

    The Generalitat's Renaissance front faces the square; its medieval Gothic front, with the chapel of Saint George, looks onto Carrer del Bisbe behind. The City Hall's square-facing front, by contrast, is a neoclassical design of the nineteenth century, while its older Gothic façade survives on the side street.

    The square is where Catalan politics plays out in public: rallies, celebrations and, on the great feast days, the human towers called castells, built by teams standing on each other's shoulders.

  6. 6

    The Temple of Augustus

    Step into an inner courtyard hiding four towering Corinthian columns from a Roman temple to Augustus.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    These four fluted Corinthian columns, hidden in a courtyard, are the remains of the Roman temple traditionally called the Temple of Augustus, the main temple of Barcino.

    They stand on the Mont Tàber, a low rise that was the highest point of the Roman town, next to where the forum lay. Reconstructions describe a temple surrounded by columns, eleven along each side and six across each end, about 35 by 17.5 metres, raised on a high podium. Its dedication to the emperor Augustus is traditional rather than proven.

    At some point after Roman times the temple was demolished and its remains were swallowed by later houses. An architect, Antoni Cellés, described and mapped the ruins in the 1830s. Three of these columns came to light in the late nineteenth century on the site of the Catalan Hiking Centre, which owns the building; a fourth, once displayed in Plaça del Rei, was later added to them. The columns are now looked after by the Barcelona City History Museum.

    A round stone set in the paving at the entrance to Carrer del Paradís marks the Mont Tàber, though the "mountain" is barely a slope in the street.

  7. 7

    Plaça del Rei

    Stand in the medieval courtyard where Catholic monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella welcomed Columbus.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This enclosed medieval square is the Plaça del Rei, the King's Square, framed by the Palau Reial Major, the palace of the counts of Barcelona and kings of Aragon.

    Behind the fan of steps and the great arched windows lies the Saló del Tinell, built by King Peter IV in 1359 to 1370, a hall spanned by huge round stone arches. Its master builder was Guillem Carbonell. The hall is about 33 metres long and 17 wide, and its six arches carry a timber roof with no columns in the way. It was the great hall of the palace, used for royal courts, banquets and ceremonies. Tradition says that Ferdinand and Isabella received Christopher Columbus here on his return from his first voyage in 1493. Beside it, the Royal Chapel of Saint Agatha, from 1302, sits on the Roman wall, and its altarpiece of the Epiphany was painted by Jaume Huguet. The five-storey tower rising above the corner is known as King Martin's Watchtower.

    The way the hall is roofed is typical of medieval Catalonia. Instead of a heavy stone vault, the builders threw six transverse arches across the room, springing from just above the floor, and laid a flat roof of timber beams from one arch to the next. This was quicker and cheaper than vaulting, the side walls could be thinner because they carried little more than their own weight, and each bay between arches helped to contain a fire. Outside, buttresses between the windows take the sideways push of the arches. The name is said to come from the tinell, a stepped sideboard set up here to display the royal plate at banquets. Centuries later the hall became a church for the nuns of the convent of Santa Clara, who walled it into smaller rooms, and the great arches were rediscovered only in the twentieth century.

    On the left stands the Lieutenant's Palace, built from 1549 for the king's viceroy. After the kings left, parts of the old palace were used by the Inquisition.

    The house on the south side, Casa Padellàs, is a merchant's palace of about 1500 that was moved here stone by stone from Carrer dels Mercaders in 1931. It houses the Barcelona City History Museum, and beneath the square lie excavated streets, workshops and houses of Roman Barcino. The iron and concrete sculpture in one corner is Topos V by Eduardo Chillida.

  8. 8

    The Roman wall on Plaça de Ramon Berenguer el Gran

    End along the outer stretch of Roman defensive wall topped by medieval chapels and arcades.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This long stretch of stone wall with its towers is the best-preserved section of the Roman wall of Barcelona, with the Royal Chapel of Saint Agatha built on top of it.

    The first wall of Barcino, from the first century BC, was simple. The one standing here is the fourth-century rebuilding: two faces of stone with rubble and mortar packed between, and some eighty towers around the circuit. Builders reused older stones, including pieces of tombs and statues, so carved fragments can be spotted among the blocks. In the Middle Ages the wall was raised and topped with new masonry, and later buildings, including the royal palace and the chapel, simply used it as a foundation, which is why so much survives.

    For centuries this side was hidden behind houses. It was uncovered in the twentieth century, when the Via Laietana was cut through the old town and the buildings in front were cleared.

    The bronze rider in the square is Ramon Berenguer III, count of Barcelona in the early twelfth century, who extended Catalan power along the coast; the statue is by Josep Llimona.
