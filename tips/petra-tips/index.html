Petra tips: which route to take, and how long you need

The official main trail, two serious climbs, the Royal Tombs and a quieter canyon to the north — with audio guides that play as you arrive.

Petra is a Nabataean caravan city, half built and half carved in the sandstone, and made possible by a system of dams, cisterns and water channels. The Nabataeans grew rich on the incense, spice and silk trade between Arabia, Egypt and Syria-Phoenicia. Rome annexed the city in AD 106. An earthquake in 363 damaged the city and its water system. Johann Ludwig Burckhardt entered in 1812, and Petra was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1985.

Which route to pick

If you have one walk in you, start with the Main Trail. It is the official route from the Visitor Centre to Qasr al-Bint, 3.9 kilometres one way. You walk mostly downhill on the way in, and uphill on the way out. Carry water and sun protection, and please do not touch or lean on the carved facades.

A Nabataean dam at the mouth of the Siq, with a tunnel beside it, still turns flash floods aside. The Siq itself is a natural cleft about 1.2 kilometres long, in places only about three metres wide, with walls that rise to around 180 metres. Clay pipes and rock-cut channels run along the sides. At the far end the canyon opens onto the Treasury, Al-Khazneh, a facade carved from a single cliff, about 37 to 40 metres high. It may have been the mausoleum of the Nabataean king Aretas IV. An urn of solid sandstone at the top is pitted with bullet marks from treasure hunters. The facade is well known from the film Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade.

Beyond the Treasury, the Street of Facades leads on to a theatre cut into the mountainside — and through older tombs, whose backs you can still see. From here you look up at the Royal Tombs, four great facades side by side on Jebel Khubtha: the Urn Tomb, the Silk Tomb, the Corinthian Tomb and the Palace Tomb. Further along, a Roman colonnaded street runs over an older Nabataean way, past the Great Temple and through the Temenos Gate, to Qasr al-Bint, the city’s main temple. It is built of masonry rather than carved, and stands about 23 metres high.

How long you need

The main trail alone is 3.9 kilometres one way. Add the Monastery’s 1.6 kilometres and roughly eight hundred steps, or the High Place climb of about three quarters of an hour, and you will want time and energy in hand. We do not list opening hours or ticket prices here, because those change; check with the official site before you go.

The climbs: the Monastery steps and the High Place

The walk to the Monastery, Ad-Deir, starts near Qasr al-Bint and climbs about 1.6 kilometres, by way of roughly eight hundred steps, many of them cut by the Nabataeans. It is steep and unshaded in places. Start early, or in the late afternoon, carry water, and keep clear of the cliff edges at the viewpoints. The facade at the top is about 45 to 48 metres high and roughly as wide, with views out to Wadi Araba.

The High Place of Sacrifice is a different ascent. The ancient steps leave the main path just before the Theatre, off the Street of Facades. The climb takes most people about three quarters of an hour. It is steep, and the way down through Wadi Farasa is a long descent on uneven steps. Bring water, wear proper shoes, and keep back from the edges at the summit. At the top is an open-air altar on a mountaintop, with views across Petra. This Earwise guide is free.

What not to miss

The Siq and the Treasury are the arrival everyone comes for. Give the Royal Tombs a climb of their own as well. The stairs are steep and unshaded. The Palace Tomb is about 49 metres wide and 46 metres high; its upper storey was finished in masonry where the cliff ran out. The Urn Tomb was later used as a church, in the year 447, under Bishop Jason. The Silk Tomb is famous for the coloured bands in the sandstone, revealed as rain and wind wore the facade smooth.

A quieter Petra sits about six kilometres to the north. Siq al-Barid, Little Petra, is entered through a narrow, shady canyon — its name means “the cold canyon” — and belongs to the same World Heritage listing and archaeological park. Among its carved halls is a painted dining room, with vines, grapes and figures still on the walls, and a short walk continues to the much older settlement of Beidha.

Early and late in the day

For the Monastery climb, start early or in the late afternoon, when the steps are less punishing. The High Place and the Royal Tombs are also steep and, in places, unshaded, so carry water, wear proper shoes, and leave enough time to come down carefully.
