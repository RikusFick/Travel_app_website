    This wall is not ancient. Sixty-five metres of mud-brick defences went up here between 2003 and 2005. The German Archaeological Institute built them to show how Hattusa’s walls may have looked, and to test how they were made. Measure the towers against the wall beside them: the pair rise twelve to thirteen metres, while the three stretches of wall reach seven to eight.

    Roughly sixty-four thousand bricks were needed, and they are nothing but earth, straw and water, left to dry in the open air rather than baked in a kiln. Supplies arrived by machine, but the building itself aimed to follow the materials and methods the Hittites had.

    Inside, the wall is a series of boxes: an outer and an inner mud-brick wall joined by short cross-walls. For its outline, including the triangular battlements along the top, the team drew on clay models that once decorated the rims of large ritual vases.

    Little of the real thing survives to copy. Hittite buildings are mostly known from their stone bases, plus a few stretches of mud brick that fire baked hard when a building burned. Those burnt stretches give the brick size, square, about forty-five centimetres across and ten thick, yet they say too little to rebuild a whole structure.

    Hattusa was the capital of a Bronze Age empire that ruled much of Anatolia and, at times, northern Syria, and its archives preserved a peace treaty with Egypt.
