  1. 1

    The mouth of Souk Semmarine

    At the north side of Jemaa el-Fna, under the arch where Souk Semmarine begins. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Behind you is the noise of the great square; ahead, a covered lane that swallows the daylight in slatted stripes. This is Souk Semmarine, leading from the great square into the souks.

    UNESCO says the Almoravids founded Marrakech in 1070 to 1072.

    Its advisers add that the Almoravids stretched their empire "from the Sahara to the Ebro".

    The same evaluation says that Ali ibn Yusuf had the city walled in 1126 and 1127. Its ramparts of clay and lime run for some ten kilometres around the old town.

    When UNESCO inscribed the medina as a World Heritage Site in 1985, one of its reasons was the old town itself. Across 700 hectares, its statement sees an outstanding living historic town. It lists the maze of lanes, the houses and souks, the fondouks, the craft workshops and the old trades.

    UNESCO adds that restoring old buildings with traditional materials has revived the building crafts. It names zellij tilework, tadelakt lime plaster, painted and carved wood, plasterwork, wrought iron and cabinetmaking. Watch for those trades in the workshops as you walk.

    If you do not want to buy, a smile and "la, shukran", meaning no thanks, is polite.

  2. 2

    The Mouassine Mosque and fountain

    In front of the Mouassine Fountain, beside the mosque. This is stop 2 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The quarter around you owes its shape to a Saadian sultan, Abdallah al-Ghalib, who built it up in the second half of the sixteenth century. That date comes from the published history of the garden palace a few steps up this street.

    The fountain in front of you is part of the Mouassine complex, beside the mosque. An architecture database dates it to 1562 and 1563, and names al-Ghalib as its patron.

    When UNESCO's advisers evaluated the medina in 1985, they listed several Saadian fountains decorated with plaster and carved wood.

    They named three: this one at Mouassine, the Chrob ou Chouf fountain, and the fountain of Bab Doukkala.

    The same evaluation sees a new flowering of the arts under the Saadians. It points to the ruins of the El Badi Palace and to the Saadian Tombs as proof.

    Look closely. Beside the fountain stand plain arches; the fountain's own arch is the decorated one. At its top, a canopy of carved wood projects over the street, and below it the wood rests on brackets of carved plaster.

    Only Muslims may enter the mosque, but the fountain belongs to the street.

  3. 3

    Le Jardin Secret

    Outside the entrance of Le Jardin Secret on Rue Mouassine. This is stop 3 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Behind this plain wall lies Le Jardin Secret, a garden palace. Its own published history tells the story of the site.

    A palace stood here when the Saadians built up the quarter in the sixteenth century. It was destroyed in the late seventeenth century, after the dynasty declined.

    Around the middle of the nineteenth century, the kaid al-Hajj Abd-Allah U-Bihi took the land, and a new palace rose on the old Saadian plan. It was a time of great growth in Marrakech, which favoured gardens and rich houses.

    Soon afterwards, the history says, U-Bihi was poisoned with tea amid intrigues for power. The property passed to the qadi Moulay Mustapha, a senior judge close to the ruling family.

    In 1912 the qadi traded the palace for the Fez house of al-Hajj Muhammad Loukrissi, head of the city's guild of clockmakers.

    He had been chosen in 1908 as chamberlain to Sultan Moulay Abd al-Hafiz.

    When the sultan went into exile in 1912, Loukrissi moved in here. He lived here until his death in 1934, and then the place quickly fell into ruin.

    The restoration project began in 2012, and the garden opened to visitors in 2016. Its gardens were laid out by the landscape designer Tom Stuart-Smith.

    They draw on an old tradition. The garden's own account says that from the Middle Ages to the early twentieth century, Marrakech was a model garden city. Its old nicknames show it: "rose among the palms", "oasis in the desert", and al-Bahja, the city of happy, open-air life.

  4. 4

    Chrob ou Chouf, the Saadian wall fountain

    In front of the Chrob ou Chouf wall fountain. This is stop 4 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This wall fountain is known as Chrob ou Chouf. UNESCO's 1985 evaluation of the medina names it among the Saadian fountains decorated with plaster and carved wood.

    At street level the fountain is plain. Raise your eyes to the canopy of carved wood, worked into arabesques and into muqarnas, the honeycomb-like cells that seem to drip from the ceiling, under a sloping roof of green tiles.

    An architecture database lists a restoration of this fountain among a set of small conservation projects in the medina, dated 2000. They were chosen for their part in everyday life: a hammam, a fountain, a market run entirely by women vendors, and a covered souk.

    The work followed talks with the local community. It kept as much of the old fabric as it could, using traditional materials and crafts.

    UNESCO's statement on the site mentions the khettaras, galleries dug underground to drain water. The 1985 evaluation counts the khettara network of the palm groves, with the bridge over the Tensift river, among the best-known engineering works of the Almohads.

    UNESCO's 2009 statement lists the khettaras falling out of use among the pressures on the medina. It names uncontrolled changes to houses and the palm groves being exploited alongside it.

    Before you drink from any public tap in the medina, check that it is marked as drinking water.

  5. 5

    The Almoravid Qubba

    At the railing above the sunken Almoravid Qubba. This is stop 5 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Below you is a small domed pavilion built by the Almoravids, the dynasty that founded the city, in the early twelfth century. A heritage catalogue calls it one of a handful of Almoravid monuments the Almohads left standing. An architecture database goes further, and calls it the only surviving building of the city's founders.

    You look down on it for a reason. An art historian notes that the street level around it has risen by about six metres.

    Its builder was Ali ibn Yusuf, the second ruler of the dynasty. His mother was a Christian, and he had lived more years in Andalusia than at home in Morocco by the time he took the throne at twenty-three.

    The catalogue credits him with bringing the art and culture of Andalusia into Morocco. The Almohads, who followed his dynasty, destroyed most of his buildings.

    An inscription in the pavilion names him. It was defaced after the Almohads took the city in 1147, most likely by them.

    What was the pavilion for? The catalogue and the database describe it as the ablutions pavilion of his great mosque, which the chroniclers mention but which is lost.

    The art historian Yasser Tabbaa doubts it. He notes that it stood about 25 metres south of the mosque's prayer wall, too far away and on the wrong side. He also dates its inscription to 1117, some fifteen years before the mosque.

    Earth and ash, several metres deep, once buried it. A heritage catalogue dates its excavation to 1952 and 1953; UNESCO's advisers give 1955.

    Look at the outside. It is a rectangle of about seven metres by five and a half, in two storeys divided at about five metres by a plain projecting band. Below are pairs of horseshoe arches on the long sides and a single lobed arch on each short side.

    Higher up, sixteen arches of different shapes let light into the upper level. The brick dome is covered with interlaced blind arches and rings of chevrons that circle a seven-pointed star. The catalogue reports that historians of Islamic art call it extraordinary.

    Next to it, the database adds, stood a great fountain. Its site was later covered by a bazaar that migrants from Fez set up in the nineteenth century.

    The building beside the Qubba is Dar Menebhi, the palace that became the Marrakech Museum of the Omar Benjelloun Foundation.
