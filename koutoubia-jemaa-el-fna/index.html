    The tower in front of you has stood over Marrakech for more than eight hundred years. UNESCO calls the Koutoubia, with its minaret of 77 metres, the city's symbol.

    The name comes from the book trade. A scholarly database explains that the eastern side of the mosque opened onto a street lined with bookshops. People called it the mosque of the booksellers, and the name stuck.

    It was built by the Almohads, who took Marrakech in 1147, and their caliph, Abd al-Mu'min, raised this mosque. A chronicle cited by the historian Gaston Deverdun dates the work closely.

    It says building began in early May 1158, and that the mosque opened with Friday prayers on 11 September that year.

    Now look at its shape. The tower is square in plan, almost thirteen metres on each side, and it rises to about 77 metres at the tip of its spire. The main shaft ends in a crown of stepped battlements. Above that sits a smaller square tower, the lantern, with its own ribbed dome.

    It is built of large stones, and the lantern uses brick. Look closely and you will see that no two faces are the same. Each side carries its own panels of carved blind arches, set at different heights. Near the top, just under the battlements, runs a broad band of green tiles.

    There are no stairs inside. An atlas of Almohad architecture describes a broad ramp instead, winding up around a stack of vaulted rooms. It finds the same plan in two other great towers of the dynasty, the Hassan Tower in Rabat and the Giralda in Seville.

    Morocco's World Heritage file for Rabat adds a contest of height. The Hassan Tower, it says, was planned at about 85 metres, and could have overtaken both of the others. One scholarly catalogue calls the Koutoubia "one of the crowning achievements of Islamic art in the West".
