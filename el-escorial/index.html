    This long granite front above the Lonja is the west front of El Escorial, the Royal Monastery of San Lorenzo, at the foot of Mount Abantos in the Sierra de Guadarrama, about forty-five kilometres north-west of Madrid.

    King Philip II built it as a monastery, a royal palace, a basilica, a library and a burial place for his dynasty, all in one. It celebrates his victory over the French at the Battle of Saint-Quentin, fought on 10 August 1557, the feast of Saint Lawrence, which is why the building is dedicated to him. Tradition says that its gridiron plan recalls the grill on which Saint Lawrence was martyred; historians point instead to older courtyard plans and to descriptions of the Temple of Solomon.

    The first stone was laid on 23 April 1563. The first architect, Juan Bautista de Toledo, had worked on Saint Peter's in Rome; when he died in 1567, his assistant Juan de Herrera took over and finished the building in 1584, in just under twenty-one years. In Spanish, "la obra de El Escorial", the work of El Escorial, still means a job that takes forever.

    The whole complex is a rectangle of about 224 by 153 metres, with a spired tower at each corner and the dome of the basilica rising in the middle. Philip asked his architects for "simplicity in the construction, severity in the whole, nobility without arrogance, majesty without ostentation". The result, plain grey granite with almost no ornament, became a style of its own, the Herrerian style.

    Of the three doors in this front, the central one, beneath a niche with a statue of Saint Lawrence, leads to the basilica; the side doors led to the school and the monastery. Hieronymite monks lived here from 1567; since 1885 it has been home to Augustinians. El Escorial has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1984.
