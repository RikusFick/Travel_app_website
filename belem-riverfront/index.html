  1. 1

    Belém Palace

    In the Praça Afonso de Albuquerque, facing the long front of the Belém Palace.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Belém Palace stretches along the far side of this square, a long, low country house looking out towards the river. It is the official residence of the President of Portugal.

    It began in the 1500s as the riverside house of a nobleman, Dom Manuel de Portugal. In 1726 King João the Fifth bought the estate and made it a royal summer residence by the water. Beside it stood a royal riding school, where royal horses were trained, and that riding school later became the home of the National Coach Museum.

    The palace stayed a royal summer estate into the last years of the monarchy, and it also lodged foreign visitors. In 1886 Prince Carlos, the heir to the throne and the future King Carlos, married Amélia of Orléans, and the couple made their home here for their first three years of marriage. Their younger son, Manuel, who would be the last king of Portugal, was born in the palace in 1889.

    After the monarchy fell in 1910 the palace passed to the new republic. In 1912 its Congress allowed the president to use an annex of the palace, for a monthly rent, and the palace has been tied to the presidency ever since.

  2. 2

    Pastéis de Belém

    On the Rua de Belém, in front of the blue awnings of the Pastéis de Belém shop.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    This shop on the Rua de Belém, under its blue awnings, is the home of the pastel de Belém, the custard tart in crisp, layered pastry that inspired the pastel de nata found all over Portugal.

    Its story leads back to the Jerónimos Monastery just along the street. Portugal's convents and monasteries were famous for their sweets, made with egg yolks, and a whole tradition of convent baking grew up across the country.

    In 1834, after the religious orders were dissolved, the monastery was closed. Next to it stood a sugar-cane refinery with a small shop attached, and in 1837 baking began in buildings beside the refinery. The shop traces its tarts to a recipe from the monastery's monks, though that monastic origin is not documented. The recipe has been kept secret ever since.

    In 2011 the pastel de Belém won the sweets category of the Seven Wonders of Portuguese Gastronomy, chosen by public vote.

  3. 3

    Praça do Império

    In the Praça do Império, the formal garden square between the Jerónimos Monastery and the river, by the central fountain.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Praça do Império, the Empire Square, is the great formal garden laid out in front of the Jerónimos Monastery, with lawns, hedges and a central fountain.

    It was created for one event: the Portuguese World Exhibition of 1940. That year, from June to December, this whole riverfront was turned into a vast fair of pavilions. It marked two anniversaries at once. By the regime's own reckoning, it was eight hundred years since the birth of the Portuguese nation in 1140, a date historians still debate, and three hundred years since Portugal regained its independence from Spain in 1640.

    The exhibition was staged by the Estado Novo, the authoritarian regime of António de Oliveira Salazar, and it told a proud story of Portugal's past: history, regional life, and the overseas empire. The pavilions were built for a single season and most were taken down afterwards, but the square remained, along with the stone seahorses by the sculptor António Duarte, made for the exhibition and set in stone in the mid-1940s.

  4. 4

    Monument to the Discoveries

    On the riverbank in front of the Monument to the Discoveries, looking up at the figures on its ramps.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Monument to the Discoveries rises fifty-six metres above the Tagus, shaped like the prow of a caravel heading out to sea, its tall stone mast and sails behind.

    At the very front stands Prince Henry the Navigator, holding a small ship. Behind him, climbing the two ramps, come thirty-two more figures from Portugal's age of discovery: navigators, map makers, missionaries, chroniclers and artists, each with a symbol of what they did. Among them are the poet Luís de Camões holding his epic, the Lusiads, and the painter Nuno Gonçalves with his palette.

    The first version stood here in 1940, for the World Exhibition, designed by the architect Cottinelli Telmo and the sculptor Leopoldo de Almeida. It was built of light materials, plaster on a frame of iron and cement. In 1960, for the five-hundredth anniversary of Henry's death, it was rebuilt to last, and it was inaugurated on the ninth of August: concrete faced with rose-coloured stone from Leiria, and the statues carved in limestone from Sintra.

    On the landward face, metal letters read: To Prince Henry and the Portuguese who discovered the paths of the sea, set over an anchor. A second inscription, over a crown of laurel, reads: On the fifth centenary of Prince Henry, 1460 to 1960. Since 1985 the monument has also been a cultural centre, with a viewpoint at the top looking down on the river and the great compass rose in the pavement below.

  5. 5

    The Compass Rose

    On the paved square north of the Monument to the Discoveries, on the great compass rose set into the ground.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The compass rose spreads across the pavement here, fifty metres wide, its points laid out in different coloured stones.

    It was a gift from South Africa, unveiled in August 1960, and it was made of different kinds of limestone, including the lioz stone of the Lisbon region. Its design is credited to the architect Luís Cristino da Silva. At its centre is a world map fourteen metres across. Ships, the carracks and caravels of the Portuguese voyages, sail across its oceans along the routes of the 1400s and 1500s, each marked with a date. Among the plant motifs and smaller compass roses on the map are sea creatures: a mermaid, a fantastic fish, and Neptune with his trident.

    A compass rose shows the directions of the winds, and for sailors it was the heart of every chart: north, south, east and west, and all the points between. Here it frames a map of the voyages that set out from this river in the 1400s and 1500s.

  6. 6

    Tejo Power Station and MAAT

    On the riverside walkway in front of the red-brick Tejo Power Station, with the low, curved white MAAT building beside it.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The red-brick halls of the Tejo Power Station stand beside the river, and next to them the curving white roof of the MAAT, the Museum of Art, Architecture and Technology.

    The power station was built to light Lisbon. It burned coal to make steam, and the steam turned the generators that powered the city. It began work in 1909 as a small plant, planned to run for only six years. Lisbon was growing fast: the electricity company's own history counts about fifteen hundred customers in 1909 and more than thirteen thousand a decade later. The plant was enlarged several times, and the red-brick halls you see were built in stages between 1914 and 1951.

    From 1951 it served as a reserve plant, switched on only when the hydroelectric dams could not meet demand. Its last days of production came in August 1972, when opponents of the Salazar regime damaged high-voltage lines near Lisbon and left parts of the area without power. The old boilers were fired up, and for about a week the Tejo Power Station fed the capital's network.

    When the station closed, its halls and boilers were kept. In 1986 it was protected as a building of public interest, and in 1990 it opened as the Museum of Electricity, which has been a permanent exhibition there since 2006.

    In 2016 the new MAAT building opened beside it, designed by the British architect Amanda Levete, a low wave of a building that rises from the riverbank.
