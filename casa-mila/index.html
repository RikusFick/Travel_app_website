    The façade of Casa Milà

    Inspect the self-supporting undulating stone exterior and tangled wrought-iron balconies from the corner.

    Free sample: stop 1

    Read along

    This undulating wall of pale stone is the façade of Casa Milà, the last private house Antoni Gaudí designed, built between 1906 and 1912 for Pere Milà and his wife Roser Segimon.

    Segimon was the wealthy widow of an "indiano", a Catalan who had made his fortune on a coffee plantation in Guatemala; Milà was a developer with a taste for display. They planned to live on the main floor and let the rest. Barcelona was not impressed: people mocked the rough stone front as "La Pedrera", the stone quarry, cartoonists drew it as an Easter cake, and neighbours on the avenue are said to have stopped greeting Milà, fearing it would drag down the value of their own houses. Before it, this corner held a detached house with a garden, which was pulled down to make way for the new building.

    The stone is limestone from the Garraf massif on the lower floor and from a quarry at Vilafranca above. It hangs on an inner frame of pillars and iron beams, so the façade carries almost no weight and the flats behind could be laid out freely. The iron balconies, twisting like seaweed, were designed with Josep Maria Jujol.

    More than six thousand stone blocks make up the front, each tied back to the frame with iron bars, and no two of them are the same. Gaudí did not draw every stone. According to the builder who ran the site, a plasterer made a model of the whole façade, the model was broken into pieces, and each stonemason took a piece and copied it at full size, finishing the blocks once they were in place.

    Along the top runs a Latin line from the Hail Mary, "Ave gratia M plena". Gaudí wanted a great statue of the Virgin of the Rosary above it, but after the anticlerical riots of the Tragic Week in 1909 the Milàs dropped it.

    The city once threatened to fine the owners or demolish the attic and roof for exceeding the permitted height. In 1909 a commission declared the house a monumental building, and it was spared.
