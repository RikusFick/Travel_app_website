    A city that vanished

    Outside the outer wall, at the entrance used by visitors on the east side. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The long brick wall in front of you once stood at the heart of a city. That city has gone. Only this mosque survived it.

    The story begins in 868. In that year a commander named Ahmad ibn Tulun was sent to Egypt to govern Fustat for the Abbasid caliphs of Baghdad. Within two years he governed the whole country. Then he stopped sending the yearly tribute to Baghdad, and ruled Egypt as his own.

    Around 870 he began a new royal city. It was called al-Qata'i, which means "the wards". Every group of his followers was given its own plot. He chose a rocky rise near the Muqattam hills, north-east of old Fustat. Its name was Jabal Yashkur. Many legends clung to this hill. People believed that Noah's ark had come to rest here, and that God had spoken to Moses on this spot.

    On the hilltop he built his great mosque. Work began in 876 and ended in 879. The old Mosque of Amr in Fustat was too small for his troops, so this became the new place for Friday prayer. Egypt's antiquities ministry sees the building as one more step in his break from Baghdad. A foundation plaque on one of the piers inside gives the patron's name and the date. It is carved in a plain, angular Kufic script.

    In 905 the Abbasids took Egypt back. Their army destroyed al-Qata'i and ploughed it under. Almost nothing of the city survived. The mosque was spared, and it is the one monument left from the whole city.

    Ibn Tulun had trained as a soldier in Samarra, the Abbasid capital in Iraq. He brought its style with him.
