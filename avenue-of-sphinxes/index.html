    Read along

    You are at the southern edge of Karnak, the vast temple area of ancient Thebes. The modern city around it is Luxor.

    The tall stone gate in front of you carries the name of Ptolemy the Third, also called Euergetes. He belonged to the Ptolemies, the Greek-speaking family that ruled Egypt after Alexander the Great. His reign fell in the third century BC.

    Behind the gate lies the Temple of Khonsu. Building began under Ramesses the Third, in the twelfth century BC. It went up on the ruins of an older temple, and later rulers kept adding to it. Khonsu was a moon god. He was the son of Amun and the goddess Mut, and the three were worshipped together as a family. Artists often showed him as a boy with a side lock of hair. Or he appears as a robed figure crowned with a full moon resting in a crescent. People prayed to him as a healer, and as a god who could drive out evil spirits.

    Now turn and face south, away from the temple. A road of stone statues runs out from here into the city. This is the Avenue of Sphinxes. Locals also call it the Kebash Road, after the Arabic word for rams. It joins Karnak to Luxor Temple, a distance of about two point seven kilometres. That is roughly a mile and two thirds. The road is about seventy-six metres wide.

    Close to Karnak, most of the figures are ram-headed sphinxes, a ram's head on a lion's body. The ram was the sacred animal of Amun. So each statue is a way of showing the god guarding his own road. On many rams, a small figure of a king stands tucked against the chest, between the front legs. This king is usually named as Amenhotep the Third, of the fourteenth century BC.

    Many statues are badly worn. Heads are missing, and some are little more than blocks. For centuries the road lay under sand, and later under houses and streets. Nobody agrees on exactly how many statues there were. The number you will hear most often is one thousand and fifty-seven. Of those, eight hundred and seven have human heads and two hundred and fifty have ram heads. The marker at the Luxor end gives one thousand and sixty. Some estimates for the original total go as high as about one thousand three hundred and fifty.
