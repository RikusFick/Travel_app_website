    A temple at the edge of Egypt

    On the open ground in front of the Great Temple, facing the cliff. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Face the cliff. The temple in front of you was not built from blocks of stone. It was cut straight into the living rock of a sandstone cliff, on the Nile's west bank. In ancient times this was the southern frontier of Egypt, facing Nubia. It lies about two hundred and eighty kilometres south of Aswan.

    The king who ordered it was Ramesses the Second. He reigned for about sixty-six years, from around 1279 to 1213 BC.

    When was the temple made? The Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities dates the cutting to around 1264 BC. The work began early in his reign, and both temples were finished by about his twenty-fifth year. Scholars read his life differently. So there are two possible spans, around 1264 to 1244 BC, or around 1244 to 1224 BC. The work took about twenty years.

    Its ancient name meant the Temple of Ramesses, Beloved of Amun. It was dedicated to Amun-Ra, Ra-Horakhty and Ptah, and to the king himself as a god. Statues of all four sit in its innermost room.

    Look at the outline of the cliff face. The front is trapezoidal, cut to imitate the sloping towers of a temple gateway. It is roughly thirty metres high and thirty-five metres long.

    It also carried a message. To the Nubians who saw it, the temple was a sign that their land lay under the pharaoh's rule.

    What you see stands on a new site, higher than the original. It was cut into blocks and moved in the 1960s, and the hill behind it was made by engineers.
