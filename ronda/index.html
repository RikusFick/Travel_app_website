    Puente Nuevo

    Read along

    You are on the Puente Nuevo, the "New Bridge" of Ronda, which spans El Tajo, a gorge about 120 metres deep cut by the Guadalevín river.

    The gorge splits Ronda in two. To the south lies La Ciudad, the old walled Moorish town; to the north, El Mercadillo, the district that grew up after the Christian conquest. For centuries the only crossings were down near the river, so joining the two halves at the top was a huge ambition.

    The first attempt at this height began in the 1730s: a single daring arch sprung from one side of the rock to the other. In 1741, just as it was being finished, it fell into the gorge. Ronda's traditional histories speak of about fifty dead, while a report by a later mayor of the town says no one was on it when it fell. Foundations for the present bridge were laid in the late 1750s, and the work took thirty-four years, until 1793. This time it was built up from the riverbed. A lower arch near the water acts as a footing, two massive piers of rubble and lime faced with cut stone rise from it, and the great central arch springs high above, with a sealed chamber under the roadway. The blocks were quarried from the bottom of the gorge itself, so the bridge matches the rock, and accounts say they were hoisted with pulleys, winches and lifting gear devised by the chief builder, Juan Antonio Díaz Machuca. The architect José Martín de Aldehuela took charge in 1785. The bridge was finished in 1793. Measured from its foundations, the bridge stands about 98 metres high.

    Above the great central arch is a chamber that was used for many purposes over the years, including as a prison. It is reached through the small square building at the end of the bridge, once the guardhouse, which now holds a small exhibition about how the bridge was built.

    The building beside the bridge, now a Parador hotel, was once Ronda's town hall.
