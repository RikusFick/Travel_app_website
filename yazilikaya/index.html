    The sanctuary gateway

    Welcome to Yazılıkaya, a Hittite rock sanctuary outside the capital, Hattusa. The limestone outcrops form the walls of two open-air chambers. Some of the rocks rise about twelve metres above you.

    Gods, goddesses, animals and imaginary creatures were carved into these rock faces. Worship here reached back to Old Hittite times, before the major decoration associated with King Tudhaliya the Fourth. Buildings in front of the rocks developed over time and formed a monumental entrance to the sanctuary. An enclosing wall screened the sanctuary, and a temple formed part of the entrance complex. The eastern wing was later adapted to improve the entrance to the smaller chamber.

    From their courtyard, a purpose-built entrance led into Chamber A. Carved stone slabs may also have closed gaps in the chamber walls. Near the entrance to that chamber, a separate relief shows two seated deities at a banquet.

    Each has a hieroglyphic inscription identifying them. If the scene is visible from your permitted viewing position, look for the seated figures and their labels.

    Chamber A: the procession of the gods

    Welcome to Chamber A, the larger of Yazılıkaya’s two rock chambers. Two processions of gods and goddesses approach a meeting on the far wall. As you face that meeting, the figures on the left are mainly male; those on the right are mainly female. Twelve gods begin the male procession. A pair of bull-men also appears among the figures.

    The storm god Teshub stands on two mountain gods, holding a large club. Opposite him is the goddess Hebat, standing on a large cat. She wears a long pleated dress and a tall headdress. Behind her, Sharruma carries a long-handled axe over his shoulder. Two goddesses stand together on a double-headed eagle.

    Some figures have hieroglyphic names carved in front of their faces. In Hebat’s label, three signs spell he-pa-tu, her Hurrian name. If you cannot make out the signs, do not worry: weathering and the angle of the light can make them difficult to read. On the wall opposite the main scene stands King Tudhaliya the Fourth.

    He is shown on two mountain peaks, holding a staff with a curved end. His clothing is the ceremonial dress of the Sun God.

    Chamber B: the king and the underworld

    Chamber B is a narrow space between the rocks. Two winged, lion-headed figures flank its entrance, their hands raised as though guarding the passage. On one wall, twelve gods carry sickle-shaped swords.

    They have been interpreted as gods of the underworld. Across from them is a sword god, interpreted as the underworld deity Nergal. The blade points down into the ground.

    Two lions point downwards along its upper part, while two lion foreparts turn upwards to form the crosspiece. Above them is a human head in a pointed hat. Nearby, the god Sharruma embraces King Tudhaliya the Fourth and guides him forward. The king wears a long robe and cloak, a rounded headdress, and carries a curved staff. Hieroglyphs identify him; his name is framed by a winged sun and royal titles.

    There is also a separate royal name panel, without a carved figure of the king. Three niches are also cut into the chamber walls. Excavation uncovered a base beside the northern wall, possibly for a statue or a cult object. The relief figures were set against smoothed rock backgrounds. Beside the sword, a prepared stretch of rock carries faint traces of another figure.

    It is uncertain whether that carving was left unfinished or is simply badly weathered. This chamber may have been a memorial to Tudhaliya, but its exact purpose remains uncertain.
