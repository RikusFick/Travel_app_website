    Sacré-Cœur Basilica crowns the hill of Montmartre, the highest natural point in Paris, with its white domes.

    It was built as a national vow after France's defeat in the war of 1870 and the upheaval that followed. Work began in 1875 to designs by Paul Abadie and was finished in 1914, but the basilica was only consecrated in 1919, after the First World War.

    The stone comes from Château-Landon, south of Paris. When it rains, it releases a mineral that keeps the walls white. The basilica is eighty-five metres long and thirty-five metres wide, and its main dome rises about eighty-three metres. In the bell tower hangs the Savoyarde, at nineteen tonnes the largest bell in France: in 1895 it took twenty-eight horses to haul it up the hill.

    Tradition holds that the name Montmartre means "mount of the martyr", after Saint Denis, the first bishop of Paris, said to have been beheaded here.

    The idea came from two Parisian Catholic laymen, Alexandre Legentil and his brother-in-law Hubert Rohault de Fleury, who made a vow in the dark months of 1870 and 1871 to build a church dedicated to the Sacred Heart of Christ if France were spared. In July 1873 the National Assembly declared the project of public utility, allowing the Archbishop of Paris to acquire land on the hilltop, then covered with open-air cafés, a fairground and small gardens. Because the Paris Commune had begun on this hill in March 1871, the basilica has long been seen by critics as a monument to its repression, though no inscription inside links the two. The money came entirely from private gifts: in one scheme, families and groups could pay for a single stone or column and have their names or initials carved on it.

    Abadie won the design competition with a Romano-Byzantine plan inspired by the domed cathedral of Saint-Front in Périgueux and Saint Mark's in Venice; accounts differ on how many entries he beat. Cardinal Guibert laid the first stone on 16 June 1875. The hill itself was the first obstacle: for centuries it had been quarried underground for gypsum, the raw material of plaster of Paris, and the ground was riddled with galleries. To reach solid rock, the builders sank eighty-three shafts about thirty metres deep and filled them with stone and concrete, so the basilica stands on hidden pillars. Abadie died in 1884, and five architects in turn carried the work on.
