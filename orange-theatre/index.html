  1. 1

    The Stage Wall

    This part of the route looks at The Stage Wall.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The stage wall of the Roman theatre of Orange is so vast that King Louis XIV called it the most beautiful wall in his kingdom.

    It is about a hundred and three metres long and thirty-seven metres high, and it is one of the best-preserved stage walls of the whole Roman world. Orange was founded as a colony for veterans of the Second Legion in about 35 BC, under the name Arausio, and the theatre was built early in the first century AD, in the reign of the emperor Augustus. High on this outer face you can see two rows of stone brackets; they held the poles of a great awning, the velarium, that shaded the audience.

    Look at how plain this face is: large blocks of stone, three storeys, and almost no ornament. All the decoration was kept for the inside. At ground level, three doors lead straight through to the stage, the central one being the royal door.

    Seen from the inside, this wall did more than look impressive. It helped to carry the voices of the actors back towards the audience. Together with the triumphal arch at the northern edge of the town, the theatre was listed by UNESCO in 1981.

  2. 2

    The Seating

    The next story is about The Seating.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The seating of the theatre is cut into the slope of the Saint-Eutrope hill, and it held between about eight and a half and nine thousand spectators.

    Entry to the shows was free, and the audience sat by rank. The lowest tiers were for the leading men of the town, with the first three rows reserved for knights; citizens and merchants sat in the middle, and the top rows were left to slaves, prostitutes and non-citizens. Most of the stone rows you see were rebuilt between 1892 and 1904 by the architect Formigé, after earlier work by Caristie.

    The shape of the seating and the great wall facing it give the theatre its famous acoustics, and even today a voice on the stage carries to the top rows.

    The theatre was closed in the year 391, and after the end of the Roman Empire it was abandoned. In the Middle Ages it became part of the town's defences, and during the Wars of Religion it served as a refuge, and in the Revolution it was used as a prison; houses were then built inside it, right up against the stage. In the nineteenth century the houses were cleared and the monument was rescued, a slow task that began in the 1820s. Prosper Mérimée, the inspector of historic monuments, pressed for the work.

    In 1869 the first festival was held here. It became the Chorégies d'Orange, one of the oldest festivals in France, and great singers and artists still perform here. In the early years drama and music shared the stage, and Sarah Bernhardt played Phèdre here in 1903. The name Chorégies recalls a tax once levied on wealthy Romans to pay for theatre productions.

    Over time the music won out. From the early 1970s the festival turned above all to opera, and it is for its summer opera nights in this ancient setting that it is best known today.

    The Stage and Augustus

    Here the guide turns to The Stage and Augustus.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The statue in the central niche, traditionally identified as Augustus, watches over the stage.

    The emperor stands in armour, with his right arm raised, presiding over every performance as a reminder of Roman power. Its identification is debated. The statue is about three and a half metres tall, and some scholars think it was made or reworked later than the reign of Augustus. The inner face of the wall was once covered with marble, columns and statues on several levels; the wooden roof that sheltered the stage has gone, and today a modern roof of glass and metal protects it, following the line of the lost Roman roof. The stage itself was built of wood over a pit that held the machinery and a curtain that was lowered, not raised, at the start of a play. The stage is about sixty-one metres long and stood roughly a metre above the orchestra, the half-circle at your feet.

    On this side, the central royal door was used by the leading actors; the side doors were for the others. Above the royal door ran a frieze of centaurs, and fragments of it survive. Excavations around the stage in the 1930s uncovered many fragments of the lost decoration. They are kept in the town's museum, across the street from the theatre, along with other finds from the excavations and repair campaigns. The plain stone you see now is the skeleton of that decoration.

  4. 4

    The Triumphal Arch

    A short story at this point covers The Triumphal Arch.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The triumphal arch of Orange stands on the old Roman road to Lyon, the Via Agrippa, at the northern edge of the town.

    It is one of the oldest and best-preserved triumphal arches of its kind, built in the first century AD. It is about nineteen and a half metres long, eight metres deep and nineteen metres high. The big stone blocks were laid without mortar, and the central arch is larger than the two at the sides. Its sides are covered with carvings: battles between Romans and Gauls, piles of captured weapons, and naval trophies with anchors, ropes and ship prows that probably recall Roman sea victories. Look closely at the battle frieze: the Gauls fight naked, with long hair and shields, while the Romans wear tunics and sometimes armour. The carving is fairly rough, with oversized hands and feet. Some of the shields in the piles of weapons carry names, perhaps those of the sculptors or of well-known armourers. On the narrow ends, trophies of captured arms stand over pairs of prisoners in chains; the west end was largely rebuilt in the nineteenth century. The top level was made to carry great statues, probably including a group on horseback, and all of them are lost.

    Look up at the battle scene on the top level of the north side, facing away from the town. One of the Roman soldiers carries a shield with the emblem of the Second Legion, the legion whose veterans founded Orange. Its emblem is the capricorn. Later, around AD 26 or 27, it was given an inscription honouring the emperor Tiberius. The letters were made of bronze and set into the stone; they are all gone, and the text has been worked out from the holes that held them.

    Today it is widely thought that the arch also honoured the victories of Germanicus, the adopted son of Tiberius, who had died in the year 19. Like the theatre, the arch owes its survival partly to being built into a medieval fortification. In the 1820s the architect Auguste Caristie removed the medieval additions and restored the arch. The arch was on France's first list of historic monuments in 1840.
