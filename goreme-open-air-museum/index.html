    A valley of monks

    This part of the route looks at A valley of monks.

    You are at the entrance of the Göreme Open-Air Museum. Welcome to Göreme. Look around you: cones, cliffs and pillars of pale rock, and everywhere small dark openings. Doors, windows, steps. Every one of them was cut by hand.

    This landscape was made by volcanoes. Millions of years ago, great eruptions in central Anatolia buried this region under thick layers of volcanic ash and pumice, which hardened into soft rock. Two extinct volcanoes mark the horizon: Erciyes, about 3,900 metres high, and Hasan Dağı. Since then, rain, frost and wind have worn the soft layers away. Where a harder layer sits on top, it protects the rock beneath, and slowly a "fairy chimney" is left standing.

    That soft rock had one great advantage: you can carve it with simple tools. And so, from the fourth century onwards, people did. Small communities of monks and hermits came here, following the teachings of Basil the Great, Bishop of Caesarea (Kayseri). Basil, his brother Gregory of Nyssa, and Gregory of Nazianzus are remembered as the Cappadocian Fathers, three of the great thinkers of the early Church.

    For about nine hundred years, until the thirteenth century, this valley was a monastic settlement. Almost every block of rock held a church, a chapel, a dining hall or a place to sit together. The churches in this valley are the ones they cut and painted.

    The Göreme valley opened to visitors as a museum in 1967, and in 1985 it became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
