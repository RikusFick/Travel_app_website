    The National Palace of Sintra stands at the heart of the town, a cluster of white buildings with two great white cones rising above them: the chimneys of the royal kitchen, thirty-three metres tall. At ground level the main front opens in an arcade, with windows above. The palace probably grew from the residence of the Moorish governors of the area. When Afonso Henriques, the first king of Portugal, took Lisbon in 1147, Sintra surrendered too. In the 1280s King Dinis lowered the taxes of the free Moors of nearby Colares in return for their care of the palace, and in 1287 he granted the town of Sintra to his queen, Isabel of Aragon. From then on Sintra was again and again given to the queens of Portugal, and its income kept their households. Its present form comes from the building campaigns of several kings. João the First, who reigned from 1385, built the great kitchen and the Swan Room. Manuel the First, in the early 1500s, added a new wing and dressed many rooms in Hispano-Moorish tiles. The court came for the hunting, with wild boar and deer in the hills, for the cool summer air, and to take refuge when plague struck Lisbon. The palace was badly damaged in the earthquake of 1755 and rebuilt, keeping the silhouette it had had since the mid-1500s. Kings and queens returned here until the end of the monarchy in 1910, and the last royal to live here was Queen Maria Pia, who left for exile. That year the palace was declared a National Monument, and in the late 1930s it began to open to the public. It is part of the Cultural Landscape of Sintra, a UNESCO World Heritage site.
