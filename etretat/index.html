    The seafront looks out over a curved pebble beach, framed by white chalk cliffs on both sides.

    The pebbles are flint. They come from the dark bands in the chalk, and the waves roll them smooth; banked up along the shore, they soak up the force of the waves and protect the town. Rain and frost open cracks in the chalk, and the sea works away at the foot of the cliffs.

    Étretat was a fishing village before it became a seaside resort in the nineteenth century. Painters and other visitors came for the light and the strange rocks, among them Eugène Boudin, Gustave Courbet and Claude Monet, who painted these cliffs many times. The resort took off only after 1843. New roads were built to Fécamp and Le Havre. In 1852 a casino of wood and slate opened on this seafront, run by a new sea-bathing company. It staged comic opera, and Jacques Offenbach named his own villa here the Villa Orphée, after Orpheus in the Underworld.

    Almost none of that seafront survives. During the Second World War the German occupiers demolished the casino and villas along here to fortify the beach against a landing.

    Look along the shore for the caloges: old fishing boats that could no longer go to sea, turned upside down and roofed with thatch or planks. Fishermen used them as sheds for nets, ropes and tools, close to the capstan that hauled each family's boat up the shingle. In the old village behind you, the large church of Notre-Dame was built in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. It is big for such a small place, probably because the village belonged to the powerful abbey of Fécamp. An old legend reaches back further still. It is said that a pious and wealthy lady named Olive was surprised on this shore by Vikings landing from their ships, and that the village owes its beginnings to that story. Near the market hall, the timber-framed Manoir de la Salamandre is one of the oldest houses in town, though it was not built here: it was taken down in Lisieux and rebuilt in Étretat.

    The Falaise d'Amont is the cliff on the right as you face the sea, topped by the small chapel of Notre-Dame de la Garde.

    Its cliff holds the smallest of Étretat's three arches, the Porte d'Amont. The first chapel up here was blessed in 1856 as a chapel for fishermen and sailors, and local seamen carried the building materials up on their backs. Occupying troops destroyed it in 1942. The present neo-Gothic chapel dates from 1950: its nave is shaped like a ship's hull, and its windows of thick slab glass were added in 1964.

    Close by stands a monument to Charles Nungesser and François Coli. In May 1927 they took off from Paris in their plane, L'Oiseau Blanc, to fly non-stop to New York, and Étretat was the last place in France where they were seen. They were never found. Military planes escorted them as far as the coast, and people here watched the white biplane head out to sea. What happened next is still one of the great unsolved mysteries of aviation. Most people at the time assumed a storm had brought it down over the Atlantic, but later researchers have gathered accounts from witnesses on the far side of the ocean who said they had heard a plane.

    The first monument to the two airmen, put up in 1928, was destroyed during the Second World War. The one you see is a concrete spire twenty-four metres tall, raised in the early 1960s. Beside it stands a small museum about the flight.

    The Porte d'Aval is the huge arch at the end of the left-hand cliff, carved by the waves pounding the chalk and flint.

    The cliffs are made of layers of chalk laid down on a seabed millions of years ago, striped with dark bands of flint. Geologists date this chalk to the Late Cretaceous, to the stages called the Turonian and the Coniacian. There are no other rocks in it, no sandstone and no yellow limestone, only white chalk and flint, which is why the beach below is made of flint pebbles. The sea keeps cutting into them, so arches form, collapse and leave pillars behind. Rainwater soaks into the porous chalk, frost splits it, and whole sections of cliff fall away; you can see the piles of fallen rock at the foot. The sea then attacks the base.

    At low tide, look down at the rocky shelf below the arch. The square hollows cut into it are what is left of an oyster park built in 1777. It is said that it was made for Queen Marie-Antoinette. The oysters did not come from Étretat at all: two boats brought them from Cancale in Brittany, they were fattened here, and then they were sent on to Paris.

    The Needle

    Play0:00 / –:––

    The Needle, L'Aiguille, is the pointed chalk rock rising out of the sea beside the Porte d'Aval; its height is given as between about fifty-one and seventy metres, depending on the source.

    It is a remnant of the cliff left behind as the waves wore the rest away. The writer Maurice Leblanc made it famous in his 1909 novel The Hollow Needle, in which the gentleman thief Arsène Lupin discovers the treasure of the kings of France hidden inside it. The story first came out as a serial before the book was published. Lupin's opponent is Isidore Beautrelet, a gifted schoolboy who plays detective. In the novel, the Hollow Needle is a secret handed down from one king of France to the next, hiding a fabulous treasure of royal jewels. Beautrelet is first misled by a château called l'Aiguille in the Creuse, which in the story Louis XIV built as a decoy. The real needle is here on the Normandy coast, and Lupin is hiding inside it. Leblanc bought a house in Étretat in 1919, now the Clos Lupin museum. It stands in the street now named after Guy de Maupassant, near La Guillette, the house Maupassant had built for himself in the 1880s.

    The Manneporte is the largest of Étretat's arches, hidden from the town on the far side of the Porte d'Aval. The cliff above it rises sheer from the sea, to between about seventy-five and eighty metres by different counts.

    Its arch is about fifty metres high and forty metres wide.

    The writer Guy de Maupassant, who spent much of his childhood in Étretat, said that a ship could sail through it under full sail. Monet painted it several times in the 1880s, in calm weather and in storms.
