    The Nativity Façade

    In front of you is the Nativity Façade, the oldest face of the Sagrada Família and the part most directly shaped by Antoni Gaudí himself. It celebrates the birth of Jesus, and it faces the rising sun in the east, a symbol of new life.

    The church was the idea of a Barcelona bookseller, Josep Maria Bocabella, who wanted an "expiatory temple" paid for entirely by donations. Work began in 1882 under the architect Francisco de Paula del Villar, with a fairly standard Gothic Revival design. A year later Villar left, and the young Gaudí took over. He changed the project completely and gave the rest of his life to it.

    The façade is divided into three doorways, for Hope, Charity and Faith. Above the central door a cypress tree, the Tree of Life, rises towards the sky. The great columns between the doors rest on animals: one stands on a sea turtle and the other on a tortoise. These turtles, and the chameleons on either side of the façade, are shown fleeing downwards, away from evil. The original façade is carved from sandstone quarried on the hill of Montjuïc, and Gaudí intended it to be painted in many colours.

    Gaudí chose to build this joyful façade first. He knew he would not live to finish the church, and he wanted a model for later builders, and something that would win people over.

    Gaudí's way of making sculpture was as unusual as his architecture. Accounts describe him taking plaster casts from real people, animals and plants, so that the figures would be true to life, before they were enlarged and carved in stone.

    Some figures here were destroyed in 1936, during the Spanish Civil War. The Japanese sculptor Etsuro Sotoo later carved the singing and musical angels you see here, and created the doors.
