    Sounion is the far southern tip of Attica. Whoever held this headland could watch the ships making for the main harbour at Piraeus, and guard the silver mines inland.

    People were buried on the cape long before any temple stood here: the Ministry of Culture reports graves from the Early Bronze Age, in the third millennium BC.

    The Ministry also names Homer as the first writer to mention the cape.

    His story here is a death: Phrontis, the helmsman bringing King Menelaus home from Troy, was struck down by a god's arrows while his hands were on the steering oar. Menelaus put in to bury him with funeral rites before he sailed on.

    The helmsman may have had a shrine of his own close by, on the hill to the north-east, where the Ministry describes an old sacred grove in which a hero, probably Phrontis, was worshipped. A rough, almost round ring of large stones marks it off, and it is older than the precinct of Athena beside it.

    Athena's temple stood on that hill opposite, and only its foundations are left. Its design was odd enough for the Roman architect Vitruvius to write about it. Columns ran along only two sides, the east and the south, and they were plain marble shafts without fluting, topped with Ionic capitals.

    In Roman times the building was taken down to its foundations and carried to Athens, to the Agora.

    A famous visitor later mixed up the two temples. Pausanias, the ancient travel writer, placed a temple of Athena on the very top of the cape.

    The Ministry says he was wrong, and that the mistake lasted until 1900, when inscriptions dug up here showed the summit temple was Poseidon's.

    The cape also had a festival, and Herodotus remembers it for a raid. Men from the island of Aegina lay in wait for the sacred ship that carried leading Athenians here, captured it, and jailed everyone on board they could seize.

    In the Ministry's account the festival came round every four years, and officials sailed to it in that sacred ship, as they did to the sanctuary on Delos.
