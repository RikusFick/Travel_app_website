  1. 1

    An island that moved

    On the stone quay at the southern end of Agilkia Island, facing the temples. This is stop 1 on the route.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    Welcome to Philae, or rather, to the island that took its place. The temples in front of you once stood on a nearby island called Philae, near the First Cataract of the Nile. In the 1960s campaign to save the monuments of Nubia, they were moved here, to Agilkia Island.

    Why move them? The old island had already suffered from the first Aswan dam. The temples are said to have been flooded each year from December to about March. In those months, visitors came by boat, sailing right into the temple court. The water washed away much of the painted colour.

    Then came the High Dam. UNESCO calls Philae the most famous of the monuments it affected. The rescue had to begin in the water. A barrier was built around the old island, and the water was pumped out. The contractor describes a double wall of steel sheet piles with sand between them.

    Every stone, block and column was catalogued and numbered. The temples were taken apart, carried across, and rebuilt here. Agilkia was reshaped to match the layout of the old island.

    When did this happen? The contractor gives its own work as 1974 to 1979. The whole project is given as 1972 to 1980. UNESCO's campaign for Nubia as a whole ran from 1960 to 1980.

    So the stones are ancient, and the island is new. As you walk, keep that in mind. The temples stand in the same order they had on Philae.
