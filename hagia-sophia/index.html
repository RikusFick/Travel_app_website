    Hagia Sophia rises beside you: massive walls, heavy buttresses leaning against them, and the great dome sitting on top.

    You're looking at a building that has been a cathedral, a mosque, a museum, and a mosque again. Few buildings anywhere have lived so many lives. It was built as a Christian church for the Byzantine emperor Justinian, and it was finished in the year 537. It was finished almost fifteen hundred years ago, and it has astonished visitors for centuries.

    The name, Hagia Sophia, is Greek and means "Holy Wisdom". In Turkish it's Ayasofya, and since 2020 it has again been used as a mosque, under the name Hagia Sophia Grand Mosque.

    Here's the story in brief. For about nine hundred years it was the great cathedral of Constantinople, the Byzantine capital. In 1204, crusaders from Western Europe captured the city and turned it into a Catholic cathedral for a few decades. Then it returned to the Orthodox Church. In 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed the Second conquered the city and made it a mosque. In 1935, under the new Turkish Republic, it became a museum. And in 2020 it became a mosque once more.

    That's why, inside, you'll find golden Christian mosaics and huge Islamic calligraphy under the same dome.

    The building has two ways in. Worshippers enter the prayer hall on the ground floor from Ayasofya Square, on the west side. Foreign visitors have their own route, entering on the east side, across from the Fountain of Sultan Ahmed III, and climbing a ramp to the upper galleries, the balconies that run around the inside of the building. From up there the prayer hall lies below, and the galleries hold the finest mosaics and the best views of the dome.

    On the west side, in front of the main façade by the worshippers' entrance, lie some broken old stones that are older than everything inside.

    Hagia Sophia is part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Historic Areas of Istanbul", listed in 1985. UNESCO says it became "a model for an entire family of churches and later mosques".
