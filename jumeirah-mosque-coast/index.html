    Read along

    In front of you is Jumeirah Mosque, often called the most photographed mosque in Dubai. Building work began in 1975 and the mosque opened in 1979, after four years of construction. At that time Jumeirah was a quiet strip of villas, sand tracks and beach, on the edge of a city that oil money was only beginning to change. Photographs from those years show the mosque standing almost alone, its dome and minarets visible from far along the shore.

    It was a family gift. Sheikh Rashid bin Saeed Al Maktoum, who ruled Dubai from 1958 to 1990, commissioned it for his son Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid, today's ruler. The design came from Cairo, from the Egyptian practice Hegazy Engineering Consultancy, and that explains the look. Its style draws on two great medieval traditions of Cairo. The Fatimids, whose rule ended in the twelfth century, favoured keel-shaped arches, carved inscriptions and geometric star patterns. The Mamluks, who ruled from Cairo until the sixteenth century, were famous for their richly decorated minarets and for domes carved in stone. Official descriptions name the Fatimid style; architectural historians also see a strong Mamluk hand, and both styles stand side by side in Cairo.

    Look at the material. The whole building is faced in a yellow-pink sandstone. Notice the two slender minarets framing the large central dome, and the two smaller domes that mirror each other at the front. Then look closely at the surfaces: the minarets and the main dome are covered in deep relief carving, interlaced bands and patterns cut into the stone. Deep carving catches shadow, which is why it reads so well in a raking light. Come back after sunset, when floodlights wash the walls from below and every line of the carving stands out.

    For years this was one of the city's symbols. The mosque was printed on the five-hundred-dirham banknote of the 2003 series, an image later replaced by Burj Khalifa.
