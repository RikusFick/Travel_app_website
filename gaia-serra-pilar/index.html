    Serra do Pilar Monastery

    On the terrace in front of the Serra do Pilar Monastery, looking across the river to Porto.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Serra do Pilar Monastery crowns this hilltop on the south bank of the Douro, a round, domed church facing the whole of Porto across the water. The terrace in front of it counts among the best viewpoints over the river and the old city.

    Its story began in the late 1530s, when King João the Third ordered Augustinian canons from an older monastery at Grijó, after a reform of their order, to move here, close to the city. The design is credited to the architects Diogo de Castilho and Jean de Rouen, known in Portugal as João de Ruão. Near the end of the sixteenth century, work began on the larger church that stands there today, and its dome was completed between 1669 and 1672.

    What makes the monastery unusual is its shape. Both the church and the cloister are circular, with a rectangular main chapel between them, all in one line. The state's own heritage description counts it among the most notable buildings of European classical architecture because of that round church and round cloister.

    In 1809, during the Peninsular War, the monastery was occupied by the troops of Arthur Wellesley, the future Duke of Wellington, and from here the attack on the city, then held by Napoleon's army, was planned. During the Siege of Porto in 1832 and 1833, when liberal and absolutist armies fought over the city, it served as an improvised fortress for the liberal side, while the absolutists held the rest of Gaia. The twentieth century brought several campaigns of repair. The church and cloister were declared National Monuments in 1910, and part of the site is still used by the Army.

    The monastery, Porto's old centre and the bridge below were inscribed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996.

    The Luís I Bridge

    On the upper deck of the Luís I Bridge, beside the metro tracks, high above the river.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Luís I Bridge leaps across the Douro in a single iron arch about one hundred and seventy metres wide, with one deck high on top and a second down at river level.

    By the late 1800s the old suspension bridge on this crossing could no longer cope with the traffic between Porto and Gaia. Gustave Eiffel's company had proposed a bridge with a single deck, but the competition that followed asked for two decks. It was won by a Belgian firm, the Société de Willebroek, with a design by the engineer Théophile Seyrig, who had worked with Eiffel and had already designed the Maria Pia railway bridge upriver.

    Building began in 1881, on the crossing of the old suspension bridge, which was taken down. The upper deck was inaugurated on the thirty-first of October 1886, and the lower deck followed within two years.

    The bridge's proper name is Luiz the First, after King Luís, spelt in the old way, and that spelling appears on the plaque above the portal of the lower deck. Since 2005 line D of the Porto metro has run across the upper deck, beside the walkways for people on foot, while the lower deck carries traffic and pedestrians along the riverbanks.

    The arch was built out over the river from both banks, with nothing to support it from below. As each new section was added, steel cables anchored to the upper deck held it in place, until the two halves met in the middle, the same method Seyrig had used upriver for the Maria Pia bridge.

    The Gaia Cable Car

    By the upper station of the Gaia Cable Car, at the Jardim do Morro, next to the bridge.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The Gaia Cable Car glides down from this garden on the hill to the riverside below, its small cabins passing over the rooftops of Gaia.

    Building started in March 2009, and the line opened in April 2011. It links the Jardim do Morro, beside the upper deck of the bridge, with the Cais de Gaia on the riverbank, where the port wine lodges stand.

    The line is a little over half a kilometre long, and the ride takes about five minutes. The upper station stands about sixty metres above sea level and the lower one only a few metres above the river. Each cabin holds up to eight passengers.

    The upper station stands in the Jardim do Morro, a garden at the foot of the Serra do Pilar and one of the classic viewpoints over the historic centre of Porto. The Jardim do Morro metro station is right beside it. The line is run by a company called Telef, based here in Gaia.

    The Rabelo Boats

    On the Cais de Gaia riverside promenade, beside the wooden rabelo boats moored at the quay.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The rabelo boats moored along this quay are the traditional wooden cargo boats of the Douro, flat-bottomed, with a mast for a single square sail and barrels on deck.

    For centuries they carried port wine down the river. The vineyards are far upstream, in the Douro valley, and the wine came down in barrels to be stored and sold here in Gaia.

    The flat bottom let the boats ride over the shallow, fast-flowing water of the upper river. They had no rudder in the usual sense: a long oar at the stern steered them through the fast water.

    In the twentieth century a chain of dams was built on the river, taming the rapids the rabelos had been made for, and the old way of carrying wine down the Douro came to an end.

    The rabelos no longer carry cargo. Many of the boats moored here are kept by port wine houses, and the name of the house is painted on the sail. Each year, on the twenty-fourth of June, the feast of São João, Porto's great midsummer festival, they race on the river.

    The Port Wine Lodges

    On the Gaia riverside, in front of the old port wine lodges along the waterfront.

    Play0:00 / –:––
    Read along

    The port wine lodges line the Gaia riverside, long, low warehouses where port has been aged in wooden casks for generations.

    Port is made from grapes grown in the Douro valley. While the wine is still fermenting, a strong grape spirit is added, which stops the fermentation and leaves natural sweetness in the wine.

    The young wine was then brought down the river to these lodges, where it was left to age before being bottled. From here it was shipped all over the world through the city of Porto across the river, and from the second half of the 1600s the wine came to carry the city's name.

    How it ages decides its style. Ruby port is kept in large vats, where it takes in little air, and stays deep red and fruity. Tawny port spends longer in wooden casks, where it takes in air slowly through the wood. It loses its red colour, turning amber and golden brown, and takes on flavours of dried fruit and nuts. Some tawnies carry an age on the label, ten, twenty, thirty or forty years, the average age of the wines in the blend. Vintage port is made only in exceptional years, and it spends a short time in wood before maturing in the bottle.

    Much of that trade was with England, and British merchants set up their own lodges here. Until 1986, port could be exported only from the lodges of Gaia; after that, wine could also be shipped straight from the Douro.
