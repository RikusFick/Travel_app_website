    The Façade on the Concorde

    The Hôtel de la Marine is the eastern of the two great colonnaded palaces that close the north side of the Place de la Concorde.

    Both were designed by the king's architect, Ange-Jacques Gabriel, as a grand stage set for a new royal square, then called Place Louis XV, and they were built between 1757 and 1774. Behind the twin façade on the other side of the Rue Royale is the Hôtel de Crillon. This one became the Garde-Meuble de la Couronne, the royal furniture store, which bought, kept and repaired the furniture, tapestries and objects of the royal palaces. In 1789 the Ministry of the Navy moved in, and it stayed for more than two hundred years, which is how the building got its name.

    The Navy left in 2015. The Centre des monuments nationaux then restored the whole building over about four years, at a cost of some one hundred and thirty-two million euros, and opened it to the public in June 2021.

    The design was a piece of urban scenery before it was a building. The façades went up first, a backdrop closing the north side of the new square with nothing behind them. Gabriel had blended ideas from several architects' proposals, and the long row of columns over an arcaded ground floor is generally said to echo the great colonnade of the Louvre. Only from the mid-1760s were real buildings raised behind the stone fronts, and this eastern one was fitted out for the royal furniture store: storerooms, workshops, stables, a chapel, a library, apartments for its officials and galleries to show off the collections.

    What sets this façade apart from the Louvre is its loggia, the covered gallery behind the columns, where the setback creates patterns of light and shadow. Its ceiling is carved with octagonal medallions symbolising the benefits the king was said to bring the nation: agriculture, commerce, music, the arts and equality.
