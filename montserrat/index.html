    These buildings tucked under the cliffs are Montserrat Abbey, Santa Maria de Montserrat, a Benedictine monastery about 48 kilometres north-west of Barcelona, founded in 1025. A community of monks still lives, works and prays here.

    The rock towers above give the mountain its name: Montserrat means "serrated mountain" in Catalan, after its saw-tooth skyline. The rock is a pinkish conglomerate, pebbles and stones cemented together by calcite. It was laid down in the Eocene as a fan of river debris at the edge of the Ebro Basin. Being harder than the softer rocks around it, it survived as the land eroded. The highest summit, Sant Jeroni, reaches 1,236 metres.

    The monastery was wrecked by French troops in 1811 and 1812, during the Peninsular War, and most of what stands here was rebuilt in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Its printing press goes back to 1499, when its first book came out.

    Reaching this ledge was always the hard part. For centuries pilgrims came on foot or by mule. A rack railway opened in 1892: a toothed wheel under the train engages a toothed rail between the tracks, so it can climb slopes too steep for ordinary wheels to grip. It closed in 1957, and a new rack line opened in 2003 on much the same route. The Aeri, a cable car swinging up the cliff face from the river valley, opened in 1930.

    In 1493 Christopher Columbus named a Caribbean island after this place: Montserrat, still a British territory, owes its name to the Virgin of Montserrat.
